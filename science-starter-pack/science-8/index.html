Launch: Laws, Theories and Bias

SL1Lt8

Laws and Theories Lab

Scientific laws and theories play specific roles in describing and explaining phenomena.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (9 knowledge, 4 skills)

Outcome: Students investigate the role of scientific laws and theories and how they contribute to scientific knowledge.

Knowledge

  • SL1.K1Scientific knowledge can refer to facts, models, laws, and theories.
  • SL1.K2Observations that have been repeatedly confirmed may be accepted as scientific facts, which can be revised or rejected with additional evidence.
  • SL1.K3Scientific laws and theories are
    • widely accepted
    • supported by large bodies of evidence
    • equally important
    • used to make predictions about phenomena
    • testable
  • SL1.K4Scientific laws
    • are descriptions of phenomena
    • can be expressed as a cause-and-effect relationship, rule, or an equation
    • are considered universal
  • SL1.K5Scientific theories explain why or how phenomena occur and integrate a wide range of scientific knowledge.
  • SL1.K6A scientific model is a representation that helps explain or predict a phenomenon.
  • SL1.K7Scientists may use models to test hypotheses when developing scientific theories, such as the Big Bang theory.
  • SL1.K8Scientific theories cannot change into laws, and scientific laws cannot change into theories.
  • SL1.K9Science-related careers advance scientific knowledge by developing, testing, and applying scientific laws and theories in areas such as
    • geology and resources, e.g., geophysicist
    • sustainability, e.g., agronomist
    • design criteria, e.g., instrumentation technician

Skills and procedures

  • SL1.S1Compare scientific laws to scientific theories.
  • SL1.S2Research a scientific law or theory.
  • SL1.S3Develop predictions for a phenomenon, using a scientific model.
  • SL1.S4Explore how a career in an applicable field of study in science uses the body of scientific knowledge.

Matter: Atoms and the Periodic Table

M1As8

Atom Builder Lab

Atoms can be defined by their structure.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (6 knowledge, 2 skills)

Outcome: Students interpret matter in relation to atomic structure and physical and chemical properties.

Knowledge

  • M1.K1All matter is composed of atoms.
  • M1.K2An atom is too small to be detected without the use of technology.
  • M1.K3An atom is electrically neutral and is composed of subatomic particles, including
    • protons, which have a positive charge
    • electrons, which have a negative charge
    • neutrons, which do not have a charge
  • M1.K4Protons and neutrons make up an atom’s nucleus.
  • M1.K5An atomic model is a theoretical representation of an atom’s structure.
  • M1.K6Electrons can be represented as orbiting an atom’s nucleus in layered shells (Bohr model), including
    • the first shell, which holds a maximum of two electrons
    • the second shell, which holds a maximum of eight electrons
    • the third shell, which holds a maximum of eight electrons

Skills and procedures

  • M1.S1Model the location and charge of subatomic particles in an atom, limited to atoms that have a maximum of 18 electrons.
  • M1.S2Research a technology used to study atoms and atomic structure.
M2Pt8

Periodic Table Lab

Information about elements and their properties can be determined by using the periodic table.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (6 knowledge, 2 skills)

Outcome: Students interpret matter in relation to atomic structure and physical and chemical properties.

Knowledge

  • M2.K1Elements are pure substances made of only one type of atom and cannot be broken down into other substances by physical or chemical means.
  • M2.K2An element is assigned a name and symbol.
  • M2.K3All atoms of the same element have the same number of protons, which corresponds to that element’s atomic number.
  • M2.K4The periodic table organizes elements according to atomic number and typically displays each element’s atomic mass and state of matter.
  • M2.K5Elements in the periodic table are organized into
    • groups, based on the number of electrons in each element’s outermost (valence) shell
    • periods, based on each element’s number of electron shells
  • M2.K6The organization of the periodic table can be used to distinguish metals from non-metals.

Skills and procedures

  • M2.S1Research how the name and symbol of an element was assigned.
  • M2.S2Determine the name, symbol, and properties of an element, using the periodic table.

Forces, Motion and Fluids

F1Fm8

Forces in Motion Lab

The motion of an object changes because of forces acting on it.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (6 knowledge, 5 skills)

Outcome: Students analyze the motion of objects resulting from interactions of forces, fluids, and pressure.

Knowledge

  • F1.K1Net force is the sum of all forces acting on an object.
  • F1.K2When the net force on an object is zero the forces acting on it are balanced and the object’s motion will not change.
  • F1.K3Changes in the speed and direction of an object’s motion are dependent on the net force.
  • F1.K4Newton’s first law of motion, or the law of inertia, states that an object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion continues moving in a straight line at a constant speed unless acted upon by an unbalanced force.
  • F1.K5The greater the mass of an object, the greater its inertia and the greater the force required to change the motion of the object.
  • F1.K6Speed is the relationship between distance and time v = d/t

Skills and procedures

  • F1.S1Describe how forces affect the motion of an object.
  • F1.S2Predict the direction of an object’s motion based on net force, limited to horizontal motion.
  • F1.S3Investigate how objects with different masses resist changes in motion when a force is applied.
  • F1.S4Solve problems related to speed, distance, and time.
  • F1.S5Conduct a controlled experiment to determine the relationship between speed, distance, and time.
F2Fp8

Fluids and Pressure Lab

The motion and arrangement of particles can be used to predict the behaviour of fluids.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (6 knowledge, 5 skills)

Outcome: Students analyze the motion of objects resulting from interactions of forces, fluids, and pressure.

Knowledge

  • F2.K1Fluids are substances that have no fixed shape and are capable of flowing, including liquids and gases.
  • F2.K2Properties of fluids include
    • pressure, which is the force exerted over an area
    • viscosity, which is a fluid’s resistance to motion
    • compressibility, which is how much a fluid’s volume changes in response to pressure
  • F2.K3Pressure is created by particles colliding with a container’s wall as the wall exerts an equal but opposite force on the particles.
  • F2.K4The space between particles affects the compressibility of a fluid.
  • F2.K5Pascal’s law, named for a French scientist, expresses pressure as the force applied perpendicularly over an area P = F/A
  • F2.K6Pascal’s law applies in technologies that use fluid to produce motion in systems, such as
    • hospital beds
    • automotive brakes
    • jackhammers

Skills and procedures

  • F2.S1Explain the relationship between pressure, force, and area.
  • F2.S2Solve problems involving calculations related to pressure, force, and area.
  • F2.S3Investigate the viscosity of various fluids.
  • F2.S4Compare the compressibility of liquids and gases.
  • F2.S5Apply the properties of fluids to move an object.

Energy: Stores, Static and Waves

EN1Es8

Energy Stores Lab

Energy can be transferred between energy stores.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (4 knowledge, 3 skills)

Outcome: Students examine energy stores and transfers in an object or a system.

Knowledge

  • EN1.K1An energy store is a model to represent how much energy is available within a system.
  • EN1.K2Change in energy stores can indicate the amount of work done on an object or in a system.
  • EN1.K3Types of energy stores include
    • thermal
    • kinetic
    • elastic
    • gravitational
    • chemical
    • magnetic
    • electrostatic
    • nuclear
  • EN1.K4Energy is transferred from one energy store to another through a pathway, including
    • heating
    • radiation
    • mechanical work
    • electrical

Skills and procedures

  • EN1.S1Identify various types of energy stores.
  • EN1.S2Provide examples of energy stores.
  • EN1.S3Describe how energy is transferred between energy stores through energy pathways.
EN2Se8

Static Charge Lab

Static electricity accumulates and can be discharged, which results in the transfer of energy.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (5 knowledge, 3 skills)

Outcome: Students examine energy stores and transfers in an object or a system.

Knowledge

  • EN2.K1Static electricity, or electrostatic charge, is the accumulation of electrons on the surface of a material and is used in equipment such as
    • laser printers
    • paint sprayers
    • air filters
  • EN2.K2Static electricity can occur when two objects are rubbed together, which transfers electrons from the surface of one material to the other and causes
    • the material that loses electrons to become positively charged
    • the material that gains electrons to become negatively charged
  • EN2.K3When charged materials interact,
    • like charges repel
    • unlike charges attract
    • charged objects are attracted to neutral objects (induction)
  • EN2.K4Static electricity is often discharged from the surface of a material in the form of a spark when a conductor touches the material.
  • EN2.K5Static electricity can cause natural phenomena, including lightning.

Skills and procedures

  • EN2.S1Explain how static electricity is discharged through the transfer of electrons.
  • EN2.S2Investigate how charged materials interact with one another.
  • EN2.S3Research how lightning results from static electricity in clouds.
EN3Wv8

Wave Energy Lab

Energy can be transferred as a wave.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (8 knowledge, 5 skills)

Outcome: Students examine energy stores and transfers in an object or a system.

Knowledge

  • EN3.K1A wave is a disturbance or oscillation that transfers energy from one point to another without transporting matter, e.g., a wave on a string.
  • EN3.K2Waves have a repeating pattern and measurable properties, including
    • wavelength
    • frequency
    • amplitude
    • period
    • speed
  • EN3.K3Waves with higher frequency and higher amplitude transfer a greater amount of energy from one energy store to another.
  • EN3.K4Waves can be classified by type, depending on whether they require a medium to propagate, for example:
    • mechanical waves, such as seismic and water, require a medium
    • electromagnetic waves, such as radio and microwave, do not require a medium
  • EN3.K5Mechanical waves oscillate the particles of a medium while transferring energy in the direction the wave travels.
  • EN3.K6When a wave encounters a new medium, the wave can be
    • transmitted
    • reflected
    • refracted
    • dispersed
    • absorbed
  • EN3.K7The law of reflection states that when a wave strikes a surface, the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.
  • EN3.K8Refraction is the bending of a wave due to a change in speed when the wave passes through a new medium.

Skills and procedures

  • EN3.S1Relate energy stores and transfers to waves.
  • EN3.S2Describe the properties of a wave.
  • EN3.S3Differentiate between mechanical and electromagnetic waves.
  • EN3.S4Interpret the behaviour of waves when encountering different surfaces or mediums.
  • EN3.S5Represent the law of reflection.

Life Science: Cells and Organ Systems

LS1Ce8

Cell Explorer Lab

Cells support life in organisms of varying complexity.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (10 knowledge, 8 skills)

Outcome: Students analyze the structures and functions of cells and organ systems.

Knowledge

  • LS1.K1Cells are the basic unit of life and vary in size, shape, and function.
  • LS1.K2Microscopes have enabled and advanced the study of cells.
  • LS1.K3The total magnification of a compound light microscope is the product of the magnification power of the microscope’s lenses.
  • LS1.K4Organelles are specialized structures that support specific functions within the cells of unicellular and multicellular organisms.
  • LS1.K5Organelles found in both plant and animal cells include
    • cell membrane
    • nucleus
    • cytoplasm
    • mitochondria
    • ribosomes
    • vacuoles
  • LS1.K6Organelles found only in plant cells include the cell wall and chloroplasts.
  • LS1.K7Some unicellular organisms, including bacteria, have no nucleus.
  • LS1.K8Some unicellular organisms, such as bacteria, fungi, and protists, can affect the health of humans and other multicellular organisms.
  • LS1.K9Processes that regulate the internal environment of a cell include
    • cellular respiration, which provides energy
    • diffusion, which enables movement of vital substances
    • osmosis, which enables movement of water
    • protein formation, which supports specific functions
  • LS1.K10The semi-permeable membrane of the cell enables diffusion and osmosis.

Skills and procedures

  • LS1.S1Use a microscope to study cells.
  • LS1.S2Calculate total magnification of a microscope.
  • LS1.S3Create diagrams of cells based on a microscope image.
  • LS1.S4Identify organelles in plant and animal cells.
  • LS1.S5Describe the function of organelles.
  • LS1.S6Research how a unicellular organism can affect the health of another organism.
  • LS1.S7Investigate the processes of diffusion and osmosis.
  • LS1.S8Describe processes that regulate the internal environment of cells.
LS2Cd8

Cell Division Lab

Cell division is essential for survival and diversity.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (6 knowledge, 3 skills)

Outcome: Students analyze the structures and functions of cells and organ systems.

Knowledge

  • LS2.K1Deoxyribonucleic acid, or DNA, is the genetic information that provides instructions necessary for survival of the cell.
  • LS2.K2Most animal and plant cells store DNA in the nucleus, while cells without a nucleus, such as bacterial cells, may store DNA in the cytoplasm.
  • LS2.K3Cells within an individual organism contain the same DNA.
  • LS2.K4Cell division enables
    • growth
    • repair
    • reproduction
    • variation
  • LS2.K5Mitosis is a type of cell division that creates two identical daughter cells and enables growth and repair.
  • LS2.K6Meiosis is a type of cell division that creates four non-identical daughter cells, which are used in sexual reproduction.

Skills and procedures

  • LS2.S1Compare the location of DNA in different types of cells.
  • LS2.S2Identify the type of cell division from a given representation.
  • LS2.S3Compare mitosis and meiosis.

Earth Science: Fossils and Resources

ES1Fo8

Fossil Record Lab

Evidence from geological records provides information about Earth’s history.

Open the labSlide deck (opens in a new tab)
Curriculum statements (8 knowledge, 5 skills)

Outcome: Students investigate Earth’s geological history and its implications for resource exploration.

Knowledge

  • ES1.K1The geological time scale organizes Earth’s history into time intervals and is used to represent
    • timing of major geological events
    • the appearance and disappearance of life forms
  • ES1.K2Relative dating is a technique used to determine younger and older rock layers.
  • ES1.K3The fossil record provides information about Earth’s history, including
    • geological events
    • past climates and environments
    • living and extinct organisms
  • ES1.K4Fossils are found in sedimentary rocks and are formed over thousands to millions of years under specific conditions.
  • ES1.K5Fossils are formed when remains or impressions of a once-living organism are mineralized or compressed.
  • ES1.K6Evidence of change over time can be found by comparing organisms and fossils.
  • ES1.K7Remains and impressions of once-living organisms can be destroyed by geological processes.
  • ES1.K8The badlands in what is now Alberta were referred to by early French explorers as “les mauvaises terres à traverser,” or bad lands to cross, and are renowned for extensive fossil beds.

Skills and procedures

  • ES1.S1Represent the geological time scale.
  • ES1.S2Distinguish between younger and older rock layers.
  • ES1.S3Explore how different types of fossils are formed.
  • ES1.S4Describe evidence of change in organisms over time.
  • ES1.S5Research the discovery of fossils in the badlands of Alberta.

Resource Extraction Lab

Curriculum statements (6 knowledge, 3 skills)

Outcome: Students investigate Earth’s geological history and its implications for resource exploration.

Open the labSlide deck (opens in a new tab)
Curriculum statements (8 knowledge, 5 skills)

Outcome: Students analyze the origin and evolution of the universe.

Sustainability Capstone

SL3Su8

Sustainable Futures Lab

Sustainability supports present needs while addressing long-term impacts.

Open the labSlide deck (opens in a new tab)
Curriculum statements (11 knowledge, 5 skills)

Outcome: Students examine how sustainability supports the needs of future generations.

Knowledge

  • SL3.K1Environmental stewardship can support sustainability by meeting present needs in ways that also consider the needs of future generations.
  • SL3.K2Sustainability refers to the ability of systems to function effectively into the future.
  • SL3.K3Actions that support sustainability include
    • using green technology
    • managing resources while protecting air, land, and water quality
    • planning urban or rural expansion while preserving natural areas
    • setting targets for reducing pollution, waste, and greenhouse gas emissions
  • SL3.K4Green technology, such as electric or hydrogen-powered vehicles, solar power, carbon capture and storage, and vertical farming, can reduce impacts of human activity on the environment.
  • SL3.K5Technologies, including AI and drones, can be leveraged to reduce environmental impacts by monitoring, predicting, and improving how resources are used.
  • SL3.K6Sustainable agriculture protects biodiversity, supports soil and ecosystem health, and maximizes long-term yield.
  • SL3.K7Sustainability initiatives have led to innovations such as precision agriculture and autonomous zero-emission mining equipment.
  • SL3.K8Cleaner energy resources, such as natural gas, hydrogen, nuclear, and renewables, can help balance energy demands with sustainability goals by reducing greenhouse gas emissions.
  • SL3.K9Hydrogen can be used in gas utility systems, transportation, industrial processes, power generation, and power storage.
  • SL3.K10Alberta has uranium resources available for use as part of a clean energy strategy that includes nuclear energy.
  • SL3.K11Sustainability efforts can have social, environmental, and economic impacts.

Skills and procedures

  • SL3.S1Relate environmental stewardship to sustainability.
  • SL3.S2Explain actions that support sustainability.
  • SL3.S3Investigate ways in which technologies can be used to enhance sustainability.
  • SL3.S4Explore ways in which energy resources can be used to support sustainable energy in Alberta.
  • SL3.S5Research possible impacts of sustainability efforts at a local or provincial level.
