Launch: Scientific Literacy

SL1Hc9

Theory Shift Lab

Understandings of phenomena can change over time.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (6 knowledge, 4 skills)

Outcome: Students relate understandings of phenomena to scientific theories.

Knowledge

  • SL1.K1When multiple scientific explanations of phenomena appear to be valid, new evidence is needed.
  • SL1.K2Accepted scientific theories are the simplest, most supported, and most comprehensive explanations of phenomena.
  • SL1.K3Peer review confirms the validity of scientific work, e.g., scientific theories, and includes
    • examination of investigation techniques
    • analysis and interpretation of data
  • SL1.K4New technologies and methods can reveal new evidence that can lead to a scientific theory or model being changed.
  • SL1.K5If substantial contradictory evidence is collected over time, a scientific theory can be
    • restricted to a specific range of conditions
    • revised to include the new evidence
    • replaced with a new theory
  • SL1.K6Science-related careers apply knowledge of phenomena in areas such as
    • electricity, e.g., electrician
    • agriculture, e.g., agricultural engineer
    • space exploration, e.g., space lawyer
    • innovation, e.g., hydrogen systems technician

Skills and procedures

  • SL1.S1Describe qualities of an accepted scientific theory.
  • SL1.S2Engage in peer review of a controlled experiment.
  • SL1.S3Research information about a scientific theory that has been restricted, revised, or replaced.
  • SL1.S4Explore how a science-related career can advance explanations of phenomena.
SL2Cb9

Claim Check Lab

Biased or selective data undermines scientific understandings.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (5 knowledge, 3 skills)

Outcome: Students analyze claims and data for bias.

Knowledge

  • SL2.K1Survivorship bias occurs when attention is placed on successful outcomes because they are visible and easier to evaluate, while failures are ignored or overlooked due to lack of visibility.
  • SL2.K2Claims that are presented as scientific but do not align with available evidence or are based on biased treatment of data are not accepted by the scientific community.
  • SL2.K3Data and evidence can be represented in ways that appear to support unscientific claims, including reporting influenced by selection bias or the use of an inappropriate scale.
  • SL2.K4Selection bias occurs when research participants or data are not randomly selected.
  • SL2.K5Critical-thinking skills can be used to analyze claims.

Skills and procedures

  • SL2.S1Describe how survivorship bias and selection bias could lead to misleading or inaccurate conclusions.
  • SL2.S2Interpret data for evidence of survivorship bias and selection bias.
  • SL2.S3Differentiate between scientific theories and unscientific claims.

Life Science: Variation and Inheritance

LS1Dn9

Variation and Inheritance Lab

Genetic traits of organisms are determined by DNA.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (8 knowledge, 4 skills)

Outcome: Students examine how variation occurs in organisms and species.

Knowledge

  • LS1.K1DNA contains the genetic code of an organism needed
    • to make each type of organism
    • to make each type of cell in an organism
    • for cell growth and repair
    • for cell reproduction
  • LS1.K2A sequence of DNA can be used to identify species and individual organisms, except for identical twins, in which DNA sequences are nearly identical.
  • LS1.K3DNA is a double helix structure and can be organized into chromosomes.
  • LS1.K4Human cells typically contain 23 pairs of chromosomes.
  • LS1.K5Chromosomes contain segments of DNA, called genes, which control the formation of proteins that affect the genetic traits of an organism.
  • LS1.K6Alleles are different forms of the same gene that result in variations of a trait.
  • LS1.K7Mutations are changes in a DNA sequence that can occur naturally during cell division or due to environmental factors and can be advantageous, harmful, or neutral.
  • LS1.K8Mutations in sex cells may result in new genetic traits that can be inherited by offspring, while mutations in body cells are not inherited and may lead to diseases.

Skills and procedures

  • LS1.S1Describe the structure and function of DNA.
  • LS1.S2Model the structure of DNA.
  • LS1.S3Explain the relationship between DNA, genes, chromosomes, and genetic traits of organisms.
  • LS1.S4Research an example of a genetic mutation.
LS2Ve9

Variation and Inheritance Lab

Genetic variation can lead to evolutionary change in populations.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (11 knowledge, 5 skills)

Outcome: Students examine how variation occurs in organisms and species.

Knowledge

  • LS2.K1Reproduction is a biological process through which organisms transmit DNA to produce offspring.
  • LS2.K2Asexual reproduction is an efficient way to produce new individuals that receive their genetic material from a single parent, through processes such as binary fission and budding.
  • LS2.K3In sexual reproduction, each parent contributes one chromosome from each of their chromosome pairs to their offspring.
  • LS2.K4Sexual reproduction results in greater genetic variation than asexual reproduction due to the mixing of DNA from two different parents during meiosis, while asexual reproduction relies mainly on mutations for genetic variation.
  • LS2.K5Genetic variation refers to the differences in DNA among individuals within a population or species.
  • LS2.K6Variation can be caused by differences in genetic material, environmental factors, or both.
  • LS2.K7Adaptations are genetic traits that provide survival and reproductive advantages.
  • LS2.K8Natural selection is the process by which adaptations become more common in future generations.
  • LS2.K9Evolution is the change in a population over generations through natural selection and does not occur within an individual’s lifetime.
  • LS2.K10Theories of natural selection and evolution are based upon Darwin’s observations and ideas.
  • LS2.K11Artificial selection is a process used by humans to selectively breed organisms for desired genetic traits and is used in agricultural products such as canola.

Skills and procedures

  • LS2.S1Compare sexual and asexual reproduction.
  • LS2.S2Research species that reproduce asexually, sexually, or both.
  • LS2.S3Describe the relationships among genetic variation, adaptation, natural selection, and evolution.
  • LS2.S4Discuss Darwin’s contribution to the theory of evolution.
  • LS2.S5Research examples of artificial selection used to develop agricultural products, including those used in Alberta.
LS3In9

Variation and Inheritance Lab

Genetic inheritance can be used to predict traits from one generation to the next.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (8 knowledge, 6 skills)

Outcome: Students examine how variation occurs in organisms and species.

Knowledge

  • LS3.K1Genetic material, passed down through sexual reproduction to offspring, results in traits that can be the same as, different from, or a blend of the traits of both parents.
  • LS3.K2Single gene inheritance describes traits determined by the combination of alleles of one gene.
  • LS3.K3Alleles can be either dominant or recessive and the combination determines the trait that is expressed when
    • at least one copy of the dominant allele is present, resulting in a dominant trait
    • two copies of the recessive allele are present, resulting in a recessive trait
  • LS3.K4A Punnett square can be used to represent genetic crosses.
  • LS3.K5Genotype is the set of alleles that determines an organism’s traits, and phenotype is the observable traits of an organism.
  • LS3.K6Most genetic traits are complex, such as hair colour and eye colour, and are the result of the interaction of multiple different genes.
  • LS3.K7Discrete variation is expressed as distinct versions of the same trait, e.g., blood type, and continuous variation in traits is expressed on a spectrum, e.g., height.
  • LS3.K8The field of genetics involves ethical issues, such as
    • DNA modification
    • ownership of genetic information
    • consent and sharing of DNA profiles

Skills and procedures

  • LS3.S1Discuss variation in phenotypes observed in plants and animals, including humans.
  • LS3.S2Distinguish between genotype and phenotype.
  • LS3.S3Represent single gene inheritance in a genetic cross.
  • LS3.S4Determine phenotype from a given genotype.
  • LS3.S5Distinguish between discrete and continuous traits.
  • LS3.S6Consider implications of an ethical issue related to genetics.

Earth Science: Plate Tectonics

ES1Pl9

Shifting Plates Lab

The layers of Earth behave differently and contribute to geological processes that continually reshape Earth’s surface.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (9 knowledge, 3 skills)

Outcome: Students investigate geological processes related to the movement of tectonic plates.

Knowledge

  • ES1.K1Layers of Earth include
    • the crust: the thin outermost layer of continental and oceanic rock
    • the mantle: the thickest and mostly solid layer between the crust and core
    • the core: the innermost layer with a fluid outer core and solid inner core
  • ES1.K2The lithosphere consists of large tectonic plates that make up Earth’s crust and the uppermost solid part of Earth’s mantle.
  • ES1.K3Tectonic plates move on the upper part of the mantle because of convection currents and gravitational forces.
  • ES1.K4The theory of continental drift states that Earth’s continents were once a large land mass that broke and drifted apart over millions of years.
  • ES1.K5The theory of continental drift is supported by evidence, including the shapes of Earth’s continents and similarities among fossils found on different continents.
  • ES1.K6The theory of plate tectonics builds on the theory of continental drift and describes Earth’s crust as seven primary tectonic plates that move over Earth’s upper mantle, driven by heat-related convection.
  • ES1.K7Tectonic plates are classified as
    • oceanic, which are thinner and denser than continental plates
    • continental, which are thicker and less dense than oceanic plates
  • ES1.K8Evidence supporting the theory of plate tectonics includes
    • seafloor spreading
    • mountain formation
    • seismic activity
    • volcanic activity
  • ES1.K9The Rocky Mountains were formed by the collision and compression of tectonic plates.

Skills and procedures

  • ES1.S1Model Earth’s layers.
  • ES1.S2Relate the theory of continental drift to the theory of plate tectonics.
  • ES1.S3Discuss how the movement of tectonic plates changes Earth’s surface.

Matter: Elements, Compounds and Reactions

M1Ec9

Compound Builder Lab

Pure substances can be classified by their composition.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (11 knowledge, 7 skills)

Outcome: Students investigate the composition and chemical interactions of elements and compounds.

Knowledge

  • M1.K1Pure substances can be classified as elements or compounds.
  • M1.K2An element can be
    • monoatomic: composed of a single atom of the same type, e.g., Rn
    • diatomic: composed of two atoms of the same type, e.g., H2
    • polyatomic: composed of more than two atoms of the same type, e.g., S8
  • M1.K3A molecule is a group of atoms that act as a single unit and can range in size, e.g., O2, CO2, DNA.
  • M1.K4A compound can be classified as ionic or molecular.
  • M1.K5An ionic compound is composed of oppositely charged ions, which combine in specific ratios resulting in a neutral charge.
  • M1.K6The ratio of atoms in an ionic compound can be determined by using the periodic table.
  • M1.K7An atom that has gained one or more electrons is a negatively charged ion, referred to as an anion, and an atom that has lost one or more electrons is a positively charged ion, referred to as a cation.
  • M1.K8A molecular compound is composed of two or more non-metal atoms that share electrons.
  • M1.K9Rules for naming ionic and molecular compounds are established by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry, or IUPAC.
  • M1.K10The IUPAC chemical formula for a compound uses the symbol for each element and shows each element’s number of atoms in subscript, if that number is greater than one.
  • M1.K11Hazards of pure substances can be identified by using the Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System, or WHMIS.

Skills and procedures

  • M1.S1Identify monoatomic, diatomic, and polyatomic elements.
  • M1.S2Model structures of elements and compounds.
  • M1.S3Explain relationships between atoms, elements, ions, molecules, and compounds.
  • M1.S4Name compounds composed of two elements when given the chemical formula.
  • M1.S5Determine the chemical formula of a compound composed of two elements when given the IUPAC name.
  • M1.S6Determine the number of atoms of each element in ionic or molecular compounds.
  • M1.S7Identify the hazards represented by WHMIS pictograms.
M2Cr9

Chemical Change Lab

Chemical reactions of pure substances can be determined by observable and quantifiable changes.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (9 knowledge, 7 skills)

Outcome: Students investigate the composition and chemical interactions of elements and compounds.

Knowledge

  • M2.K1A chemical reaction is a process in which the atoms that make up the original substances, or reactants, regroup into new substances or products.
  • M2.K2Matter is not created or destroyed during a chemical reaction; therefore, the mass of the reactants is equal to the mass of the products.
  • M2.K3Observable evidence of chemical change includes
    • change in colour or odour
    • production of gas or precipitate
    • change in energy, e.g., production of light or change in temperature
  • M2.K4Physical change, such as melting an ice cube or dissolving sugar in water, does not result in new products.
  • M2.K5The reactivity of a pure substance depends on the number of electrons in the substance’s outermost (valence) shell.
  • M2.K6Atoms are more stable when their outermost (valence) electron shell is full.
  • M2.K7Chemical reactions can be represented in various ways, including with chemical equations and by modelling.
  • M2.K8The rate of chemical reactions is affected by factors, such as temperature and concentration, that influence how often particles collide.
  • M2.K9WHMIS and other safety protocols need to be considered when working with chemicals.

Skills and procedures

  • M2.S1Perform chemical reactions, using a variety of reactants, to demonstrate chemical change.
  • M2.S2Differentiate between physical and chemical change.
  • M2.S3Perform a chemical reaction to demonstrate how mass is conserved.
  • M2.S4Discuss WHMIS and other safety protocols.
  • M2.S5Represent chemical reactions as chemical equations.
  • M2.S6Demonstrate chemical reactions, using models.
  • M2.S7Explain the role of electrons in reactivity of pure substances.

Electricity

E1Ci9

Circuit Builder Lab

Energy can be transferred by a current within a circuit.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (17 knowledge, 8 skills)

Outcome: Students examine electric current and conservation of energy.

Knowledge

  • E1.K1Electric current is a transfer of energy through the flow of electrons in a conductor.
  • E1.K2The rate at which a charge passes a given point in a circuit is expressed in amperes, named after a French physicist (André-Marie Ampère).
  • E1.K3A circuit is created when current flows continuously through a closed path.
  • E1.K4Potential difference, or voltage, expressed in volts, causes current to flow because of a difference in electric charge between two points along a circuit.
  • E1.K5The main components that can make up a circuit include
    • an energy store, e.g., a battery or cell
    • a conductor
    • a load device, which transfers energy into another energy store, e.g., a light bulb
    • a control device, e.g., a switch or a resistor
  • E1.K6Components of circuits can be arranged in series or in parallel and can be represented with schematic diagrams that use component symbols.
  • E1.K7In a series circuit, current
    • flows along a single path
    • is the same at any point in the circuit
  • E1.K8In a parallel circuit,
    • current flows along multiple paths
    • the total current is equal to the sum of the current in the branches
    • not every branch needs to be closed for the circuit to function
    • voltage in each branch is the same
  • E1.K9Conventional current, defined as the direction in which a positive charge would flow, was established before the discovery of electrons.
  • E1.K10Technologies used to measure voltage, current, and resistance include voltmeters, ammeters, and multimeters.
  • E1.K11Conductors, such as copper, aluminium, and tap water, are materials that allow a charge to be transferred.
  • E1.K12Insulators, such as rubber, wood, and deionized water, are materials that impede the transfer of a charge.
  • E1.K13Resistance, expressed in ohms, is a measure of how much a circuit component reduces current.
  • E1.K14Properties of conductors, such as material, length, and cross-sectional area, affect resistance.
  • E1.K15Resistors can be used to control current in devices.
  • E1.K16Ohm’s law expresses the relationship between voltage, current, and resistance V =I×R.
  • E1.K17Safe practices are required when handling or near electric current.

Skills and procedures

  • E1.S1Design schematic diagrams of series and parallel circuits.
  • E1.S2Investigate the use of control devices to manage the flow of electric current.
  • E1.S3Measure voltage, current, and resistance in circuits.
  • E1.S4Compare the characteristics of series and parallel circuits.
  • E1.S5Troubleshoot series and parallel circuits.
  • E1.S6Explain the relationship between voltage, current, and resistance.
  • E1.S7Apply Ohm’s law to solve problems involving calculations related to voltage, current, and resistance.
  • E1.S8Identify safe and unsafe practices around electric current.
E2Ed9

Energy Efficiency Lab

The conservation of energy is evident in the energy transfers of electrical devices.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (9 knowledge, 7 skills)

Outcome: Students examine electric current and conservation of energy.

Knowledge

  • E2.K1Power, expressed in watts, is the rate at which an electrical device transfers energy.
  • E2.K2The power rating can be used to calculate the energy input required by a device.
  • E2.K3The relationship between power and time can be used to determine energy input E = P × t.
  • E2.K4The law of conservation of energy states that energy
    • cannot be created or destroyed
    • can be transferred from one energy store to another
  • E2.K5During an energy transfer, energy dissipates from a system through heating the system’s surroundings, reducing the amount of useful energy available.
  • E2.K6The energy efficiency of electrical devices can be determined by comparing the input from the energy store to the output of the device, quantified in a ratio.
  • E2.K7Appliances or devices can be rated according to their energy efficiency by programs such as EnerGuide or Energy Star.
  • E2.K8The cost of operating electrical devices is the product of the energy rating in kilowatt hours and the price of power per kilowatt hour.
  • E2.K9In Alberta, electricity can be derived from various sources, including oil, natural gas, coal, biomass, solar radiation, and wind.

Skills and procedures

  • E2.S1Explain the relationships between power, energy, and efficiency.
  • E2.S2Solve problems involving energy, power, and time.
  • E2.S3Apply the law of conservation of energy to account for the total energy involved in energy transfers.
  • E2.S4Relate energy efficiency to energy stores and transfers.
  • E2.S5Solve problems involving the efficiency of various electrical devices.
  • E2.S6Solve problems involving the cost of operating electrical devices.
  • E2.S7Explore sources of electricity in Alberta.

W1Sw9

Sound Waves Lab

Sound waves transfer energy from the source of the sound wave and behave predictably.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (4 knowledge, 4 skills)

Outcome: Students investigate energy transfers in relation to sound waves and light waves.

Knowledge

  • W1.K1Sound waves are mechanical waves that
    • require a vibrating source
    • require a medium to propagate
    • travel in straight lines
    • travel in all directions from the source
  • W1.K2The frequency of a sound wave determines the pitch, and the amplitude of a sound wave determines the volume.
  • W1.K3The speed of a sound wave depends on the temperature and type of medium through which the wave travels.
  • W1.K4In most conditions, sound waves travel faster
    • in higher temperatures
    • through denser materials, e.g., sound travels faster through water than air

Skills and procedures

  • W1.S1Describe how sound travels.
  • W1.S2Identify different mediums through which sound waves can travel.
  • W1.S3Compare pitch and volume, using sound-wave diagrams.

Open the labSlide deck
Curriculum statements (7 knowledge, 4 skills)

Outcome: Students investigate energy transfers in relation to sound waves and light waves.

Knowledge

  • W2.K1Light waves are electromagnetic waves that
    • require a light source
    • do not require a medium to propagate
    • travel in straight lines
    • travel in all directions from the source
  • W2.K2The amplitude of a light wave determines brightness, and the frequency of a light wave determines colour.
  • W2.K3Light waves behave differently when interacting with mediums of different transparency, e.g., transparent, translucent, or opaque.
  • W2.K4The human eye can see only a portion of the electromagnetic spectrum, referred to as the visible light spectrum.

  • W2.K7A shadow is caused when light rays are absorbed by a body, creating an area of darkness behind the body.

  • W2.S2Investigate how light waves interact with different mediums.
  • W2.S3Explore the effect of a prism on white light.
  • W2.S4Discuss the formation of shadows in relation to the behaviour of light.
