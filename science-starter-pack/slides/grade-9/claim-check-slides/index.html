Part 1 · Learn section 1

Claims and evidence

Anyone can make a claim. Science accepts a claim only when it fits fair evidence.

This part covers SL2.K2 and skill S3. Ask: what is the difference between “I have a theory” in everyday talk and “the theory of plate tectonics” in science? Lab link: Learn tab, Section 1.

SL2.K2 · SL2.S3 · Learn 1

Theory or unscientific claim?

Scientific theory

A well-tested explanation of how part of the natural world works, supported by many lines of evidence from many independent investigations.

Revised when strong new evidence appears.

Cell theory · germ theory · plate tectonics

Unscientific claim

May sound scientific, with lab coats, big words or graphs, but does not match the evidence or depends on biased treatment of data.

Usually stays the same no matter what the evidence shows.

Not accepted by the scientific community

Science 9 · SL2 · Claims and evidence

Stress that in everyday talk “theory” can mean a guess, but in science a theory is one of the strongest kinds of understanding we have. Picking only the results that agree is one example of biased treatment of data. Ask students for one example of each from their own lives or the news, without naming classmates. Lab link: Learn tab, Section 1 (Evidence profile).

Crystal water: a claim from the lab that crystals give water a healing energy no instrument can detect. Its only support is customer testimonials.

Science 9 · SL2 · Claims and evidence

Germ theory: many diseases are caused by microorganisms that pass from host to host. It was built on work by many scientists, including Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch, and its predictions, such as handwashing reducing infections, keep holding up. Crystal water fails the first check: if no observation could ever show it is wrong, it cannot be tested, so it cannot be scientific. Testimonials are chosen stories, not fair data. Try the other claims in the lab (full moon emergency rush, balance bracelet, astrology personality): those can be tested, but they fail when tested fairly. Part 2 · Learn sections 2 and 3

Two kinds of bias

Survivorship bias counts only the winners. Selection bias picks a sample that does not match the population.

This part covers SL2.K1, K4 and skills S1 and S2. Quick warm-up: “My grandpa’s 1974 fridge still works, so old appliances were built better.” What is missing from that evidence? Lab link: Learn tab, Sections 2 and 3.

SL2.K1 · Learn 2

Survivorship bias: counting only the winners

Attention goes to successful outcomes because they are easy to see. Failures are overlooked because they have disappeared from view.

“Old houses were built better.”

The weak old houses were torn down long ago. Only sturdy ones are left to see.

“That famous player quit school and got rich.”

Thousands who did the same and did not get rich are never interviewed.

Science 9 · SL2 · Two kinds of bias

Ask students to suggest their own examples. Prompt: who is missing from the story? Survivorship bias is a special case of selection bias: the “sample” is only the survivors. Lab link: Learn tab, Section 2; Sort It tab.

SL2.K1 · World War II · Abraham Wald

Armour the places where the returning planes had no holes.

Planes hit in the engines mostly did not come back, so their damage was never counted. Few holes there meant those hits were deadly.

Tell the story first, then show the conclusion. Many returning planes had bullet holes in the wings and body, and the obvious idea was to add armour there. Mathematician Abraham Wald pointed out that the data came only from planes that survived. Ask: why is this a perfect example of survivorship bias? Lab link: Learn tab, Section 2 (Plane damage model: switch between “Planes that returned” and “All planes that flew”).

SL2.K4 · Learn 3

Selection bias: who got picked?

Population

The whole group you want to know about.

Sample

The part you actually measure.

Representative

Like the population in the ways that matter.

Random sample

Every member has an equal chance of being picked.

Selection bias happens when the way participants or data are chosen makes the sample different from the population. Asking only friends, volunteers, or people in one place are common causes.

Science 9 · SL2 · Two kinds of bias

Example from the lab’s hockey survey model (simulated Edmonton district, 400 Grade 9 students): asking at the arena almost always overestimates how many play hockey, and asking in the library tends to underestimate. A random draw wobbles a little around the true value by chance, which is not bias. Accuracy note (SL2.K4): the draft defines selection bias as participants or data that are not randomly selected. Non-random selection is the most common cause, but not every non-random sample is biased, and a random sample can still be unrepresentative by chance. The lab teaches the fuller definition shown here. Lab link: Learn tab, Section 3 (Hockey survey model).

Lab Bench · Station B · SL2.S1 · SL2.S2

Does a bigger sample fix bias?

A simulated district of 2000 Grade 9 students. Goal: the average daily screen time. Each survey gives one average.

Random draw from class lists

Centred on the truth

Results scatter around the true average. Bigger samples cluster more tightly.

Esports club or volunteer poll

Centred in the wrong place

A bigger sample only makes the wrong answer more consistent.

Size reduces chance variation. Only a fair method removes bias.

Science 9 · SL2 · Two kinds of bias

Before showing the cards, ask students to predict: if we survey 400 esports club members instead of 40, will the estimate get closer to the truth? Then run 30 surveys of each method in the lab and compare the dot clouds. The other methods in the lab are a volunteer online poll and asking at 7:45 a.m. track practice. Data are simulated. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Station B (Sample builder). Written Review short answer 7 practises this idea.

Lab Bench · Station C · SL2.S1 · SL2.K1

Were old houses really built better?

Simulation: 300 houses built in 1925 in an Edmonton neighbourhood. Each decade, a sturdy house has a 97% chance of still standing and a basic house an 80% chance.

YearSturdyBasicSturdy share
1925 (built)9021030%
1975 (5 decades)about 77about 69about 53%
2025 (10 decades)about 66about 22about 75%

Most old houses still standing are sturdy, even though most were built basic.

Science 9 · SL2 · Two kinds of bias

Worked example. 30% of 300 = 90 sturdy and 210 basic. After 10 decades: 90 × 0.97 to the power 10 ≈ 66 sturdy; 210 × 0.80 to the power 10 ≈ 22 basic. Sturdy share = 66 ÷ (66 + 22) ≈ 75%. These are expected values from the lab’s simulation rules, made up for practice; each run of the lab varies a little. Ask: if you walked down this street in 2025, what would you conclude about 1925 builders? Why is that conclusion biased? Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Station C (Survivor Street, slide time forward).

Part 3 · Learn sections 4 and 5

Data dressed up to fool you

Real numbers can still mislead: a stretched scale, a hand-picked window. Then a toolkit to catch them.

This part covers SL2.K3 and K5. Ask students whether they have seen a graph in an ad or video that looked dramatic. What made it look that way? Lab link: Learn tab, Sections 4 and 5.

SL2.K3 · Learn 4 · Bench D

The same data, two scales

Axis starts at 0. A = 62%, B = 66%. They look almost the same, because they nearly are.

Axis starts at 60. Same data. B now looks 3 times taller than A.

actual ratio = B ÷ A = 66 ÷ 62 ≈ 1.06 · apparent ratio = (B − s) ÷ (A − s) = 6 ÷ 2 = 3

Science 9 · SL2 · Data dressed up

Example data from the lab: students who said they enjoy science at two schools. A truncated axis is one kind of inappropriate scale. B is only about 6% bigger, but the graph makes it look 3 times bigger. Students do not need to memorize the formulas; they are there to check a graph. Practice: an axis starts at 48 and compares 50 and 54. Apparent ratio = 6 ÷ 2 = 3; actual ratio = 54 ÷ 50 = 1.08. Lab link: Learn tab, Section 4 (Axis slider); Lab Bench, Station D (Scale fixer) for randomized practice.

SL2.K3 · Learn 4

Cherry-picking the window

A chosen 4 years

“It’s going down!”

These four years happen to fall. Who chose this window, and why?

All 40 years

A slow rise

With all the data, the long-term trend goes up, with ups and downs along the way.

Selective reporting shows only the data that agrees: a short window, a few chosen sites, or only the trials that worked. It is selection bias in how results are reported.

Science 9 · SL2 · Data dressed up

The lab’s window picker uses example data: a measurement that rises slowly over 40 years with ups and downs. Ask: what could this measurement be in real life? Discuss carefully and without naming real people. Sensitive topic note: students may bring up climate or health claims from social media; keep the focus on the method (what was left out) rather than on debating the topic. Lab link: Learn tab, Section 4 (Window picker). Written Review short answer 8.

Lab Bench · Station A · SL2.S2

Bias detective: which problem?

1

A magazine interviews ten famous rock stars. All ten quit music lessons as kids. Headline: “Music lessons don’t matter for success.”

2

To find out if Edmontonians want more bike lanes, a group surveys 200 people at a bike shop on Whyte Avenue. 92% say yes.

3

An ad compares sugar in two cereals: 10 g and 11 g. The axis starts at 9.5 g, so the 11 g bar looks three times taller.

Survivorship bias, selection bias, misleading scale, or fair data?

Science 9 · SL2 · Data dressed up

These cases are from the lab and are made up for practice. Answers. 1: survivorship bias; only people who became famous were interviewed, and thousands who quit lessons and never succeeded are invisible. 2: selection bias; people at a bike shop are more likely to cycle, so the sample does not represent all Edmontonians. 3: misleading scale; the truncated axis turns a 10% difference into what looks like a 300% difference. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Station A (Bias detective) for more cases.

SL2.K5 · Learn 5

Six questions for any claim

What evidence is given?

FocusFuel: only what customers say about their own marks.

How was the data selected?

50 customers chosen by the company, not a random sample.

Are failures missing?

Yes. Only customers who renewed were asked: survivorship bias.

Is there a fair comparison?

No group of similar students who did not chew the gum.

Has anyone independent tested it?

No independent study is mentioned.

Who gains if I believe it?

The company selling it. A reason to check, not proof it is false.

Science 9 · SL2 · Data dressed up

Return to the FocusFuel ad from the hook. Critical thinking means questioning a claim carefully and fairly before you accept or reject it; it is not the same as rejecting everything. A strong conclusion names the evidence, explains how the data was chosen, and admits what is still unknown. Also point out that marks often rise as students learn, gum or no gum, which is why a comparison group matters. Lab link: Learn tab, Section 5 (tap each question beside the ad).

Quick check

Three questions

1

A chess club surveys its own members: 95% enjoy board games. Can they say 95% of all students at their school do?

2

A volunteer-only survey is biased. Will a bigger sample fix it?

3

Give two differences between a scientific theory and an unscientific claim.

Science 9 · SL2 · Review

Answers. 1: No. This is selection bias: chess club members chose to join a board-game club, so they are not representative of the school. 2: No. A bigger sample only reduces chance variation; every extra volunteer is chosen in the same biased way, so the estimate stays wrong, just more consistently. The method must change, for example to random selection. 3: A theory is supported by many lines of evidence from many independent investigations, while an unscientific claim does not match the evidence or depends on biased data. A theory is testable and is revised when strong new evidence appears, while an unscientific claim is often untestable or stays the same. Lab link: Quiz tab for a 10-question round.

Extended response · SL2.S3

Plate tectonics or astrology?

Explain why the scientific community accepts plate tectonics but does not accept astrology as an explanation of personality. Refer to evidence, testing and the treatment of data.

Independent lines of evidence for plate tectonics

Repeated testing and revision

Astrology tested and not supported

Remembered hits, ignored misses

Science 9 · SL2 · Review

This is a Written Review extended response in the lab. Model answer: Plate tectonics is supported by many independent lines of evidence: matching rocks and fossils across oceans, sea-floor magnetic stripes, earthquake and volcano patterns, and GPS measurements of plate movement. It has been tested repeatedly and fits other knowledge. It was accepted after scientists revised an older view. Astrology makes testable predictions, but large studies comparing birth dates and personality find no link beyond chance. Support for astrology comes from remembered hits and ignored misses, which is a biased treatment of data. So one fits the evidence and the other does not. Respect note: some students or families may value astrology culturally or for fun; keep the focus on what counts as scientific evidence, not on judging people. Debate option: one side argues as a 1925 skeptic of continental drift, the other as a 1968 geologist. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended responses.
