Part 1 · SL1.K1 and SL1.K2

Competing explanations

When two explanations both fit, new evidence decides. The winner is the most supported, most comprehensive and simplest.

Section opener. Define a phenomenon: something in nature we can observe, like a rainbow or a mass extinction. A scientific theory is a well-tested explanation of why or how a phenomenon happens, much more than a guess.

SL1.K2 · Accepted theories

Three qualities of an accepted theory

1

Most supported

Agrees with the most evidence, from many tests repeated by different people.

2

Most comprehensive

Explains the widest range of observations and makes predictions that come true.

3

Simplest

Needs the fewest extra assumptions or special exceptions.

Evidence comes first. Simplicity breaks a tie between explanations that fit the evidence equally well.

Science 9 · SL1 · Competing explanations

Stress the order: simplicity alone never beats evidence. Worked example from Lab Bench Station A: three explanations of why a pond near Elk Island freezes later some years each explain 12 of 12 observations with 5 supporting studies, but need 3, 1 and 2 extra assumptions. B needs the fewest, so B is simplest and would be accepted. Lab link: Learn tab, section 2; Lab Bench, Station A (theory scorecard).

SL1.K2 · Explanation showdown

Earth-centred or Sun-centred?

QualityWhy the Sun-centred model won
Most supportedGalileo’s telescope showed Venus going through a full set of phases. Bessel measured stellar parallax in 1838.
Most comprehensiveKepler’s orbits, explained by Newton’s gravity, describe planets, moons and comets. They predicted Halley’s comet’s 1758 return.
SimplestThe Earth-centred model needed many circles on circles. Ellipses around the Sun need none.

Result: the Sun-centred model (Copernicus 1543, Kepler 1609) replaced Ptolemy’s Earth-centred model (about 150 CE).

Part 2 · SL1.K3

Checking the work

Before research is published, other experts examine how it was done and what the data really shows.

Section opener. Ask: before you hand in a lab report, who checks it, and what do they look for? Students will become reviewers themselves at the Peer Review Desk.

SL1.K3 · Peer review

The path of a scientific paper

1 · Investigate

A team plans a controlled investigation and collects data.

2 · Write

They describe methods and data well enough for others to repeat it.

3 · Submit

A journal editor checks the paper fits and is complete.

4 · Review

Two or three experts in the field examine it, often anonymously.

5 · Decide

Accept, revise or reject. Authors fix problems and resubmit.

6 · Publish and repeat

Others test and repeat it. Results that hold up become evidence.

Science 9 · SL1 · Checking the work

Walk through the six steps. Point out that step 5 can loop several times, and step 6 never really ends: published work keeps being tested. Ask: why might reviewers stay anonymous? Lab link: Learn tab, section 3 (the path of a scientific paper).

SL1.K3 · What reviewers examine

Two things every reviewer checks

Investigation techniques

Was it controlled? Was only one variable changed? Enough trials and a big enough sample? Careful measurements?

Analysis and interpretation

Are the calculations correct? Do graphs show the data fairly? Does the conclusion match the data?

In 1963, two journals rejected Canadian geophysicist Lawrence Morley’s paper on seafloor magnetic stripes. Similar work by Vine and Matthews became key evidence for plate tectonics.

Science 9 · SL1 · Checking the work

Accuracy flag for SL1.K3: the draft says peer review “confirms the validity” of scientific work. Teach it more precisely: peer review checks and evaluates validity, but it cannot guarantee a result is correct, and theories rest on many peer-reviewed studies rather than being reviewed as a single item. The Morley story shows reviewers can reject a good idea. Ask: if peer review is not perfect, what else protects science from mistakes? (Replication by other scientists.) Lab link: Learn tab, section 3.

SL1.S2 · Lab Bench: peer review desk

Review: does road salt melt ice faster?

Manuscript MS-0412 · student example

Salt tray on a sunny windowsill. Sand and plain trays in the hallway. Each tested once.

Melt times: salt 18 min, sand 41 min, nothing 44 min.

Claim: “Salt melts ice 2.4 times faster in all winter weather.”

Technique: two variables changed, salt and sunlight.

Technique: one trial each. Repeat at least three and average.

Analysis: tested indoors at one temperature, so “all winter weather” goes too far.

Recommendation: major revisions.

Science 9 · SL1 · Checking the work

Show the manuscript first and let pairs list problems before revealing the right column. Sort each problem into investigation techniques or analysis and interpretation. One tray per group is also a small sample, a minor point. Reviewer math from Station B: trials of 14.2 s, 15.0 s and 14.5 s give a mean of 43.7 ÷ 3 = 14.6 s and a range of 0.8 s. The manuscript is a made-up student example. Lab link: Lab Bench, Station B (peer review desk and reviewer math); Written Review extended 2.

Remember

Peer review is a strong check, not a guarantee.

Reviewers can miss mistakes. That is why other scientists repeat important experiments.

This slide carries the SL1.K3 accuracy flag: the draft wording says peer review “confirms the validity” of scientific work; the lab teaches it as a strong but imperfect check followed by replication. Quick discussion: which is stronger evidence, one peer-reviewed study or ten studies by different teams that agree?

Part 3 · SL1.K4 and SL1.K5

When evidence changes

New tools reveal new evidence. When enough of it contradicts a theory, the theory changes.

Section opener. Ask: what can you see with a microscope or telescope that you cannot see with your eyes? How might that change what people believe?

SL1.K5 · Restricted, revised or replaced

Three ways a theory can change

Restricted

Still works, but only within a certain range of conditions.

Revised

Keeps its main idea but changes to include the new evidence.

Replaced

Dropped for a new theory that explains the evidence better.

One odd result does not overturn a theory. It could be a mistake. Change comes when substantial contradictory evidence builds up over time.

SL1.K5 · A restricted theory at work

Newton still builds bridges. Einstein runs GPS.

38

microseconds per day: the clock correction GPS satellites need because of relativity

Without that correction, GPS positions would drift by kilometres.

Newton’s laws still work extremely well at everyday speeds for bridges, cars and rocket launches. They are restricted to speeds much slower than light and gravity much weaker than near a black hole.

Science 9 · SL1 · When evidence changes

This is the clearest example of a restricted theory: still correct and useful inside its range. Contrast with replaced theories such as spontaneous generation, which nobody uses any more. Ask: is a restricted theory wrong? Push students to say it is incomplete, not useless. The 38 microseconds figure comes from the lab’s Newton’s laws case file. Part 4 · SL1.K6

Careers that explain phenomena

People in many jobs observe problems, gather data, test ideas and improve how we explain things.

Section opener. Ask students to name an adult they know whose job involves testing why something happens. Most jobs involve more science than people expect.

SL1.K6 · Science-related careers

Four careers, four phenomena

Electricity

Electrician

Tests hypotheses about a tripping breaker with a meter, then explains and fixes the fault.

Agriculture

Agricultural engineer

Uses soil moisture readings and drone images to explain why part of a canola field yields less.

Space exploration

Space lawyer

Uses tracking data and orbital science to work out where damaging satellite debris came from.

Innovation

Hydrogen systems technician

Logs pressure, temperature and power data to explain why a fuel cell truck loses power on cold mornings.

Quick check

Three questions

1

Two explanations of a phenomenon both seem valid. What do scientists need, and why?

2

Name the two things peer reviewers examine.

3

Newton’s laws still work at everyday speeds but not near the speed of light. Was the theory restricted, revised or replaced?

Science 9 · SL1 · Review

Answers. 1: new evidence, ideally evidence that one explanation predicts and the other does not, such as the Chicxulub crater for the impact explanation. 2: investigation techniques, and analysis and interpretation of data. 3: restricted, because it still works within a range of conditions. Extended response · Debate

Is changing your mind a strength?

Some people say, “Scientists keep changing their minds, so we can’t trust science.” Write a response that explains why changing theories is a strength of science.

A strong answer includes

Theories change when substantial evidence builds up · peer review and replication · an example · restricted theories stay useful · a clear position

Science 9 · SL1 · Review

Run as a four corners debate first (strongly agree to strongly disagree with the quoted claim), then have students write. Strong examples: the atom model revised many times while its main idea survived; Newton’s laws restricted but still used to build bridges; spontaneous generation replaced after many experiments.
