Sound waves

Sound waves transfer energy from the source of the sound wave and behave predictably.

Section 1 · W1.K1

How sound starts and travels

A vibrating source, a medium, and paths that spread out in every direction.

This section covers Learn Lessons 1 to 3 in the lab. Ask: what do a guitar, your voice and a speaker have in common? Collect ideas before moving on. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 to 3.

Lesson 1 · W1.K1 · W1.S1

Every sound starts with a vibration

Speaker cone

Crowded bands: compressions

Spread-out bands: rarefactions

A vibrating source moves quickly back and forth: vocal cords, a guitar string, a drum skin, a speaker cone.

Sound is a mechanical wave. Particles only move back and forth. The energy moves on.

Science 9 · W1 · How sound starts and travels

Have students touch their throat and hum to feel the vibration. Then open the speaker model and start it. Ask: does the red particle travel away from the speaker, or just move back and forth? It only moves back and forth around its resting place; the pattern of compressions and rarefactions moves outward. Stop the speaker: no vibration, no sound. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

Lesson 2 · W1.K1 · W1.S2

Sound needs a medium

A medium is the material a wave travels through. No particles, no sound.

Gas

Air

Carries most sounds you hear.

Liquid

Water

Whales call across the ocean.

Solid

Steel, wood, bone

Even your skull carries your voice.

Vacuum

Outer space

No sound. Astronauts use radio.

Bell jar: pump the air out and the ringing fades, even though the bell keeps shaking.

Science 9 · W1 · How sound starts and travels

Open the bell jar model and drag the air slider down slowly. Ask: is the bell still vibrating? Yes. So why does the sound fade? Fewer particles to pass the vibration along. Then tap the six media chips (air, water, steel, skull, ground, outer space). Quick think-pair-share: name one more solid, liquid and gas that carries sound. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2.

Lesson 3 · W1.K1 · W1.S1

Out in every direction, in straight lines

Everyone around a campfire hears the story. In one even material, sound spreads out along straight paths called rays.

Farther away, the energy is shared over a bigger area, so the sound is quieter.

Science 9 · W1 · How sound starts and travels

Open the rings model and tap different listeners: all of them hear the same pitch, and the farther ones hear it more quietly. Accuracy flag (W1.K1): the lab teaches “travel in straight lines” as straight outward paths through one even medium. Sound also bends around corners and through doorways, and bends when air temperature changes with height. Expect students to ask how they can hear someone around a corner; acknowledge it and keep the straight-line rule for one even material. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3.

Section 2 · W1.K2 · W1.S3

Reading sound-wave diagrams

How close the waves are tells you the pitch. How tall they are tells you the volume.

This section covers Learn Lessons 4 to 6 and Lab Bench Investigation 1. Before the next slide, ask: if you could see sound, what would it look like? Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4 to 6.

Lesson 4 · W1.K2 · W1.S3

Turning squeezed air into a wavy line

Top: air particles

Bottom: the sound-wave diagram (air pressure over time)

Crest = compression, where the air is squeezed most.

Trough = rarefaction, where the air is most spread out.

Science 9 · W1 · Reading sound-wave diagrams

Point out that the dashed lines connect each crowded band to a crest on the graph. Stress the common misconception: the diagram is a graph of pressure, not a picture of air moving up and down. In a sound wave, the particles move back and forth along the direction the sound travels. Open the model and press Play to watch crests line up with compressions. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4.

Lesson 5 · W1.K2 · W1.S3

Frequency sets the pitch

Low pitch

Few waves in the same time: low frequency. A bass drum.

High pitch

Many waves in the same time: high frequency. A whistle.

20 to 20 000 Hz

Human hearing range. Frequency = vibrations each second, in hertz (Hz).

Science 9 · W1 · Reading sound-wave diagrams

Pitch is how high or low a sound seems; frequency sets it. Use the pitch dial in the lab and play a few tones (turn the classroom volume down first). Ask students to count the waves in the window at 300 Hz and at 600 Hz. Mention ultrasound (above about 20 000 Hz, bats and medical scanners) and infrasound (below about 20 Hz, elephants and some storms). The lab shows λ = v ÷ f as context only; students do not memorize it. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5.

Lesson 6 · W1.K2 · W1.S3

Amplitude sets the volume

Soft: small amplitude

Loud: large amplitude, same pitch

Example soundAbout
Whisper30 dB
Conversation60 dB
Lawn mower90 dB
Rock concert110 dB

Long exposure above about 85 dB can damage hearing. Protect your ears.

Science 9 · W1 · Reading sound-wave diagrams

Pluck a guitar string or a ruler on a desk gently, then hard. Same pitch, louder sound, bigger amplitude, more energy. Use the lab’s volume dial and example sound chips. Accuracy flag (W1.K2): volume is taught as loudness, what we hear; loudness also drops with distance as energy spreads out (Lesson 3). Decibel values and the 85 dB guide are rounded context values, not for memorizing. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6.

Investigation 1 · Compare two sounds

Closer waves: higher pitch.
Taller waves: louder.

Worked example: A shows 6 waves, B shows 3. A has the higher pitch. A’s crests are half as tall as B’s. B is louder.

Science 9 · W1 · Reading sound-wave diagrams

This is the main idea of the section, and the worked example from Lab Bench Investigation 1. Remind students that pitch and volume can change separately: you can sing the same note softly or loudly. Then have students do five rounds of Investigation 1 in pairs, using the dropdowns for higher pitch and louder, and report their score. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 1.

Section 3 · W1.K3 · W1.K4 · W1.S4

The speed of sound

It depends on the medium and on the temperature.

This section covers Learn Lessons 7 and 8 and Lab Bench Investigations 2 to 4. Return to the hook question: which do students now think arrives first, the tap through the fence or through the air? Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 7 and 8.

Lesson 7 · W1.K3 · W1.K4 · W1.S2

The medium changes the speed

Air

343 m/s

Helium

about 1000 m/s

Water

about 1480 m/s

Lead

about 2200 m/s

Steel

about 5900 m/s

Rounded values at about 20 °C. Speed = distance ÷ time (v = d ÷ t).

In most cases: solids fastest, then liquids, then gases.

Science 9 · W1 · The speed of sound

Run the 1 km race in the lab: the steel pulse finishes in about 0.17 s, water in about 0.68 s, and air in about 2.9 s. Ask: which state of matter wins? Have students identify the medium in everyday scenarios (whale calls, a knock on a door, a tin-can telephone). Values are rounded reference values; steel ranges from about 5000 to 6000 m/s depending on its form. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7.

Lesson 7 · W1.K4

Denser is usually faster, but not always

Water is denser than air, and sound is faster in water. That pattern holds for most everyday materials. But two cases break it:

Lead vs steel

Lead is denser, yet sound is slower in lead.

Helium vs air

Helium is less dense, yet sound is faster in helium.

What matters most is how stiff the material is: how hard its close-packed particles push back when squeezed.

Science 9 · W1 · The speed of sound

Accuracy flag (W1.K4): the draft says sound travels faster “through denser materials”. The lab keeps the draft example (water faster than air) and the general pattern (solids, then liquids, then gases), but explains it with stiffness and adds two counterexamples: lead is denser than steel but slower, and helium is less dense than air but faster. Densities are rounded (air about 1.2, helium about 0.17, water about 1000, steel about 7850, lead about 11 300 kg/m³). This idea returns in the debate slide. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7 (race table shows density beside speed).

Science 9 · W1 · The speed of sound

Use the Alberta thermometer model: tap the January, spring, classroom and July chips and watch the particles and the speed. The formula is a tool for air only; students do not memorize it. Ask: by how much does the speed change from January to July in Edmonton? About 36 m/s. In water, sound is also a little faster when warmer, in most conditions. Values are rounded. Investigation 2 · Thunder timer · W1.S4

How far away is the lightning?

Worked example

A storm near Red Deer on a 20 °C evening. Thunder arrives 6 s after the flash.

v ≈ 331 + 0.6 × 20 = 343 m/s
d = v × t = 343 × 6
d ≈ 2058 m, about 2.1 km

Quick rule

About 3 seconds per kilometre

Light arrives almost instantly. The thunder is the sound that takes time.

If you can hear thunder, go indoors. Practice values in the lab are made up.

Science 9 · W1 · The speed of sound

This is the main Lab Bench investigation. Model the worked example, then have students complete three new storms in the Thunder timer (each storm has a new temperature and time; answers are accepted within 3%). Common error: answering in kilometres when the box asks for metres. Extension: how would the distance estimate change on a −5 °C day with the same time? Safety: if you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 2.

Investigations 3 and 4 · W1.S4 · W1.S2

Echoes and two-path races

Fish finder

A click goes down through lake water (about 1480 m/s). The echo returns after 0.02 s.

d = v × t ÷ 2
= 1480 × 0.02 ÷ 2
= 14.8 m deep

Steel pipe, 500 m

Through steel: 500 ÷ 5900 ≈ 0.085 s
Through air: 500 ÷ 343 ≈ 1.46 s

Two taps. The steel path wins by about 1.4 s.

Science 9 · W1 · The speed of sound

Echo: the sound travels there and back, so divide by 2. A common error is forgetting to halve; the lab’s feedback catches this. The steel pipe answers the hook question: you hear two taps, the one through the metal first. Have students try the Two-path race with water vs air and with air at −30 °C vs +30 °C. Practice values are made up. Safety: never put an ear on a railway or LRT track. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigations 3 and 4.

Quick check

Three questions

1

Why can’t astronauts on a spacewalk hear each other shout?

2

Sound A shows 8 waves and sound B shows 4 in the same time. Which has the higher pitch?

3

Use v ≈ 331 + 0.6 × T. What is the speed of sound in air at 10 °C?

Science 9 · W1 · Review

Answers. 1: Space is almost a vacuum, so there are almost no particles to carry sound; sound is a mechanical wave and needs a medium. 2: Sound A, because more waves in the same time means a higher frequency, and frequency sets pitch. 3: 331 + 0.6 × 10 = 337 m/s. Follow up with a Quiz round in the lab (10 questions from a bank of 24). Lab link: Quiz tab.

Agree or disagree? Use evidence.

Take a position

Always, usually, or never?

Use two examples

One that supports it, one that breaks it.

Improve the claim

Science 9 · W1 · Review

Run as a four-corners debate (always, usually, sometimes, never), then have students write the response in the lab. Model answer key ideas: the claim is often true but not always. Supporting evidence: water (about 1480 m/s) is faster than air (about 343 m/s). Counterexamples: lead is denser than steel but slower (about 2200 vs 5900 m/s); helium is less dense than air but about three times faster. Better reason: stiffness, how hard particles push back when squeezed. Improved claim: sound usually travels fastest in solids, then liquids, then gases, mainly because of stiffness. Accuracy flag (W1.K4): this precision goes beyond the draft wording, so check it against the final curriculum. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 3.
