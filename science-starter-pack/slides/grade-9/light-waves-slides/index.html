Light waves

Light waves transfer energy from the source of the light wave.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Light Waves Lab

Predict

A bell and a lamp sit inside a sealed jar. We pump out all the air. What happens?

A

We stop hearing the bell and stop seeing the lamp.

B

We stop hearing the bell, but still see the lamp.

C

We still hear the bell and still see the lamp.

Have students vote with fingers (1, 2 or 3) and justify with a partner. Do not reveal the answer yet. Answer: B. Sound needs a medium (W1), so the bell goes quiet. Part 1 · W2.K1

How light travels

A source, energy, no medium, straight lines in every direction.

This part covers W2.K1: light waves are electromagnetic waves that require a light source, do not require a medium, travel in straight lines and travel in all directions from the source. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 to 3.

W2.K1 · A light source

Light carries energy from a source

SOURCE

The Sun

Changes nuclear energy in its core into light.

WAVE

Light

Carries the energy outward in all directions.

TARGET

Canola leaf

Stores it as chemical energy in food.

Other sources: a campfire (chemical energy), an LED lamp (electrical energy), a firefly (chemical energy). No source, no light.

Science 9 · W2 · How light travels

Use the list of sources from the cover slide. For each, ask: what energy does it start with? Then ask where the light lands and what the energy becomes there: a solar panel (electrical energy), a leaf (chemical energy by photosynthesis), dark pavement (thermal energy), your eye (signals to the brain). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 (pick a source and a target in the model).

W2.K1 · No medium needed

Light can cross empty space

Light is an electromagnetic wave: a wave of changing electric and magnetic fields. It does not need a medium, a material to travel through.

Sound is a mechanical wave and does need one. That is why the bell in the empty jar went quiet but the lamp stayed bright.

300 000 km/s

Speed of light in a vacuum (3.00 × 10⁸ m/s)

≈ 8.3 min

Sun to Earth: 150 000 000 km ÷ 300 000 km/s

Science 9 · W2 · How light travels

Reveal the hook answer: B. Work the Sun example together: time = distance ÷ speed = 150 000 000 ÷ 300 000 = 500 s, about 8.3 minutes. Extension: the Moon is about 384 000 km away, so moonlight takes about 1.3 s. Students use the formula as a tool; they do not memorize it. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2.

W2.K1 · Rays

Straight lines, in every direction

A bulb sends light out in all directions, so everyone in the room sees it. We draw each path as a ray.

The three-card test: you see the lamp only when all three holes line up. Light cannot curve to find a hole that is out of line.

Light changes direction when it reflects or enters a new material, like air into water. That bending comes in W3.

Science 9 · W2 · How light travels

If you have index cards, a hole punch and a flashlight, do the three-card test live: punch holes in the same spot on three cards, stand them in clay, and line them up with a string pulled tight through the holes. Then shift the middle card. Accuracy note (W2.K1): “travel in straight lines” is taught as straight paths through one even material. Light changes direction when it reflects or passes into a new material, which is W3, so keep that note visible so students are not confused later. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3.

Part 2 · W2.K2 · W2.K4 · W2.K5

Brightness, colour and the spectrum

What a light wave’s size and speed of wiggling tell us, and what a prism reveals.

This part covers W2.K2 (amplitude and brightness, frequency and colour), W2.K4 (the visible light spectrum) and W2.K5 (white light and a prism). Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4 to 6, then Lab Bench Investigations 1 and 3.

W2.K2 · Parts of a light wave

Amplitude sets brightness. Frequency sets colour.

Amplitude → brightness

The height of a crest above the middle line. Bigger waves carry more energy, so the light looks brighter.

Frequency → colour

Waves passing each second, in terahertz (THz). Red is lowest, violet is highest.

c = f × λ  ·  λ (nm) = 300 000 ÷ f (THz)

Higher frequency means shorter wavelength. Use it as a tool, not something to memorize.

Science 9 · W2 · Brightness, colour and the spectrum

Connect to W1: for sound, frequency set pitch and amplitude set volume. For light, frequency sets colour and amplitude sets brightness. Demo in the lab: raise the amplitude slider (brighter lamp, same colour), then the frequency slider (colour moves from red toward violet). Accuracy notes (W2.K2): strictly, the energy carried grows with the square of the amplitude, and brightness also falls with distance as light spreads out. Frequency sets the colour of a single-frequency light; most colours we see are mixes, for example screens make yellow from red and green light. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4.

Science 9 · W2 · Brightness, colour and the spectrum

Walk the bands from left to right with an everyday use for each: radio and TV, microwave ovens and Wi-Fi, TV remotes and thermal cameras, everything we see, sunburn, medical images, cancer treatment. Ask: which of these have you used today? Bees can see some ultraviolet, so “visible” depends on the eye. Visible range is rounded to about 400 to 700 nm (about 430 to 750 THz); band edges vary between sources. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (tap each band).

Model the worked example, then give one to try: 450 THz (answer: 300 000 ÷ 450 ≈ 667 nm, red). Then 600 THz (answer: 500 nm, green). Students then do randomized practice in the lab. W2.K5 · Dispersion

White light is every colour together

Sunlight holds all the colours of the visible spectrum. In a prism, each colour bends a slightly different amount, so the colours spread apart. This is dispersion.

Violet bends the most. Red bends the least. Raindrops make rainbows the same way.

white light

red

violet

Science 9 · W2 · Brightness, colour and the spectrum

If you have a prism, use sunlight through a window or a strong flashlight onto white paper. Safety: never look at the Sun through the prism. Alberta connection: on very cold days, ice crystals can make bright spots beside the Sun called sundogs. Ask: why does violet end up lowest? (It bends the most.) Accuracy note (W2.K5): “white light contains all colours” is taught for sunlight. Many white LEDs and screens make light that looks white from only a few colours, so do not present it as true of every white light. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6.

W2.K5 · Investigation 3 · Newton’s two prisms, 1666

A prism separates colours. It does not make them.

Send one colour through a second prism: it bends, but it stays the same colour.

Tell the story: Isaac Newton let a narrow beam of sunlight through a prism, then used a card with a slit to send just one colour into a second prism. The single colour bent but did not split. If prisms added colour, the second prism should have added more. Ask students to predict before revealing. Summarized generally; please do not add invented details. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 3 (choose a colour for the slit, then try the quick check).

Part 3 · W2.K3 · W2.K6 · W2.K7

Materials, seeing and shadows

What happens when light meets an object, and why the space behind it goes dark.

This part covers W2.K3 (transparent, translucent and opaque), W2.K6 (we see bodies only if they produce or reflect light) and W2.K7 (shadows). Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 7 to 9, then Lab Bench Investigations 2 and 4.

W2.K3 · Investigation 2

Transparent, translucent, opaque

Transparent

Almost all light passes straight through. Images are clear.

window glass

Translucent

Some light gets through but is scattered. Images are blurry.

wax paper

Opaque

No light gets through. It is reflected or absorbed.

cardboard

Light meter test (made-up practice data): a lamp gives 1000 lux. Wax paper lets 420 lux through, which is 42%, and the sign looks blurry, so it is translucent.

Science 9 · W2 · Materials, seeing and shadows

Key vocabulary: transmission (passes through), reflection (bounces off), absorption (taken in, usually warming the material). Challenge question: a tinted car window lets only about 35% of light through, but you still see clearly. Which group? (Transparent, because the image is clear.) The meter readings in the lab are simulated practice data. If you have a light meter app, students can test real classroom materials. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7, then Lab Bench tab, Investigation 2.

W2.K6 · How we see

You see an object only when its light reaches your eye

Luminous

Makes its own light, which travels straight to your eye. The Sun, a lamp, a campfire.

Non-luminous

Reflects light from a source into your eye. A mug, this page, the Moon.

In a room with no light at all, you see nothing. The objects are still there, but no light leaves them to reach you.

Science 9 · W2 · Materials, seeing and shadows

Ask students to draw ray diagrams for two cases: looking at a lamp (one ray, lamp to eye) and looking at a mug (lamp to mug, then mug to eye). Common misconception: rays come out of the eye. Arrows always point into the eye. Moonlight is sunlight reflected off the Moon. Note: transparent objects such as glass are seen mainly because they reflect and bend a little of the light. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 8.

W2.K7 · Shadows

A shadow is where light is blocked

An opaque object absorbs or reflects the light that hits it. Light travels in straight lines, so it cannot fill the space behind.

A wide source makes two parts: the dark umbra and the partly lit penumbra.

umbra

penumbra

object

Move the object closer to the source and the shadow grows. Translucent objects make faint shadows.

Science 9 · W2 · Materials, seeing and shadows

Demo with a flashlight and a hand puppet, then with a long fluorescent tube or a wide LED panel to show the blurry edge. Ask: why are shadows sharp under a small bulb? (Every point of the object blocks light from one point.) Accuracy note (W2.K7): the draft says a shadow forms when light is “absorbed by a body”. The lab teaches the more precise idea that a shadow forms where an opaque object blocks light by absorbing or reflecting it; a mirror also casts a shadow. Umbra and penumbra are added to explain shadow edges. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 9 (try “Wide light” and move the object).

W2.S4 · Investigation 4

Predict the size of a shadow

H = h × D ÷ d

shadow height = object height × (source to screen) ÷ (source to object)

Straight rays past the top and bottom of the object spread apart, so the shadow is bigger than the object.

Worked example

A 10 cm puppet is 50 cm from a small flashlight. The wall is 200 cm away.

H = 10 × 200 ÷ 50 = 40 cm

Move the puppet to 100 cm: H = 20 cm. Farther from the source, smaller shadow.

Science 9 · W2 · Materials, seeing and shadows

Model the worked example, then try one together: a 12 cm toy is 40 cm from the flashlight and the wall is 240 cm away. Answer: 12 × 240 ÷ 40 = 72 cm. Students then do randomized practice in the lab (problems are made-up practice data). The formula comes from similar triangles; students use it as a tool, not something to memorize. Optional hands-on version: flashlight, small toy, metre sticks and a wall; compare measured and predicted shadow heights. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 4.

Quick check

Three questions

1

Why can sunlight reach Earth, but no sound from the Sun ever does?

2

Two lamps both glow green. One looks brighter. What is different about its light waves?

3

Frosted glass lets in lots of light, but you cannot see clearly through it. Is it transparent, translucent or opaque?

Science 9 · W2 · Review

Answers. 1: Light is an electromagnetic wave and needs no medium, so it crosses nearly empty space. Sound is a mechanical wave and needs a medium, so it cannot cross space. 2: The brighter lamp’s waves have a bigger amplitude and carry more energy. The frequency (and so the colour) is the same. 3: Translucent. Light gets through but is scattered, so images are blurry. Follow up with a Quiz round in the lab: 10 questions from a bank of 24. Extended response · Evaluate a claim

“A prism adds colours to white light.”

Use evidence from Newton’s two-prism experiment to agree or disagree. A strong answer:

Takes a clear position

Describes what the first prism does

Describes the second prism result

Explains dispersion as separation

Science 9 · W2 · Review

Run as a four-corners debate (strongly agree to strongly disagree), then have students write. Model answer: the claim is not supported. One prism spreads white light into a spectrum. When one colour, such as green, goes through a slit into a second prism, it bends but stays green, and no new colours appear. If prisms added colours, the second prism should add more. The evidence shows white light already holds all the colours, and a prism only separates them because each colour bends a different amount. This is Written Review extended response 3 in the lab, with a key-idea checklist. Lab link: Written Review tab.
