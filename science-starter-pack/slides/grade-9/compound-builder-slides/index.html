Science 9 · Matter: Elements, Compounds and Reactions · M1

Elements and compounds

Pure substances can be classified by their composition.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Compound Builder Lab

Welcome. Today starts the Science 9 matter unit. We sort every pure substance into elements and compounds, build ions, and learn the IUPAC rules for names and formulas. We finish with WHMIS safety symbols, which we will need for the reactions unit (M2). Students can follow along in the Compound Builder Lab. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

Learning goals

By the end of this unit, I can…

1. identify monatomic, diatomic and polyatomic elements.

2. model the structures of elements and compounds.

3. explain how atoms, elements, ions, molecules and compounds are related.

4. name a two-element compound from its chemical formula.

5. write the formula of a two-element compound from its IUPAC name.

6. count the atoms of each element in ionic and molecular compounds.

7. identify the hazards shown by WHMIS pictograms.

Science 9 · M1 · Goals

These are the seven skills statements for M1 (M1.S1 to M1.S7), written as “I can” goals. Ask students to rate themselves 1 to 3 on each now, and again at the wrap-up. Goal 1 uses “monatomic”; the draft curriculum spells it “monoatomic”. Both mean “one atom”. Lab link: the Coverage tab lists every statement and where the lab teaches it.

Science 9 · M1 · Hook

Give students one minute to write a prediction, then share a few. Listen for ideas like “they make a new substance” or “something happens to the atoms”. Do not resolve it yet. We return to this on the ions slides: sodium loses an electron, chlorine gains one, and the new compound has completely different properties. Safety note: do not demonstrate sodium with chlorine in class. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 (particle boxes include table salt).

Part 1 · M1.K1 to K3

Elements and compounds

Every pure substance is one or the other. Then we look at how element particles are built.

Section opener. Quick review from Science 7: a pure substance has only one kind of particle all the way through; a mixture has two or more. Ask: is air a pure substance? (No, a mixture of nitrogen, oxygen, argon and more.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 to 3.

M1.K1 · Two kinds of pure substance

Elements and compounds

Element

Made of only one kind of atom. It cannot be broken down into simpler substances, even by a chemical change.

O₂ · Cu · He · S₈

Compound

Two or more different elements chemically joined in a fixed ratio. Only a chemical change can split it.

H₂O · CO₂ · NaCl · NH₃

Water is always 2 hydrogen atoms for every 1 oxygen atom. Electricity can split it into hydrogen gas and oxygen gas.

Science 9 · M1 · Elements and compounds

Key move: a compound’s properties differ from its elements (sodium plus chlorine gives table salt, from the hook). Ask: O₂ has two atoms. Why is it still an element? (Both atoms are oxygen.) Check for the common error that “more than one atom” means compound. Splitting water by electrolysis is a chemical change. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1. Students pick each of six particle boxes and decide element or compound.

M1.K2 · M1.S1

Element particles come in three shapes

Monatomic

Single atoms that stay apart.

He · Ne · Ar · Rn

Diatomic

Two atoms of the same kind.

H₂ · O₂ · Cl₂

Polyatomic

More than two atoms of the same kind.

O₃ · P₄ · S₈

Science 9 · M1 · Elements and compounds

All three are elements because each particle has only one kind of atom. Accuracy note (M1.K2): the draft says “monoatomic”; the lab teaches “monatomic” as the common spelling and says both mean one atom. Metals and carbon form networks and are written with the symbol alone (Cu, C), so do not call them monatomic by default. “Polyatomic element” is different from “polyatomic ion”, which is not taught in M1. Alberta link: sour gas plants stack recovered sulfur into yellow blocks made of S₈ rings. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (element particle explorer).

M1.K3 · Atoms acting as one unit

Molecules, from tiny to huge

MoleculeAtoms

A molecule is a group of atoms joined together that acts as a single unit.

It can be an element (O₂) or a compound (CO₂, H₂O).

Careful: table salt and metals are not made of molecules. Salt is a lattice of ions.

Science 9 · M1 · Elements and compounds

The draft lists O₂, CO₂ and DNA as examples. Atom counts: glucose 6 + 12 + 6 = 24; human insulin is C₂₅₇H₃₈₃N₆₅O₇₇S₆, which adds to 788. Alberta and Canada link: insulin was first purified for patients in Toronto in 1922; biochemist James Collip, on leave from the University of Alberta, worked out how to purify it. The DNA figure is left as “billions” on purpose (no exact count). Accuracy note (M1.K3): the lab adds that ionic compounds and metals do not form molecules; NaCl is described by a formula unit. Part 2 · M1.K4 to K8

Ions and compounds

Atoms can gain, lose or share electrons. That decides what kind of compound forms.

Section opener. Review the atom from Science 8: protons in the nucleus, electrons in shells. Ask: which particle can move from one atom to another? (Electrons. Protons never change in a chemical change.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4 to 6.

M1.K7 · Gaining and losing electrons

Ions: cations and anions

Na⁺

Cation (positive)

Sodium loses 1 electron. 11 protons, 10 electrons. Metals form cations.

Name: sodium ion

Cl⁻

Anion (negative)

Chlorine gains 1 electron. 17 protons, 18 electrons. Non-metals form anions.

Name: chloride ion (ends in -ide)

Both now have a full outer shell, like a noble gas. The protons never change.

Science 9 · M1 · Ions and compounds

Return to the hook: this is what changes when sodium and chlorine join. Memory tricks: the “t” in cation looks like a plus sign; an anion is “a negative ion”. Draw the Bohr models: sodium 2, 8, 1 becomes 2, 8; chlorine 2, 8, 7 becomes 2, 8, 8. Ask: if an atom has 8 protons and 10 electrons, what is it? (O²⁻, an oxide anion.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (ion maker: add and remove electrons and watch the charge).

Science 9 · M1 · Ions and compounds

Accuracy note (M1.K8): the draft says a molecular compound is “two or more non-metal atoms that share electrons”. The lab teaches two or more DIFFERENT non-metal elements, since H₂ and O₂ share electrons but are elements. Metalloids are not used in M1 naming. Two metals do not form an ionic or molecular compound; melted together they make an alloy, which is a mixture. Quick sort: KBr (ionic), SO₂ (molecular), Al₂O₃ (ionic), NH₃ (molecular). Part 3 · M1.K9 · M1.K10

Names and formulas

One set of rules, used by chemists everywhere: IUPAC.

Section opener. IUPAC is the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry. Ask: why would a chemist in Edmonton and a chemist in Japan need the same name for a substance? (Safety, trade, research.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 7 and 8.

Reverse it for M1.S5: read the prefixes back as subscripts. Sulfur hexafluoride is SF₆; dinitrogen monoxide is N₂O. M1.K10 · M1.S6 · Reading a formula

C₆H₁₂O₆

6 carbon + 12 hydrogen + 6 oxygen = 24 atoms in one glucose molecule.

A symbol starts with a capital: Co is cobalt, CO is carbon and oxygen.

A subscript counts the atoms of the element just before it.

No subscript means 1. IUPAC never writes a 1.

Science 9 · M1 · Names and formulas

Accent statement slide. For a molecular compound, the counts are for one molecule. For an ionic compound they are for one formula unit, the smallest neutral ratio (Al₂O₃: 2 Al + 3 O = 5 atoms). Quick practice: C₃H₈ propane (11 atoms), CaCl₂ (3), N₂O₅ (7). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 8 (formula decoder: type any formula), then Lab Bench, Investigation 4 (atom counter).

Part 4 · M1.K11 · M1.S7

Hazard symbols: WHMIS

Many pure substances are hazardous. Labels tell you how, before you open the bottle.

Section opener. WHMIS is the Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System. School science labs follow it. Ask: where have you seen a red diamond symbol? (Cleaning cupboards at work, gas cylinders, the school prep room.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 9.

M1.S3 · Putting it together

Molecule? Compound? Both?

SubstanceMolecule?Compound?Why
O₂YesNoatoms share electrons, but only oxygen
NaClNoYestwo elements, but a lattice of ions

Atoms make elements. Atoms that gain or lose electrons become ions, which build ionic compounds. Atoms that share electrons form molecules, which can be elements or molecular compounds.

Quick check

Three questions

1

Is S₈ monatomic, diatomic or polyatomic? Is it an element or a compound?

2

Write the formula for calcium nitride. How many atoms are in one formula unit?

3

Name N₂O₅. Is it ionic or molecular?

Science 9 · M1 · Review

Answers. 1: S₈ is polyatomic (8 atoms per molecule) and an element (only sulfur). 2: Ca²⁺ and N³⁻ balance at 6, so Ca₃N₂; 3 + 2 = 5 atoms per formula unit. 3: dinitrogen pentaoxide (pentoxide is also accepted); molecular, because nitrogen and oxygen are both non-metals. Use mini whiteboards. For more practice: Quiz tab (10 random questions from 26) and Sort It decks.

Extended response · Evaluate a claim

“Sodium chloride is a molecule made of one sodium atom and one chlorine atom.”

What part of this claim is correct? What needs fixing? Use the words ion, lattice and ratio in your answer, and write a better statement.

Science 9 · M1 · Review

From the lab’s Written Review, extended response 2. Use think, pair, share or a four-corners vote (agree, partly agree, partly disagree, disagree). Model answer: the 1 to 1 ratio is correct. But NaCl is not a molecule and is not made of neutral atoms. Sodium gives an electron to chlorine, so the compound is made of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions packed in a crystal lattice with no separate units. The formula shows the ratio of ions (one formula unit). Better: sodium chloride is an ionic compound of sodium and chloride ions in a 1 to 1 ratio.
