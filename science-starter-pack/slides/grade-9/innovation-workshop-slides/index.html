Design and innovation

Innovation stems from creativity when developing and implementing solutions.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Innovation Workshop Lab

Welcome students to the Computer Science unit’s design and innovation lesson. This deck follows the Innovation Workshop Lab in teaching order. Ask: “What is something you use every day that someone invented or improved?” Collect three or four answers on the board and keep them for later slides. Lab link: open the lab’s Learn tab before class so students can follow along.

Give students one minute to choose and write a reason. Take a quick vote. Do not give the answer yet. Answer (revealed on the “idea to innovation” slide): only C is an innovation, because it is an improved solution that has been implemented and is in real use. Part 1 · CS2.K1

Design processes

How a need becomes a solution people actually use.

Section opener. This part covers CS2.K1: design processes lead to innovations when new or improved solutions are implemented. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 and 2.

CS2.K1 · CS2.S1

A design process is a loop

1

Define

Who has the need, and what does success look like?

2

Research

Learn about the users, the setting and existing solutions.

3

Ideate

Brainstorm many ideas. Unusual ones are welcome.

4

Prototype

Build an early model that is quick and cheap to change.

5

Test and improve

Try it with users, collect data, then go back and improve.

6

Implement

Put the best version into real use. Feedback can restart the loop.

Test results send designers back to ideate and prototype again. That is why the steps form a loop.

Science 9 · CS2 · Design processes

Running example: a cold, dark bus stop on a windy Edmonton street in January. Walk through each step for the shelter. Define: riders, including people using wheelchairs and strollers, need a warmer, lit, easy-to-enter shelter. Research: measure wind, ask riders. Ideate: wind walls, heaters, solar lights, arrival signs. Prototype: cardboard model or one test panel. Test: measure temperature inside, ask riders to rate comfort. Implement: install it and keep collecting feedback. Accuracy note (CS2.K1): the six-step loop is one common model; the draft curriculum does not name specific steps, so accept other versions students know from Grade 7. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 (tap each step on the loop).

Reveal the hook answer: only C (low-floor buses in use) is an innovation. A is an idea and B is a prototype. Stress the word implemented: put into real use. Accuracy note (CS2.K1): the lab adds the difference between an invention or prototype and an innovation as context; the statement itself only says innovations happen when solutions are implemented. Discussion: “Can something be an innovation if only a few people use it?” (Yes, if it is in real use by the people it was made for.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (move the slider to “Implemented”).

Part 2 · CS2.K2

The innovation mindset

Innovation is not luck. It starts with intent.

Section opener for CS2.K2: innovation involves approaching design processes with the intent of exploring new or improved ways, creating solutions that add value, and taking risks. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 3 and 4.

CS2.K2

Three intents innovators bring

Explore

Look for new or improved ways to address needs, wants or problems.

Add value

Create solutions that are clearly better for someone.

Take risks

Try ideas that might not work, and learn from them.

Science 9 · CS2 · Innovation mindset

Ask students to match each intent to the bus shelter. Explore: “What if the shelter did not need a roof at all?” or looking at shelters in other winter cities. Add value: making sure people using wheelchairs can get in. Take risks: trying a design nobody has tested. Lab link: Sort It tab, deck “Which innovation intent?”

CS2.K2

Value is the benefit people get

Saves time

Online library holds

Saves money

LED street lights use less electricity

Safety and health

Insulin treatment

Environment

LED lights last longer and use less energy

Access for more people

Low-floor buses with ramps

Ask

Who gets this value, and is anyone left out?

A solution that is new but adds no value is not much use.

Science 9 · CS2 · Innovation mindset

Have pairs choose one innovation from today and name its biggest kind of value. Then ask the “who is left out?” question: for example, online holds help people who cannot browse in person, but only if they have internet access. The value lens bars in the lab are rough class judgments, not measurements. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (value lens).

CS2.K2 · Taking risks

Test small. Fail cheap. Learn fast.

Innovators take smart risks: never with anyone’s safety, always with early tests.

Statement slide. Accuracy note (CS2.K2): the draft lists “taking risks” without limits. The lab teaches it as smart, calculated risk: never risking safety, testing early with cheap prototypes, and treating failure as data. Discuss: “What is the difference between a smart risk and a careless one?” Example: testing a new heater design on a person without safety checks is careless; testing a cardboard shelter model in the wind is smart. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (risk dial).

CS2.K2

Newer ideas, bigger gains, more risk

Small tweak

Likely to work the first time.

The gain is usually small.

Bold new idea

The first version will probably fail.

The gain could be big.

Cheap prototypes keep the cost of each failure low, so a team can afford to try bold ideas.

Science 9 · CS2 · Innovation mindset

Demonstrate the lab’s risk dial live: move newness from 1 to 5 with early prototyping on, then switch it off and show how the cost of failure jumps. Remind students the risk dial is a made-up model that shows a pattern, not real data. Discussion: “Bombardier built and rebuilt many snow vehicles before the B7. Was each failed version a waste?” Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4.

Part 3 · CS2.K3

Responsible innovation

Every solution has impacts, good and bad.

Section opener for CS2.K3: responsible innovation considers positive and negative potential impacts of solutions. This part also builds skill CS2.S2: evaluate potential impacts of solutions. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 5 and 6.

CS2.K3 · CS2.S2

Shared e-scooters: four impacts

People · positive · intended

Quick, cheap short trips without a car.

Environment · positive · intended

Some short car trips are replaced.

Community · negative · unintended

Scooters left on sidewalks block wheelchairs.

People · negative · unintended

Riders fall, especially on rough or icy surfaces.

Intended impacts were planned. Unintended impacts were not, and may show up years later.

Science 9 · CS2 · Responsible innovation

Define impact: an effect on people, communities, the economy or the environment. Ask students to add one more impact of e-scooters and label it. Point out that most negative impacts here were unintended. Accuracy note (CS2.K3): the lab organizes impacts by area, intended or unintended, and short or long term, which goes slightly beyond the statement. Many cities, including Edmonton, have tried shared e-scooters; keep local claims general. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (impact web), and Lab Bench, Investigation 2.

CS2.K3

When good solutions caused harm

Leaded gasoline

Positive: engines ran smoothly without knocking.

Negative: lead in the air harmed health, especially children’s brains.

Response: Canada banned it for cars in 1990.

CFC refrigerants

Positive: fridges became safer, with gases that are not toxic and do not burn.

Negative: leaked CFCs thinned the ozone layer.

Response: phased out by the Montreal Protocol (1987).

Science 9 · CS2 · Responsible innovation

Both harms were unintended and took years to discover. Ask: “What could designers have done differently?” (Test longer, monitor after release, listen to scientists raising concerns.) Note the hopeful ending: the ozone layer is slowly recovering, which shows people can change course. Accuracy flag for Lance: dates (leaded gasoline banned for cars in Canada in 1990; Montreal Protocol 1987) need a quick check. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (choose Leaded gasoline and CFC refrigerants).

CS2.K3 · CS2.S2

Which harm needs attention first?

risk score = likelihood × severity

Homework app sends too many alerts at night

4 × 2 = 8

Student data shared without permission

2 × 5 = 10 · first

Each scale runs from 1 to 5. A plan to reduce a harm is called mitigation.

Science 9 · CS2 · Responsible innovation

Worked example from the lab. Likelihood: how likely is it to happen? Severity: how bad would it be? The data risk scores higher (10 versus 8), so the team deals with it first, for example by storing less data. Students do not need to memorize the formula. Accuracy note (CS2.K3): the risk score is added beyond the statement as one evaluation tool; remind students that values and the voices of the people affected also matter, and a score cannot capture everything. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (risk matrix), and Lab Bench, Investigation 4 (randomized practice).

Lab Bench · CS2.S1

Design sprint: a better bus shelter

Criteria

Comfort 7 or higher
Cost at or under $12 000
Fully accessible
Energy use 10 or lower

VersionComfortCostMet
Old shelter2$01 of 4
Wind wall, heater, solar light8$8 0003 of 4
Add level pad and wide entrance8$10 8004 of 4

Comfort change: (8 − 2) ÷ 2 × 100% = 300% increase. Version 2 can be implemented.

All costs and scores are made up for practice.

Science 9 · CS2 · Practice and review

Walk through the worked example, then let students try their own designs in the lab. Version 1 meets comfort, cost and energy but fails accessibility, so a rider using a wheelchair still cannot get in. Version 2 adds the level pad and wide entrance and meets all four criteria. Energy use is 6 (heater only). Ask: “Which criterion would you add?” Remind students that the shelter only becomes an innovation when it is implemented. Percent change formula: (new − old) ÷ old × 100%. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 1 (design sprint) and Investigation 3 (percent change practice).

Quick check

Three questions

1

A working prototype has passed every test in the lab. Is it an innovation yet? Why?

2

Name the three intents innovators bring to design processes.

3

An impact has likelihood 4 and severity 3. What is its risk score?

Science 9 · CS2 · Practice and review

Answers. 1: No. It becomes an innovation only when it is implemented, meaning put into real use by the people it was made for. 2: Exploring new or improved ways to address needs, wants or problems; creating solutions that add value; taking risks. 3: 4 × 3 = 12, a high score, so the team should plan a mitigation before launch. Use mini whiteboards or a quick hand signal. Lab link: Quiz tab for a full 10-question round.

Debate · CS2.K3

Move fast, or study every impact first?

Move fast

Benefits reach people sooner, and real use reveals real problems.

Study first

Some harms, like leaded gasoline, take years to find and are hard to undo.

Take a position. Support it with at least one example from this lesson, and answer the other side.

Science 9 · CS2 · Practice and review

This is Extended Response 3 in the lab. Run as a four corners or line-up debate, then have students write a paragraph. A strong answer takes a clear position, uses a historical example such as leaded gasoline or CFCs, considers the other side, and connects to responsible innovation. A balanced position might favour fast, small, safe prototypes with careful checks before full implementation. Keep the discussion respectful; some students may have family members who work in industries named here. Lab link: Written Review tab, Extended response 3 (model answer and key-idea checklist).
