Science 9 · Electricity · E1

Circuits and Ohm’s law

Energy can be transferred by a current within a circuit.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Circuit Builder Lab

Welcome students to the Electricity unit. Read the understanding aloud: energy can be transferred by a current within a circuit. Ask: where did you use electric current before you got to school today? (Alarm, lights, toaster, phone, bus.) Every one of those is a circuit that moves energy from a store to a device. This deck follows the Circuit Builder Lab lesson by lesson. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1. All lab and slide data labelled as practice is made up for learning.

Part 1 · Lessons 1 to 3

Current and circuits

What moves in a wire, why it needs a closed loop, and what pushes it.

Section opener. This part covers E1.K1, K2, K3, K4 and K9. Ask students to keep the Learn tab open on Lessons 1 to 3 while you teach.

Lesson 1 · E1.K1 · E1.K2

Current is moving charge that carries energy

Metal wires are full of free electrons. Connect a battery and they all drift the same way. This flow of charge is an electric current, and it transfers energy to the devices in the circuit.

Electrons drift slowly, often less than a millimetre each second. The energy still arrives almost instantly, because every electron in the wire starts moving at once.

1 A = 1 C/s

One ampere: one coulomb of charge passes a point each second. Current = charge ÷ time.

André-Marie Ampère

A French physicist who showed in the 1820s how electric currents push and pull on each other. The unit of current is named after him.

Science 9 · E1 · Current and circuits

Teaching move: open the lab’s count-the-charge model and set the current to 1 A, then 2 A. Ask: what doubled? (The charge passing the checkpoint each second.) Stress that current is a rate, like litres per second in a hose. Review flag E1.K1: the draft says current is a transfer of energy through the flow of electrons. The lab teaches the more precise idea that current is the flow of charge and it transfers energy; electrons drift slowly while energy arrives almost instantly. In liquids such as tap water the moving charges are ions, not electrons (Lesson 8). Review flag E1.K2: Ampère is described briefly as a French physicist; please review the wording. The coulomb and I = Q ÷ t are context only. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

Lesson 2 · E1.K3 · E1.K9

A circuit is a closed loop

Orange arrows: conventional current, from + around to −.

Current flows only around a closed path. Open a switch and the gap stops the current everywhere in the loop at once.

1700s

Conventional current is agreed on: the way a positive charge would flow, from + to −.

1897

The electron is discovered. It is negative, so it actually drifts from − to +.

Science 9 · E1 · Current and circuits

Demonstrate in the lab’s close-the-loop model: tap the switch open and closed, then toggle between conventional current and electron flow. Discussion prompt: if scientists know electrons flow the other way, why keep the old convention? (Every diagram, rule and textbook already used it, and both descriptions predict the same results.) Teacher note E1.K9: the lab dates the convention to the 1700s without naming Franklin, and the electron to 1897. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2.

Lesson 3 · E1.K4

Voltage is the push

A battery separates charge: one terminal becomes negative, the other positive. This creates a potential difference, or voltage, between them.

Voltage tells how much energy each coulomb of charge gains from the source. Join the terminals with a closed path and charge flows.

1 V = 1 J/C

CellsVoltageCurrent

Lab model: 1.5 V cells joined end to end, with a bulb that acts like a 10 Ω resistor. More voltage, more current.

Science 9 · E1 · Current and circuits

Use the stack-the-cells model. Ask students to predict the current for 4 cells before you move the slider. Analogy: voltage is like the height a water pump lifts water; the higher the lift, the more energy each litre has to give on the way down. Review flag E1.K4: the draft says voltage causes current “because of a difference in electric charge between two points”. The lab teaches the more precise idea: the battery separates charge, creating a difference in electric potential energy per coulomb between two points (1 V = 1 J/C). The draft wording is kept as the cause. The table values come from the lab’s fixed 10 Ω bulb model; real bulb filaments change resistance as they heat. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3.

Part 2 · Lessons 4 to 6

Parts and diagrams

The four jobs in a circuit, the symbols we draw them with, and two ways to connect them.

Section opener. This part covers E1.K5, K6, K7, K8 and the skills S1 and S4. Students will need the Learn tab, Lessons 4 to 6, and later Lab Bench Investigation 1 (schematic designer).

Lesson 4 · E1.K5

Four jobs in every circuit

01 · STORE

Energy store

A cell or battery stores energy chemically and supplies it to the circuit.

02 · PATH

Conductor

Wires let charge move between the parts.

03 · USE

Load device

Transfers energy into another store: a bulb (light), a motor (motion), a speaker (sound).

04 · MANAGE

Control device

Manages the current: a switch turns it on and off, a resistor limits it.

A flashlight has all four: batteries, metal strips, a bulb or LED, and a switch. Many LED flashlights also have a resistor to protect the LED.

Science 9 · E1 · Parts and diagrams

Have students tap each part of the flashlight circuit in the lab and name its job before you reveal the card. Quick sort: ask for one more example of each job from around the school (smoke detector battery, hallway wiring, projector fan motor, light switch). The Sort It deck “Which job?” practises this. Review flag E1.K5: the draft lists a resistor as a control device; the lab keeps that and notes once that a resistor also transfers some energy to the thermal store (it warms up). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4.

Science 9 · E1 · Parts and diagrams

Return to the hook: the old holiday lights are in series, so one dead bulb opens the only path. Kitchen lights are in parallel, so each bulb has its own path. In the lab’s series or parallel model, unscrew a bulb in series, then open one branch switch in parallel, and have students describe what changed. Teacher notes E1.K7/K8: “voltage shared between the parts” in series is brief context, not in the draft statements, used again in Investigation 2. “Not every branch needs to be closed” is taught as: other branches keep working when one is open. Ask: which arrangement draws more current from the battery? (Parallel, 1.80 A versus 0.20 A.) Follow-up: so which one drains the battery faster? (Parallel, because more charge flows each second.) Part 3 · Lessons 7 to 9

Measuring and materials

How to read a circuit with meters, which materials conduct, and what slows the current down.

Section opener. This part covers E1.K10 to K15 and the skills S2 and S3. Pair it with a hands-on station using multimeters and low-voltage cells if you can; the lab’s readings are simulated practice data.

Lesson 7 · E1.K10 · E1.S3

Connect the right meter the right way

MeterMeasuresUnitHow to connect
AmmetercurrentAIn series, so current passes through
VoltmetervoltageVAcross a part, one lead on each side
Multimeterall threeV, A, ΩTurn the dial; for Ω, power off

An ammeter across a bulb makes a short circuit. A voltmeter in series almost stops the current. Start on the highest range and check the dial first.

Science 9 · E1 · Measuring and materials

In the lab’s connect-the-meter model, try every wrong combination with the class and read the explanations aloud: they are the most common real mistakes. Why does an ammeter go in series? It must count all the charge passing. Why is a voltmeter connected across? It compares two points. Why power off for resistance? The ohmmeter sends its own small current; a live circuit gives a false reading and can damage the meter. Model readings in the lab: 0.30 A, 3.0 V and 10 Ω for a 3 V cell and 10 Ω bulb. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7, then Lab Bench, Investigation 2.

Lesson 8 · E1.K11 · E1.K12

Conductors and insulators

Conductors

Let charge move through them.

Copper for house wiring. Aluminium for many power lines, because it is light. Tap water, because dissolved minerals form ions that carry charge.

Insulators

Resist the movement of charge.

Rubber and plastic coat wires. Dry wood resists charge. Deionized water has its ions removed, so it barely conducts.

Safety: treat all water, and wet skin, as a conductor around electricity.

Science 9 · E1 · Measuring and materials

Use the conductor tester in the lab. Have students predict lit, dim or dark for each material before you test it. Tap water is the surprise: the bulb stays dark, but a sensitive ammeter shows about 0.002 A (lab practice value), so it is a weak conductor. Deionized water reads 0, until anything dissolves in it. Review flag E1.K11/K12: tap water is taught as a conductor because of dissolved ions (weaker than metals but enough to be dangerous at outlet voltages); deionized water is taught as a poor conductor that becomes conductive once contaminated. Dry wood is an insulator; wet wood can conduct. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 8; Sort It, Conductor or insulator?

Lesson 9 · E1.K13 · E1.K14

Resistance reduces current

Resistance, measured in ohms (Ω), tells how much a part reduces the current. Three properties of a wire change it:

Material

about 65 ×

Nichrome, used in toasters, has about 65 times the resistance of copper of the same size.

Length

2 × longer

Double the length, double the resistance.

Cross-sectional area

thicker = less

A thicker wire is like a wider hallway: more charge gets through.

Reference wire in the lab: copper, 1 m long, 0.5 mm across, about 0.087 Ω. Values use rounded room-temperature data.

Science 9 · E1 · Measuring and materials

Open the wire builder. Ask students to make a wire with the same resistance as the reference but twice as long (they need a thicker wire). Then switch to nichrome and ask why toaster and kettle elements use it (high resistance, so the current transfers lots of energy to the thermal store). Review flag E1.K14: resistances use rounded room-temperature resistivities (copper 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m, aluminium 2.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m, nichrome 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m); the formula is not shown to students. Temperature is mentioned once as context: most metal wires have more resistance when hot. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 9.

Lesson 9 and Investigation 3 · E1.K15 · E1.S2

Resistors control current

A resistor has a set resistance. It protects an LED from too much current. A variable resistor dims a light or sets a fan speed.

In the lab, a 9 V battery runs a 15 Ω lamp through a switch, a variable resistor and a 0.5 A fuse.

WORKED EXAMPLE

R total = 15 Ω + 30 Ω = 45 Ω
I = 9 V ÷ 45 Ω = 0.20 A

Turn the resistor down to 0 Ω: I = 9 V ÷ 15 Ω = 0.60 A. That is over 0.5 A, so the fuse blows and opens the circuit.

Science 9 · E1 · Measuring and materials

Run Investigation 3 live. Ask students to find the lowest resistor setting that keeps the fuse safe (3 Ω gives exactly 0.50 A; anything below blows it). Then ask: which parts here are control devices? (Switch, variable resistor and fuse all manage the current.) Discussion prompt: why is a fuse rated, and why must a blown fuse be replaced with the same rating? Note that series resistances adding (15 + 30) is context used for this example. Lamp modelled as a fixed 15 Ω resistor. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 3.

Part 4 · Lessons 10 and 11

Ohm’s law and safety

One equation that links voltage, current and resistance, and the habits that keep you safe.

Section opener. This part covers E1.K16 and K17 and the skills S5, S6, S7 and S8. Students will use Lab Bench Investigations 4 and 5 and the Learn tab, Lessons 10 and 11.

Lesson 10 · E1.K16 · E1.S6 · E1.S7

Ohm’s law

V = I × R

I = V ÷ R · R = V ÷ I

Same resistance, double the voltage: the current doubles.

Same voltage, more resistance: the current goes down.

Worked example: a 12 Ω toaster element on a 120 V outlet. I = 120 V ÷ 12 Ω = 10 A.

V in volts, I in amperes, R in ohms. You do not need to memorize it: use the triangle in the lab.

Science 9 · E1 · Ohm’s law and safety

Use the Ohm’s law graph: hold R at 10 Ω and move V from 3 to 6 to 12 V; the current goes 0.3, 0.6, 1.2 A along a straight line. Then hold V and raise R. Ask students to say the pattern in words before showing the equation. Practice: Lab Bench, Investigation 5 has a calculator, this worked example and random problems (some in mA, so remind students 250 mA = 0.25 A). Review flag E1.K16: Ohm’s law is taught for resistors at a steady temperature. Bulb filaments are not ohmic because their resistance rises as they heat; the lab models every bulb as a fixed resistor and says so. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 10; Lab Bench, Investigation 5.

Lab Bench · Investigation 4 · E1.S5

Troubleshoot: find the fault

SYMPTOMS · PRACTICE CASE

Three bulbs in parallel on a 6 V battery. Bulb 2 is off. Bulbs 1 and 3 are lit.

Branch 2 current: 0.00 A.

Ohmmeter across bulb 2, power off: 10 Ω.

REASONING

1. Bulbs 1 and 3 work, so the battery and main line are fine.

2. Branch 2 has a gap, because its current is 0 A.

3. Bulb 2 reads a normal 10 Ω, so the bulb is good.

Fault: a loose wire in branch 2.

Science 9 · E1 · Ohm’s law and safety

Model the reasoning out loud, then let students try five random circuits in Investigation 4. Ask: what reading would have told you the bulb was burned out? (OL on the ohmmeter, meaning no path.) In a series circuit with one burned-out bulb, all bulbs go off and the full 6 V appears across the broken bulb, because it is the gap. Case values are made-up practice data; every working bulb is modelled as 10 Ω. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 4 (troubleshooter).

Quick check

Three questions

1

A parallel circuit has branch currents of 0.3 A, 0.5 A and 0.2 A. What is the total current?

2

How is a voltmeter connected to measure the voltage across a bulb?

3

A bulb draws 0.25 A from a 6 V battery. What is its resistance?

Science 9 · E1 · Review

Answers. 1: 1.0 A, because branch currents add up to the total (E1.K8). 2: across the bulb, in parallel, with one lead on each side (E1.K10). 3: R = V ÷ I = 6 V ÷ 0.25 A = 24 Ω (E1.K16). Extended response

Fix the holiday lights

A string of three lights is wired in series, and one dead bulb darkens the whole string. Explain why. Then design a better circuit, describe or draw its schematic, and explain its advantages.

Use the words: path, branch, current, voltage.

This is Written Review extended response 1. Model answer: in series there is only one path, so a burned-out bulb leaves a gap and the current stops everywhere. A better design puts the bulbs in parallel, each on its own branch across the battery. A burned-out bulb then opens only its own branch, the others keep working, and each bulb gets the full voltage, so they stay bright. A main switch before the branches split can still turn them all on and off. Key ideas checklist: one path in series; parallel design; schematic with branches; others keep working; full voltage or a main switch. Extension debate: parallel strings draw more total current from the source. Is that a fair trade for reliability?
