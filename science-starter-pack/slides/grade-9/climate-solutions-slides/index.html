Science 9 · Environmental Capstone · SL3

Climate change and sustainability

Complex environmental issues can be addressed by environmental stewardship and sustainability.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Climate Solutions Lab

Welcome to the Environmental Capstone. This deck follows the Climate Solutions Lab in teaching order: Learn tab, Lessons 1 to 10, then the Lab Bench investigations. Have students open the lab on their devices before you begin. Frame the unit: these are big, tangled issues, and we will look at what people, farmers, governments and industries can actually do. Sensitive topic: some students feel anxious about climate change. Keep the focus on solutions and agency, and let students know it is normal to have strong feelings about it.

Learning goals

What you will be able to do

  • I can research a complex environmental issue and weigh possible solutions.
  • I can examine ways to slow the acceleration of climate change.
  • I can explore government environmental actions.
  • I can investigate critical minerals that support clean energy.
  • I can connect stewardship and sustainability to the demand for resources.
  • I can discuss environmental considerations of AI and digital technologies.

Predict

Could one invention fix climate change on its own?

Write yes or no and one reason. We will come back to your answer at the end.

Give students one minute to write a prediction. Most will say no; push them to explain why. Listen for ideas such as “there are many causes” or “people would still need to change.” This sets up the idea of a complex environmental issue in Lesson 1. Collect a few answers on the board and keep them for the wrap-up. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

Part 1

Big, tangled problems and the data behind them

Complex issues, stewardship, sustainability and monitoring air and water.

Section opener for Lessons 1 and 2 in the Learn tab. Covers SL3.K1 and SL3.K2.

SL3.K1

Five complex environmental issues

Many causes, many people affected, no single fix.

Climate change

Long-term warming sped up by greenhouse gases.

Plastic pollution

Waste that breaks into microplastics.

Resource scarcity

Not enough water or minerals for every need.

Food insecurity

No reliable access to enough safe, nutritious food.

Water and air quality

Pollution that harms people, animals and plants.

They are tangled together: a drought made worse by warming can make water scarce, lower harvests and deepen food insecurity.

Science 9 · SL3 · Issues and data

Ask: Which of these issues affect Alberta? Students may name wildfire smoke (air quality), dry years in southern Alberta (water scarcity), and food bank use (food insecurity). Then ask how two issues connect. The drought example is on the slide. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1, issue explorer.

SL3.K1

Stewardship and sustainability

Stewardship

Taking responsible care of the environment so it stays healthy

Example: a class cleans a creek each spring and records what it finds.

Sustainability

Meeting our needs today without making it harder for future generations to meet theirs

Example: a farm manages its water so there is enough in future dry years.

Both can help address climate change and the other complex issues at the same time.

Science 9 · SL3 · Issues and data

Have students write their own example of each word. Check that sustainability examples look ahead to the future, not just today. Ask: Can one action be both? (Yes: reducing food waste cares for the environment now and saves resources for later.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1; Flashcards tab for both terms.

SL3.K2

Monitoring supports decisions

Canada’s Air Quality Health Index (AQHI) turns air pollution data into a health risk number.

AQHIHealth riskInformed decision for a sports practice
1 to 3LowPractice outside as planned.
4 to 6ModeratePractice outside; students with asthma may take breaks.
7 to 10HighMove hard practice indoors.
Above 10Very highCancel or move all outdoor activity inside.

Water is monitored too: dissolved oxygen, pH, turbidity (cloudiness) and nitrate.

Science 9 · SL3 · Issues and data

Ask: When have you seen Edmonton’s AQHI high? Wildfire smoke days are the usual answer. Monitoring means measuring again and again over time, so trends and sudden changes show up. Then open Lesson 2 and try the river sample check with its three made-up sites; the storm sample fails on dissolved oxygen and turbidity. Accuracy note for the teacher: AQHI categories and advice are paraphrased from Environment and Climate Change Canada; the water guideline values in the lab are simplified for practice. The lab also mentions, in general terms, that many First Nations and Métis communities run their own land and water monitoring programs; keep that general and consider a community partner. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2.

Part 2

Speeding up and slowing down climate change

How human activity accelerates warming, and two ways to slow it.

Section opener for Lessons 3 and 4 and Lab Bench Investigation 2. Covers SL3.K3 and SL3.S2. Sensitive topic: acknowledge that climate change can feel overwhelming, and keep returning to what people can do.

SL3.K3

Humans are speeding up climate change

280

ppm of CO₂ before the industrial era

425

ppm of CO₂ in 2024

+50%

more than half again

Burning coal, oil and natural gas and clearing forests add greenhouse gases. More of these gases trap more energy, so Earth warms faster than natural changes ever did.

Science 9 · SL3 · Climate change

Key message: scientists agree human activity is the main cause of the warming since the 1800s (IPCC). Because humans speed it up, human choices can slow it down. Show the lab’s CO₂ bar chart (Lesson 3) and point out that the rise is getting faster: about 37 ppm from 1960 to 1990, then about 36 ppm in only 20 years from 1990 to 2010. Accuracy flag (SL3.K3): CO₂ values are rounded yearly averages (ice cores before 1958, Mauna Loa after) and need a currency check. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3.

SL3.K3

Two ways to slow climate change

Replace or improve

Swap inefficient technologies for ones that do the same job with less energy

LED bulbs, high-efficiency furnaces, heat pumps, better insulation, electric vehicles.

Switch energy

Move to renewable and low-carbon energy resources

Renewable: sun, wind, moving water, Earth’s heat. Low-carbon also includes nuclear, which is not renewable.

Alberta’s last coal power plants were converted to natural gas by 2024, and southern Alberta now has many wind and solar farms.

Science 9 · SL3 · Climate change

Check understanding with the Sort It tab, energy sources deck: students sort sources into renewable, low-carbon but not renewable, and high-carbon fossil fuel. Common confusion: all renewables are low-carbon, but not all low-carbon sources are renewable (nuclear). Then try the household swap simulator in Lesson 4; household numbers are made up for practice. Accuracy flag (SL3.K3): the Alberta coal-to-gas conversion completed in 2024 needs a currency check. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4.

Lab Bench · SL3.S2

Calculate the savings

emissions (kg CO₂) = amount of activity × emission factor

Gasoline car

15 000 km × 0.2 kg/km

3000 kg

Electric car

15 000 km × 0.08 kg/km

1200 kg

Savings per year

3000 − 1200

1800 kg

Practice values, made up and rounded. That is a 60% cut.

Science 9 · SL3 · Climate change

Walk through the worked example, then have students open the Lab Bench tab, Investigation 2, and do three randomized practice problems. The electric car factor depends on how the grid makes its electricity: a cleaner grid gives a lower factor. Extension: ask what would happen to the electric car’s number if the grid used more wind and solar. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 2.

Part 3

Farming for a changing climate

Climate-smart agriculture, farm technologies and healthy soils.

Section opener for Lessons 5 and 6. Covers SL3.K4, K5 and K6. Many students have family connections to farming; invite them to share what they know.

SL3.K4

Climate-smart agriculture has three goals

Productivity

Grow more food efficiently

Less waste of water, fuel and fertilizer for each tonne of crop.

Resilience

Cope with a changing climate

Handle drought, floods, heat and new pests.

Mitigation

Lower greenhouse gas emissions

Emit less, or store carbon in soil, wherever possible.

A good practice often supports more than one goal at once.

Science 9 · SL3 · Farming

The three goals follow the Food and Agriculture Organization’s definition of climate-smart agriculture. Ask students to give a farm example for each goal. Then use the Sort It tab, climate-smart goals deck, for practice. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5.

SL3.K5

Technologies that support the goals

TechnologyWhat it doesGoals
Carbon capture and storageCaptures CO₂ at industrial sites and stores it deep undergroundMitigation
AI-powered monitoringReads drone, satellite and sensor data to spot crop stress earlyAll three
Smart irrigationWaters only when soil sensors show crops need itProductivity, resilience
Greenhouse systemsGrow crops indoors with controlled heat, light and waterProductivity, resilience

Science 9 · SL3 · Farming

Have students tap each technology in the Lesson 5 model and watch which goals light up. Accuracy flag (SL3.K5): carbon capture and storage is mostly used at large industrial sites. In Alberta, CO₂ from a fertilizer plant and other sites near Edmonton is piped and stored underground (Alberta Carbon Trunk Line); its link to farming is mainly through lowering the emissions of making fertilizer. On-farm carbon storage happens mostly in soil. Please verify current project details. Greenhouses heated with natural gas add emissions, so efficient heating matters. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5.

SL3.K6

Healthy soils store carbon

1

Plants take CO₂ from the air in photosynthesis.

2

Roots and crop leftovers break down into soil organic matter, the dark carbon-rich layer.

3

Healthy soil holds water and nutrients, supporting healthy, nutrient dense crops.

4

Zero tillage, cover crops and pulse rotations build it. Burning stubble and bare fields lose it.

Science 9 · SL3 · Farming

Ask: Why is soil dark near the surface? Organic matter, which is carbon-rich. Zero tillage is widely used on the prairies. Use the Lesson 6 soil carbon model to show the direction of change; its scores are made up. Accuracy flag (SL3.K6): the link between soil health and nutrient density is taught carefully. Healthy soil supports healthy crops, which can be more nutrient rich, but research on soil health and crop nutrient density is still developing. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6.

Part 4

Minerals, governments and growing demand

Critical minerals, government actions, population and the footprint of digital technology.

Section opener for Lessons 7 to 10 and Lab Bench Investigations 3 to 6. Covers SL3.K7 to K11 and S3 to S6. When discussing government actions, stay neutral: the goal is to understand how each type of action works, not to decide which political approach is right.

SL3.K7

Critical minerals for clean energy

Needed for important technologies, with a supply that could be at risk.

Lithium · Li

Batteries for electric vehicles and for storing wind and solar power

In Alberta: dissolved in deep underground brine under central Alberta. Pilot plants test direct lithium extraction.

Uranium · U

Fuel for nuclear power plants, a low-carbon energy source

In Alberta: part of the uranium-rich Athabasca Basin in the northeast, where companies are exploring.

Any extraction must be weighed against its effects on land, water and nearby communities.

Science 9 · SL3 · Minerals and government

Ask: Which devices in this room use lithium batteries? Then discuss why a country might worry about the supply of a mineral. Accuracy flag (SL3.K7): Canada’s operating uranium mines are in Saskatchewan, and Alberta’s uranium work is exploration in its part of the Athabasca Basin, so “developing extraction methods” is taught in detail for lithium and only generally for uranium. Please verify current project status. Projects need agreement with nearby First Nations and Métis communities; keep that discussion general. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7.

Lab Bench · SL3.S4

Lithium from brine

1

Pump brine up from a well 2 to 3 km deep.

2

A material grabs lithium and lets most other substances pass.

3

Wash off the lithium and process it for batteries.

4

Pump the leftover brine back underground.

Worked example · practice values

lithium (g) = volume (L) × concentration (mg/L) ÷ 1000

5000 L × 75 mg/L ÷ 1000 = 375 g in the brine

80% recovered: 375 × 0.80 = 300 g = 0.3 kg

At about 8 kg per car battery, that is under 4% of one battery.

Science 9 · SL3 · Minerals and government

Students open Lab Bench Investigation 4 and move the sliders, then complete randomized practice problems. Ask: How many litres of this brine would one car battery need? About 8000 ÷ (75 × 0.8) ≈ 133 000 L. That scale is why brine is pumped continuously. Accuracy flag (SL3.K7): brine concentrations (30 to 150 mg/L in the lab) and about 8 kg of lithium per electric car battery are rounded estimates. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 4.

SL3.K8 · SL3.K9

Government environmental actions

Regulations

Rules for resource extraction

How mines and wells are approved, run and cleaned up.

Pollution limits

A legal maximum for industry

The most of a pollutant a plant may release, checked by monitors.

Incentives

Rewards for eco-friendly choices

Rebates or grants for green technologies and transportation.

Public support

Educational campaigns explain the reasons. Monetary incentives, such as a deposit refund, make the choice cheaper.

Science 9 · SL3 · Minerals and government

Run Lab Bench Investigation 3, the policy designer: five made-up scenarios where students choose an action type and a way to build public support. Then use the Sort It tab, government actions deck. Keep the discussion neutral: people disagree about which actions are best and how much they should cost, so focus on how each type works. Accuracy note: specific programs and taxes are not named because they change often and are politically contested. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 8; Lab Bench tab, Investigation 3.

SL3.K10 · SL3.S5

More people, more demand

2.5 B

people in 1950

8.2 B

people today

9.7 B

projected for 2050

total demand = number of people × use per person

Population growth pushes demand up. Stewardship lowers use per person, so it matters more as the population grows.

Science 9 · SL3 · Minerals and government

Worked example from Lab Bench Investigation 5: a town grows by 25% (factor 1.25) and saves water so use per person falls 20% (factor 0.80). 1.25 × 0.80 = 1.00, so total demand stays the same. Ask: By how much would use per person need to fall to keep world demand steady in 2050? About 15% (9.7 ÷ 8.2 ≈ 1.18, and 1.18 × 0.85 ≈ 1.0). Accuracy flag (SL3.K10): population figures are rounded from UN World Population Prospects 2024; demand depends on use per person as well as population. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 9; Lab Bench tab, Investigation 5.

SL3.K11 · SL3.S6

The cloud is on the ground

Apps and AI run in data centres: buildings full of real machines.

Electricity

Data centres run all day. About 1.5% of world electricity in 2024, and growing.

Cooling water

Many use water to cool machines, a concern in dry regions.

Minerals

Devices need mined metals such as lithium, cobalt and copper.

E-waste

About 62 million tonnes in 2022; under a quarter recycled properly.

Digital tools can also help: AI that spots crop stress or saves energy in buildings. Good decisions weigh both sides.

Science 9 · SL3 · Minerals and government

Have students complete Lab Bench Investigation 6, digital choices: six choices to rate as raising or lowering impact, then the discussion frame about an AI energy tool for the school. Ask: What could you do with your own devices? Keep phones longer, use e-waste drop-offs. Accuracy flag (SL3.K11): data centre electricity (about 1.5% in 2024, IEA estimate) and e-waste (about 62 million tonnes in 2022, under a quarter documented as recycled, Global E-waste Monitor 2024) need a currency check. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 10; Lab Bench tab, Investigation 6.

Quick check

Three questions

1

Nuclear power is low-carbon. Is it renewable? Explain.

2

Name the three goals of climate-smart agriculture.

3

The AQHI is 8. What should a coach do with a hard practice?

Write your answers, then compare with a partner.

Science 9 · SL3 · Review

Answers. 1: No. Uranium is mined and does not refill, but nuclear power releases very little CO₂, so it is low-carbon but not renewable. 2: Productivity, resilience and mitigation. 3: AQHI 7 to 10 is high risk, so move the hard practice indoors. For more practice, students can do a Quiz tab round (10 questions from a bank of 26). Lab link: Quiz tab.

Debate

Should Alberta expand lithium extraction from brine?

Give at least two benefits for clean energy, at least two environmental considerations, and a clear position with conditions.

This is extended response 3 in the Written Review tab. Strong answers: benefits include supporting electric vehicles and energy storage, using existing well sites and skills, and relying less on imports. Considerations include energy and water use, chemicals, safe reinjection of brine, and effects on land and nearby communities, including First Nations and Métis. A strong position names conditions, such as strong regulations and monitoring. Run as a four corners or a pair debate. Remind students to use evidence, not just opinion. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 3.
