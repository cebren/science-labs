SP2.K1 · Interdisciplinary

One question, many sciences

Biology

What can life survive? Studies extremophiles and the signs life leaves behind.

Chemistry

Which molecules does life use? Identifies gases and molecules in plumes, rocks and air.

Geology

Did water flow here? Reads rocks for past lakes and rivers, and picks landing sites.

Astronomy and physics

Where are the worlds? Finds planets and reads starlight that passed through their air.

Engineering and computing

How do we get there? Builds rovers, orbiters and software, and keeps Earth microbes off them.

Big idea

Interdisciplinary means joining fields. No single science could answer these questions alone.

Science 9 · SP2 · The science of life in space

Ask students which field they would join and why. Point out that the clean-room rule in engineering exists so Earth microbes are not mistaken for alien life. The fields listed are examples, not a fixed list. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 (meet the astrobiology team) and Sort It, Which field? deck.

SP2.K2 · The checklist

What does life need?

Liquid water

Where the chemistry of life happens.

An energy source

Sunlight, or chemical energy from hot rock.

Building blocks

Mainly carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulfur.

Stable conditions

Lasting long enough for life to start and survive.

Not on the list: oxygen in the air, sunlight, or a rocky surface. Many microbes live without them.

Science 9 · SP2 · The science of life in space

This checklist is a common simplification used by astrobiologists, and the lab uses it throughout. Ask: why is oxygen not on the list? (Many microbes live without oxygen, and early Earth had almost none.) Ask: why is sunlight not required? (Vent microbes use chemical energy.) SP2.K2 · Extremophiles

Life at the edges

Extremophiles are living things that thrive in extreme places. If life survives these on Earth, harsher worlds are worth searching.

Deep-sea vents

Total darkness and water hotter than 100 °C near the vents. Microbes use chemical energy, not sunlight.

Under Antarctic ice

Microbes live in dark, near-freezing lakes sealed under the ice, a little like Europa’s hidden ocean.

High radiation

The bacterium Deinococcus radiodurans repairs badly damaged DNA and survives huge radiation doses.

Close to home: Banff

Banff’s warm mineral springs are not extreme, but they host microbial mats and the endangered Banff Springs snail, found nowhere else.

Science 9 · SP2 · The science of life in space

Link each example to a place in space: vents to Europa and Enceladus, ice lakes to Europa, radiation to the surface of Mars. Accuracy note: the Banff springs are warm rather than extreme; they are used as a local example of life in unusual water, and the snail’s status should be checked on the Species at Risk registry. Tardigrades appear in the lab: they survive drying and cold by going dormant, but need liquid water to be active. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (life at the edges).

SP2.K2 · Habitable zone

Not too hot, not too cold

The habitable zone is the range of distances from a star where a planet with enough air could have liquid water on its surface.

Venus · 0.72 AU

Too close: too hot

Earth · 1 AU

Inside the zone

Mars · 1.52 AU

Near the outer edge

Jupiter · 5.2 AU

Far too cold

Science 9 · SP2 · Places to look

The green band is the Sun’s habitable zone, about 0.95 to 1.67 AU. Spacing in the drawing is not to scale. Stress that being in the zone is a starting point, not a promise: Mars is near the outer edge, but its air is too thin to keep liquid water at the surface today. Moons such as Europa lie far outside the zone yet may hide oceans warmed from inside. Accuracy note (SP2.K2): the edges 0.95√L to 1.67√L are a rounded conservative estimate; published edges vary by model. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (place a planet).

Lab Bench · Investigation 2

Worked example: a brighter star

zone edges ≈ 0.95 × √L to 1.67 × √L (in AU)

The problem

A star gives off 4 times as much light as the Sun (L = 4). A planet orbits at 2.5 AU. Is it in the habitable zone?

Practice star, made up.

The solution

√4 = 2
inner = 0.95 × 2 = 1.9 AU
outer = 1.67 × 2 = 3.34 AU
2.5 AU is between them: inside the zone

L is luminosity, compared with the Sun (Sun = 1). One AU is about 150 million km.

Science 9 · SP2 · Places to look

Model the steps aloud, then have students try one from the lab. Point out the pattern: a brighter star pushes the zone farther out, and a dim red dwarf pulls it in close (Proxima Centauri, L about 0.0017, has its zone at roughly 0.04 to 0.07 AU). Students do not need to memorize the formula. Accuracy note: the 0.95 and 1.67 factors are a rounded conservative estimate. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 2 (habitable zone calculator, New problem button for randomized practice).

SP2.K2 · SP2.S2 · Exoplanets

Finding planets around other stars

Transit method

When a planet passes in front of its star, the starlight dims a tiny bit. A bigger planet blocks more light.

dip ≈ (planet radius ÷ star radius)²

Spectroscopy

Starlight that passes through a planet’s air carries fingerprints of its gases. A gas that could point to life is a biosignature.

6000+

exoplanets confirmed by 2025

0.0084%

dip when an Earth-size planet crosses the Sun

NIRISS

Canadian instrument on Webb that studies planet air

Science 9 · SP2 · Places to look

Work the Earth example: (6371 km ÷ 696 000 km)² ≈ 0.0000838, or about 0.0084%. A Jupiter-size planet gives about a 1% dip. Ask why small planets are easier to spot around small red dwarf stars (the ratio is bigger). Examples from the lab: Proxima b (4.2 light-years), TRAPPIST-1e (about 40 light-years), K2-18 b (about 124 light-years; Webb found methane and carbon dioxide in 2023, and a 2025 claim of dimethyl sulfide is unconfirmed). Accuracy note: the 6000 figure is from the NASA Exoplanet Archive in 2025 and keeps growing. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (watch a transit) and Lab Bench, Investigation 3 (transit depth calculator).

SP2.K1 · How science works

“We have not found it yet” is an honest answer.

A claim of life needs strong evidence, ruled-out explanations and checks by other teams.

Accent statement slide. Use the lab’s Biosignature judge reports: evidence of water is not life; a single claim that others cannot repeat stays “possible”; many lines of evidence confirmed by independent labs make a strong case. Ask: why might scientists be extra careful with a claim this big? Discuss the Viking soil tests (1976) as a puzzling result most scientists now explain with soil chemistry. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 1 (biosignature judge).

SP2.K1 · SP2.S1 · Missions

How the search moves forward

1976 · Viking

First life-detection tests on Mars soil. Results were puzzling, not proof.

1995 · 51 Pegasi b

Swiss astronomers find the first planet around a Sun-like star.

2005 · Cassini

Spots water plumes at Enceladus, later flies through them.

2021 · Perseverance

Studies an old Mars lake bed and seals rock samples. Its MOXIE test made oxygen from Mars air.

2021 · Webb

Reads the air of exoplanets with infrared light. Canada built two of its parts.

2030s · Icy moons

Europa Clipper (2030), JUICE (2031) and Dragonfly to Titan (planned 2034).

Science 9 · SP2 · Places to look

Walk the timeline left to right. Point out worldwide teamwork: NASA, the European Space Agency, Swiss astronomers (Mayor and Queloz, 2019 Nobel Prize), and Canada (Webb’s Fine Guidance Sensor and NIRISS). Mention SETI briefly: radio searches for signals from technology, none confirmed; most astrobiology looks for microbes. Accuracy note: dates for future missions are plans and may change. Part 3

Living out there

Lava tubes, research bases and how they could help exploration.

Section opener. Ask: what would make living on the Moon dangerous? List hazards: radiation, tiny space rocks, extreme temperatures, no air. Keep the list for the lava tube slide. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 7 and 8, and Lab Bench, Investigation 5.

SP2.K3 · Lava tubes

Tunnels made by lava

1 · Crust

The top of a lava river cools and hardens into a crust.

2 · Drain

Hot lava keeps flowing underneath, then drains away and leaves a hollow tube.

3 · Pit

Where part of the roof collapses, a pit opens. Spacecraft have photographed pits on the Moon and Mars.

Science 9 · SP2 · Living out there

Drawing: dark rock with a long hollow tube; dashed orange lines are radiation stopping at the rock; the white capsule is a habitat inside the tube; the opening above the middle is a pit. In 2024, scientists used radar data to show that a pit in the Moon’s Mare Tranquillitatis opens into a cave passage, the first direct evidence of an accessible lunar cave. Lava tubes on Earth, such as those in Hawaii and Iceland, are used to train astronauts. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7 (surface or tube?).

SP2.K4 · Labs and habitats

Three ways bases help exploration

Reducing costs

Ice in dark craters near the Moon’s south pole could become drinking water, oxygen and rocket fuel. Lunar soil could be 3D printed into walls and landing pads. Less has to be launched from Earth.

More technology beyond Earth

Workshops, spare parts, 3D printers and fuel depots close to where they are needed, instead of days or months away.

Longer research missions

Crews could stay for months, run long experiments, study samples on site, and use the quiet far side of the Moon for radio telescopes.

Bases also bring costs, risks to crews, and the duty not to spread Earth microbes to places that might have life.

Science 9 · SP2 · Living out there

These three benefits come straight from SP2.K4. Ask students to add one more example for each. Real tests: NASA’s Artemis program plans to land near the Moon’s south pole partly because of its ice; MOXIE on Perseverance made oxygen from Mars air between 2021 and 2023. Lab Bench · Investigation 5

Worked example: a water budget

The problem

A crew of 4 stays 180 days on the Moon. After recycling, each person uses 1 kg of new water per day. Shipping costs $500 000 per kg.

Practice values, made up.

The solution

water = crew × days × kg per day
= 4 × 180 × 1 = 720 kg
cost = 720 × $500 000
= $360 000 000

Melting local ice instead would avoid most of that shipping cost, minus the cost of the ice-mining equipment.

Science 9 · SP2 · Living out there

Model the steps, then let students change one value at a time in the lab and describe the pattern (doubling the crew doubles the water and the cost). Be clear that the shipping cost and water use are made-up practice values, not real mission figures. Extension: have students choose a site and purpose in the Base planner and explain which of the three benefits their base gives. Quick check

Three questions

1. Why is astrobiology called interdisciplinary?

2. A star has L = 0.25. Is a planet at 0.6 AU in its habitable zone? (Zone ≈ 0.95√L to 1.67√L)

3. Give two reasons a lava tube could be a good place for a Moon base.

Science 9 · SP2 · Review

Answers. 1: It joins many sciences, such as biology, chemistry, geology, astronomy and engineering, because no single field can answer whether life exists elsewhere. 2: √0.25 = 0.5, so the zone is 0.475 to 0.835 AU; 0.6 AU is inside. 3: Any two of: rock blocks most radiation; the roof stops micrometeorites; the temperature stays steady. Extended response · Debate

Lava tube or south pole surface?

Should the first permanent base on the Moon be built inside a lava tube or on the surface near the south pole? Take a position and support it.

For the lava tube

Shielding from radiation and micrometeorites. Steady temperature saves energy and protects health.

For the south pole surface

Close to water ice and long sunlight for power. Easier to land and build.

Use evidence for both sides, then answer the other side’s best point.

Science 9 · SP2 · Review

Run as a four-corners debate or a written response. A strong answer weighs both sides: a tube protects crews but is hard to reach, dark inside and must be scouted by robots; the surface is near ice and sunlight but exposes crews to radiation, temperature swings and dust. A reasonable position: start on the surface near the ice with shielded modules, send robots to map tubes, then move living quarters into a tube once one is proven safe.
