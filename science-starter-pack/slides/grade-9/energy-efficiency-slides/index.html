Science 9 · Electricity · E2

Energy conservation in devices

The conservation of energy is evident in the energy transfers of electrical devices.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Energy Efficiency Lab

Welcome students to the second electricity unit. In E1 they built circuits and used Ohm’s law. Today the question changes from “how does current flow?” to “where does the energy go, and what does it cost?”. Have the Energy Efficiency Lab open on the projector. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

Learning goals

By the end of this topic

I can explain the relationships between power, energy and efficiency.

I can solve problems involving energy, power and time.

I can use the law of conservation of energy to account for all the energy in a transfer.

I can relate energy efficiency to energy stores and transfers.

I can solve problems about the efficiency of electrical devices.

I can solve problems about the cost of running electrical devices.

I can explore the sources of electricity in Alberta.

Part 1 · E2.K1 to E2.K3

Power and energy

How fast a device transfers energy, and how much it transfers in total.

Section opener. Ask: what does the W on a light bulb box mean? Collect ideas without correcting yet. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 and 2.

E2.K1 · Power

Power is how fast energy is transferred

1 W = 1 J/s

One watt means one joule of energy is transferred each second. A kilowatt (kW) is 1000 W.

P = E ÷ t

DevicePower rating
LED bulb9 W
Laptop60 W
Microwave1000 W
Kettle1500 W

Science 9 · E2 · Power and energy

Key point: power is a RATE, not an amount. A kettle transfers 1500 J every second it runs. Power ratings in the table are typical rounded values, made up for practice. Ask: if the kettle and the LED both run for one minute, how many times more energy does the kettle transfer? (1500 ÷ 9, about 167 times.) Teacher note: the rating is the rate at normal operation; some devices (a fridge cycling, a charger tapering) do not always run at their rating. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1, energy race model.

E2.K2 · E2.K3 · Energy from power and time

E = P × t

Joules

watts × seconds = joules

Use for short times and small amounts of energy.

Kilowatt hours

kilowatts × hours = kWh

Use for household energy and bills.

1 kWh = 3 600 000 J = 3.6 MJ

Science 9 · E2 · Power and energy

The power rating lets us find the energy input a device needs: multiply by time. Stress matching units: watts with seconds, or kilowatts with hours. A kilowatt hour is a unit of ENERGY even though it contains the word kilowatt; many students think it is power. Show where 3 600 000 comes from: 1000 W × 3600 s. Formulas are shown for use, not memorization. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2, energy calculator.

Worked example · E2.S2

A 1500 W kettle runs for 3 minutes

Step 1

t = 3 × 60 = 180 s

Step 2

E = 1500 W × 180 s = 270 000 J

Step 3

In kWh: 1.5 kW × 0.05 h = 0.075 kWh

Check

0.075 × 3 600 000 J = 270 000 J

Science 9 · E2 · Power and energy

Model the steps aloud. Then give a you-try: a 60 W laptop runs for 2 h. Answer: 0.06 kW × 2 h = 0.12 kWh, or 60 W × 7200 s = 432 000 J. Then let students use the randomized practice. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 1 (power, energy and time), which also asks for power and time as the unknown.

Part 2 · E2.K4 to E2.K6

Conservation and efficiency

Follow every joule through a device. None is made and none is lost.

Section opener. Quick recall from Science 8: name as many energy stores as you can in 30 seconds (chemical, kinetic, thermal, gravitational, elastic, nuclear). Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 3 to 5.

E2.K4 · Law of conservation of energy

Energy is never made or lost

Energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can only be transferred from one energy store to another.

An electric current is not a store. It is a way of transferring energy between stores.

Battery fan, made-up numbers

StoreStartAfter 40 s
Chemical (battery)2000 J1200 J
Kinetic (blades)0 J60 J
Thermal (room)0 J740 J
Total2000 J2000 J

Science 9 · E2 · Conservation and efficiency

Point to the total row: it never changes. Ask: where did the 800 J that left the battery go? (60 J to the kinetic store of the blades, 740 J heated the motor, the air and the room, including sound that ends up warming the air.) Once the blades spin steadily, their kinetic store stays the same; the rest heats the room. Numbers are made up for practice. Teacher note: stores follow the Science 8 list; current, light and sound are taught as ways of transferring energy, not stores. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3, energy ledger.

E2.K5 · Dissipation

Some energy always spreads out as heat

Dissipated energy heats the surroundings. It still exists, but it is spread so thinly that it is hard to use again.

Incandescent bulb · every 100 J in

About 5 J light (blue) · about 95 J heats the bulb and room (orange)

LED bulb · every 100 J in

About 30 J light (blue) · about 70 J heats the bulb base (orange)

Science 9 · E2 · Conservation and efficiency

These bars work like a Sankey diagram flattened into one line: the whole bar is the energy in, split into useful and wasted. In the lab, the Sankey arrows show the same split with widths. Ask: why does an old bulb feel hot to touch while an LED is only warm? Review flag for teachers: the splits are rounded typical values (incandescent about 5% light, LED about 30% light); real values vary by model. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4, Sankey diagram (try all five devices).

The rule that never breaks

energy in = useful energy out + wasted energy out

Every device, every power plant, every time.

Science 9 · E2 · Conservation and efficiency

Pause here. This one line is the law of conservation of energy written as an accounting rule. Students use it in every Lab Bench problem. Ask a student to say it in their own words, then ask: could the useful part ever be bigger than the input? (No, that would create energy.) Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 2, balance the energy accounts.

E2.K6 · Efficiency

Efficiency compares useful output to input

efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input × 100%

Worked example: LED bulb

900 J in, 270 J light out
270 ÷ 900 × 100% = 30%
wasted = 900 − 270 = 630 J

Always below 100%

Over 100% would create energy. Exactly 100% never happens, because some energy always dissipates.

Science 9 · E2 · Conservation and efficiency

Efficiency is a ratio, usually a percentage. Power can be used in place of energy if both are in the same units. Review flag (E2.K6): the draft says efficiency compares “the input from the energy store to the output of the device”. We teach the precise ratio: USEFUL output ÷ TOTAL input. Common error: dividing input by output. Ask: which number must be bigger? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (efficiency gauge), then Lab Bench, Investigation 3.

Labels and bills

E2.K7 · Energy ratings

Two programs help you compare

EnerGuide

A label on major appliances such as fridges, washers and dryers.

Shows the estimated energy use in kWh per year, with a scale comparing similar models.

Run by Natural Resources Canada. EnerGuide ratings exist for whole houses too.

ENERGY STAR

A symbol on models that meet strict efficiency standards.

It marks models that are among the most efficient in their category.

It shows no number. It is a pass mark.

Science 9 · E2 · Labels and bills

Lower EnerGuide number = less energy each year = lower bill and less demand on power plants. E2.K8 · Cost of operating

cost = energy (kWh) × price per kWh

Worked example: space heater

1.2 kW, 5 h a day for 30 days, at 15¢ per kWh

E = 1.2 kW × 150 h = 180 kWh
cost = 180 × $0.15 = $27.00

Back to our prediction

Kettle: 7.5 kWh → $1.13
Fridge: 36 kWh → $5.40

The fridge costs more because it runs all day.

Science 9 · E2 · Labels and bills

Return to the hook. Kettle: 1.5 kW × (10 min = 1/6 h) × 30 days = 7.5 kWh, × $0.15 = $1.125, about $1.13. Fridge: 0.05 kW × 24 h × 30 = 36 kWh, × $0.15 = $5.40. Review flag (E2.K8): the draft says “price of power per kilowatt hour”. We teach price per kWh of ENERGY, since the kWh is a unit of energy. The 15¢ price is made up for practice; real Alberta prices change monthly and real bills add delivery and other charges. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7, running cost calculator.

Lab Bench · Investigation 2 · E2.S3 · E2.S4

Balance the energy accounts

An elevator motor takes in 10 000 J of electrical energy. 7000 J goes to the gravitational store of the lifted car. How much dissipates, and how efficient is the motor?

Step 1 · conservation

wasted = 10 000 − 7000
= 3000 J

Step 2 · efficiency

7000 ÷ 10 000 × 100%
= 70%

Step 3 · stores

The 3000 J heats the motor and cables. It is still there, in the thermal store.

Science 9 · E2 · Labels and bills

This is the main Lab Bench investigation for conservation and efficiency. The values are made up for practice. Model it, then have students do five randomized problems in the lab. The lab draws the Sankey diagram after each check. Extension: the same motor runs on a 2 kW supply for 5 s; is that the same 10 000 J? (Yes, E = 2000 W × 5 s.) Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 2, then Investigation 3 (efficiency problems) and Investigation 4 (home energy audit) for cost.

How Alberta’s mix has changed

For decades

Coal was the main source

Coal plants worked like gas plants but released more carbon dioxide.

2024

Coal ends

The last coal units stopped burning coal. Several now burn natural gas.

Today

Gas, wind and solar

Gas leads. Wind and solar grow fast in the south. Diesel from oil powers a few remote places.

Quick check

Three questions

1

How much energy does a 60 W bulb transfer in 100 s?

2

A bulb takes in 200 J and gives out 50 J of light. What is its efficiency, and where did the other 150 J go?

3

A 0.1 kW TV runs 4 h a day for 30 days at 15¢ per kWh. What does it cost?

Science 9 · E2 · Review

Answers. 1: E = 60 W × 100 s = 6000 J. 2: 50 ÷ 200 × 100% = 25%; the other 150 J dissipated, heating the bulb and the room; it was not destroyed. 3: E = 0.1 kW × 4 h × 30 = 12 kWh; cost = 12 × $0.15 = $1.80 (practice price). Use mini whiteboards. If many miss question 2, revisit the energy-in rule slide. Lab link: Quiz tab (10 questions from a bank of 25).

Extended response · E2.S1 · E2.S6

Is switching to LED bulbs worth it?

Your family wants to replace ten 60 W incandescent bulbs with 9 W LED bulbs that give the same light. Each bulb is on 5 h a day.

Energy: find the kWh used in one year by each set.

Cost: use the practice price of 15¢ per kWh.

Explain: use efficiency and dissipation to say why.

Science 9 · E2 · Review

Model answer (from the Written Review, extended response 1). Old bulbs: 10 × 0.06 kW × 5 h × 365 = 1095 kWh, about $164. LEDs: 10 × 0.009 kW × 5 × 365 = 164.25 kWh, about $24.64. Saving about 931 kWh and about $140 a year. LEDs turn a larger share of their input into light; incandescent bulbs dissipate most of their energy by heating the room. Debate extension: “In an Edmonton winter, the heat from old bulbs is not wasted.” Students should weigh that the furnace (usually gas) heats the house at lower cost, and in summer the extra heat is unwanted. Key ideas checklist: energy for both, cost for both, efficiency explanation, link to conservation, clear conclusion. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 1.
