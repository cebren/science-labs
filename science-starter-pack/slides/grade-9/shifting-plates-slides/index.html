Earth’s layers and plate tectonics

The layers of Earth behave differently and contribute to geological processes that continually reshape Earth’s surface.

Part 1 · Lessons 1 to 3

Inside Earth

Crust, mantle and core. The stiff lithosphere and the soft layer under it. What makes the plates move.

This part covers ES1.K1, K2 and K3. Ask: no one has ever drilled through the crust, so how could we know what is inside Earth? ES1.K1 · Lesson 1

Crust, mantle and core

Crust

7 km ocean · 35 to 40 km land

Solid rock

The thin outer layer we live on.

Mantle

to about 2900 km deep

Mostly solid rock

The thickest layer. Hot rock that flows a few centimetres a year.

Outer core

2900 to 5150 km

Liquid metal

Iron and nickel. Its swirling makes the magnetic field.

Inner core

5150 to 6371 km

Solid metal

Hottest layer, about 5400 °C, kept solid by huge pressure.

If Earth were an apple, the crust would be thinner than the skin.

Science 9 · ES1 · Inside Earth

Teach the four layers from the outside in. Stress two surprises: the mantle is mostly solid even though it flows, and the inner core is solid even though it is the hottest layer, because of pressure. Scientists know about the liquid outer core because S waves from earthquakes cannot pass through it. Depths and temperatures are rounded reference values. Accuracy note for teachers: values are rounded; the crust in the lab diagram is drawn thicker than true scale.

ES1.K2 · Lesson 2

Two ways to slice the top of Earth

By what it is made of

Crust on top of mantle rock

The boundary is a change in the kind of rock.

By how it behaves

Lithosphere on top of asthenosphere

Stiff, solid shell (about 100 km) over hot, soft rock that flows slowly.

A tectonic plate is a piece of lithosphere: the crust plus the uppermost solid part of the mantle.

Science 9 · ES1 · Inside Earth

Common confusion: students think plates are just crust. The lithosphere includes the top of the mantle. Analogy: a cracked eggshell (lithosphere) sitting on a soft layer (asthenosphere). Ask: which word describes material, and which describes behaviour? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (switch between “What it is made of” and “How it behaves”).

ES1.K3 · Lesson 3

What moves the plates

Heat

Convection currents

Mantle rock heated from below rises, cools near the top, then sinks again. Plates ride on top of the loop.

Gravity

Slab pull

An old, cold, dense plate edge sinks into the mantle and pulls the rest of the plate behind it.

Gravity

Ridge push

New plate is hot and sits high at a ridge. As it cools it slides down and away.

Mantle rock moves only a few centimetres a year, like soup in a pot in very slow motion.

Science 9 · ES1 · Inside Earth

Demonstrate convection with food colouring in warm water if you have time. Link warm material to lower density. ES1.K3 names both convection currents and gravitational forces; the lab teaches gravity as slab pull and ridge push. Accuracy note: many geologists consider slab pull the largest force on most plates, while the draft K6 names only heat-related convection. Part 2 · Lessons 4 to 6

From drift to plates

How one scientist’s puzzle became a theory that scientists around the world built together.

This part covers ES1.K4 to K7 and skill S2. Hold up a world map or globe and ask students what they notice about the coasts of South America and Africa. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4 to 6.

ES1.K4 · Lesson 4

Wegener’s idea: continental drift

1912

German scientist Alfred Wegener proposes that all the continents were once joined in one land mass.

Pangaea

He named it Pangaea, “all lands”. It began breaking apart about 200 million years ago.

Drift

The pieces slowly drifted apart over millions of years to where they are today.

Science 9 · ES1 · From drift to plates

Accuracy flag (ES1.K4): the draft calls it “the theory of continental drift”. Teach it as Wegener’s proposal, which is commonly called the theory of continental drift, and point out that most scientists did not accept it for about 40 years. This sets up skill S2. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (drag the slider back to 200 million years ago).

ES1.K5 · Lesson 4

Wegener’s evidence

Shapes of the continents

The east coast of South America fits into the west coast of Africa like puzzle pieces. The fit is even better at the edges of the continental shelves.

Matching fossils

FossilFound in
Mesosaurus, a freshwater reptileBrazil and southern Africa
Glossopteris, a seed fernSouth America, Africa, India, Antarctica, Australia
Cynognathus, a land reptileSouth America and Africa

Science 9 · ES1 · From drift to plates

Ask: why is a freshwater reptile like Mesosaurus such strong evidence? (It could not swim across a salty ocean.) Why do heavy Glossopteris seeds matter? (They could not blow across an ocean.) The simplest explanation is that the land was once joined. Wegener also used matching rock layers, matching mountain belts and signs of ancient glaciers in places that are warm today. ES1.K5 names continent shapes and fossils; the other clues are brief context. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 fossil buttons.

Why it was rejected

Wegener had strong evidence, but no force that could move a continent.

He suggested continents ploughed through the ocean floor. Other scientists showed the rock was far too strong for that.

Pause here for discussion: is evidence enough to accept an idea, or do scientists also need an explanation of how it works? This connects to Grade 9 scientific literacy work on how theories change. Answer to come: the mechanism arrived with mantle convection, seafloor spreading and gravity. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 closing paragraph.

This is the key slide for skill S2. Ask pairs to explain the difference in one sentence each, then share. Science 9 · ES1 · From drift to plates

Analogy: wood floating on water. Less dense continental crust floats higher, which is why continents stand above sea level. Oceanic crust is young because it keeps sinking back into the mantle. Accuracy flag (ES1.K7): the draft classifies whole plates as oceanic or continental. Most large plates carry both kinds, so the lab teaches oceanic and continental lithosphere and notes that a plate is often named by the kind at its leading edge. Values are rounded. A surface that keeps changing

The evidence that plates move, what happens where they meet, and how the Rockies west of Edmonton were built.

This part covers ES1.K8, K9 and skill S3. Ask: have you been to the mountains? What kinds of rock did you see? Some students may have seen fossil shells in Banff or Jasper. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 7 and 8, and the Lab Bench tab.

ES1.K8 · Lesson 7

Four clues that plates move

Seafloor spreading

New ocean floor forms at ridges and moves away on both sides.

Mountain formation

Plates pushing together crumple rock into ranges with sea fossils near the tops.

Seismic activity

Earthquakes cluster in narrow belts along plate edges.

Volcanic activity

Most volcanoes sit at spreading ridges or above sinking plates.

Science 9 · ES1 · A changing surface

Discussion prompt: if earthquakes happened randomly everywhere, would that support or weaken plate tectonics? (Weaken. Their pattern along plate edges is what makes them evidence.) Mention that a few volcanoes, like Hawaii, sit over hot spots in the middle of a plate. Geological hazards are taught in ES2; keep this slide on evidence. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7 (“Tap a clue”).

ridge: youngest rock

older rock →

New rock records Earth’s magnetic field as it cools. The field flips from time to time. Dark stripes formed with the field as it is today, light stripes when it was reversed. The pattern matches on both sides.

Lab Bench · Investigation 2 · ES1.S3

How far do plates travel?

v = d ÷ t  so  d = v × t

The Atlantic widens about 2.5 cm a year. How far does it spread in 1 million years?

d = 2.5 cm/yr × 1 000 000 yr
= 2 500 000 cm
÷ 100 000 cm per km = 25 km

Over 180 million years

≈ 4500 km

Tiny yearly steps add up to the width of an ocean.

Science 9 · ES1 · A changing surface

Model the worked example, then have students try the randomized practice in the lab. Students do not need to memorize the formula. Shortcut: 1 cm per year for 1 million years is 10 km. Return to the hook: Edmonton moves about 2 cm a year on the North American Plate, so about 20 km per million years. The 4500 km figure is an estimate using a steady rate. ES1.K9 · Lesson 8

How the Rockies formed

1

Shallow seas

Sand, mud and shells settle in flat layers and harden into sedimentary rock.

2

A plate sinks

An oceanic plate to the west sinks under North America.

3

Islands collide

Island chains crash into the coast and squeeze the land: compression.

4

Layers stack up

Rock layers slide east along thrust faults and pile up like shoved rugs.

5

Carved today

Rivers and glaciers carve peaks. Sea fossils sit high in Banff and Jasper.

At Mount Yamnuska near Canmore, older limestone sits on top of younger rock along the McConnell Thrust.

Science 9 · ES1 · A changing surface

Walk through the five steps, ideally with the lab’s step-by-step diagram projected. Ask: why is older rock on top of younger rock at Yamnuska surprising? (Normally younger layers settle on top; a thrust fault pushed an older slab over a younger one.) Accuracy flag (ES1.K9): the draft says collision and compression of plates. Answers. 1: the outer core, liquid iron and nickel. 2: a mechanism (mantle convection and gravity: slab pull and ridge push) plus new evidence such as seafloor spreading and magnetic stripes. 3: the oceanic plate sinks because it is denser; this is subduction. Science 9 · ES1 · Review

This is Written Review extended response 1 in the lab. The five boxes are the key-idea checklist. Model answer: Continental drift (Wegener, 1912) said the continents were once joined as Pangaea and drifted apart. It was supported by the fit of the coastlines and by matching fossils such as Mesosaurus, but it could not explain what moved the continents, so it was rejected. In the 1950s and 1960s, ocean-floor mapping, rock ages and magnetic stripes showed seafloor spreading. Plate tectonics built on drift: the lithosphere is broken into seven major plates and many smaller ones that move over the upper mantle, driven by convection and gravity. It explains why continents move and also where earthquakes, volcanoes and mountains form. It was accepted because it had a mechanism and many lines of evidence. Option: run as a debate, one side defending Wegener in 1925 and one side in 1968. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 1.
