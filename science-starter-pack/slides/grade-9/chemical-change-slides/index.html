Science 9 · Matter: Elements, Compounds and Reactions · M2

Chemical reactions

Chemical reactions of pure substances can be determined by observable and quantifiable changes.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Chemical Change Lab

Welcome students to the M2 unit on chemical reactions. Read the understanding statement aloud and underline two words: observable (what we can see, smell or feel) and quantifiable (what we can measure, like mass). Tell students that by the end they will be able to prove a reaction happened, weigh it, write it as an equation and explain it with electrons. Lab link: open the Chemical Change Lab, Learn tab, so students see where the interactive models live.

Predict

A sealed bottle fizzes. What happens to its mass?

Vinegar and baking soda sit in a capped bottle on a scale. It reads 160.00 g. You tip it to mix them, and it fizzes hard.

A

More than 160.00 g

B

Exactly 160.00 g

C

Less than 160.00 g

Science 9 · M2 · Launch

Have students vote with fingers (1, 2 or 3) and justify their choice to a partner. Do not reveal the answer yet. Many will pick C because “the gas is lighter” or A because “something new was made”. The answer is B: the bottle is a closed system, so the carbon dioxide stays inside and mass is conserved. Return to this prediction on the mass slide. The 160.00 g value is made up for practice. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (weigh a reaction).

Part 1 · M2.K1, K3, K4

What is a chemical change?

Atoms regroup into new substances, and we spot it by the evidence.

Section opener. Ask: “What is the difference between ice melting and toast burning?” Collect ideas on the board and keep them for the physical versus chemical slide. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 to 3.

M2.K1 · M2.S6

Atoms regroup into new substances

Reactants

2H₂ + O₂

Products

2H₂O

Grey = hydrogen, red = oxygen. 4 H and 2 O before. 4 H and 2 O after.

Same atoms, new partners, new substances with new properties.

Science 9 · M2 · What is a chemical change?

Key idea (M2.K1): reactants regroup into products; no atoms appear or disappear. Ask students to count the atoms on both sides with you. Contrast properties: hydrogen and oxygen both feed fire, but water puts fires out. Alberta link from the lab: natural gas (methane) burning in a furnace regroups into carbon dioxide and water vapour. This is also the first model (M2.S6). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1. Press React on each reaction, including methane burning and making ammonia. Note: the lab draws sodium chloride as Na and Cl pairs for counting only; it is really a lattice of ions.

M2.K3 · M2.S1

Four kinds of evidence

Colour or odour

Blue copper(II) sulfate fades as reddish-brown copper coats steel wool.

Gas produced

Baking soda fizzes in vinegar as carbon dioxide forms.

Precipitate

A solid forms when two solutions mix. Calcium chloride and baking soda solutions turn cloudy white.

Energy change

Light is given off, or the temperature rises or falls. A glow stick glows.

Science 9 · M2 · What is a chemical change?

Teach the four clues from M2.K3. For each card ask: “What would you see, hear, smell or feel?” Safety: never sniff a chemical directly; demonstrate wafting. Precipitate is a new word: a solid that forms when two solutions are mixed. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (evidence spotter) has six scenes; students tick every clue they see. Then Lab Bench, Investigation 1 (reaction trials) records evidence in a results table. These virtual trials are practice; pair them with real reactions for M2.S1. Confirm concentrations and disposal for copper(II) sulfate and calcium chloride with your division’s guidelines.

Careful

One clue is not proof.

Boiling water makes bubbles, but the gas is still water. Look for more than one clue, and ask: was a new substance made?

Accent slide. Ask: “If bubbles always meant a chemical change, what would boiling water be?” Evidence suggests a chemical change; it does not prove one. The more clues, the stronger the case. Accuracy note for the teacher (M2.K3): the lab teaches evidence as suggesting, not proving, a chemical change, and uses boiling water as the trap case. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 1, trial “Boiling water”.

M2.K4 uses melting ice and dissolving sugar as the physical change examples. Stress the test question: were new substances made? Sugar dissolving is physical because the sugar particles spread out but stay sugar, and evaporation recovers it. Teacher note from the lab: students are not asked about borderline cases such as salts that warm or cool water as they dissolve. Part 2 · M2.K2, K7

Mass and equations

Every atom is accounted for. We can weigh it and we can write it.

Section opener for conservation of mass and chemical equations. Remind students of their prediction on the launch slide. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4 and 7; Lab Bench, Investigations 2 and 3.

M2.K2

Mass of reactants = mass of products

Closed bottle (g)

182.40 → 182.40

The gas is trapped. Nothing enters or leaves.

Open flask (g)

182.40 → 180.75

Carbon dioxide escapes into the room.

Steel wool burns (g)

5.00 → 5.42

Oxygen from the air joins the iron.

Matter is not created or destroyed. To test the law, use a closed system. Antoine Lavoisier showed this in the late 1700s by weighing sealed reactions.

Science 9 · M2 · Mass and equations

Reveal the launch prediction: B, the sealed bottle stays the same. Then ask why the open flask seems to lose mass (the gas leaves) and why steel wool seems to gain mass (oxygen from the air joins). The mass was never created or destroyed. All three sets of values are made up for practice. Accuracy note (M2.K2): the lab teaches conservation for a closed system and shows the open-container cases so students do not think mass vanishes. Real classroom bottles should use small amounts, because gas pressure builds up. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (weigh a reaction), with the three set-ups.

Lab Bench · Investigation 2 · M2.S3

Weigh before and after

An open flask of vinegar and baking soda has a mass of 182.40 g before mixing and 180.75 g after the fizzing stops. How much gas escaped?

In a capped bottle, the scale would still read 182.40 g, because the gas stays inside.

gas escaped = before − after

= 182.40 g − 180.75 g

= 1.65 g

The atoms did not vanish. They left as carbon dioxide gas.

Science 9 · M2 · Mass and equations

Work through the example together, then have students run the virtual trials. The values are made up for practice. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 2 (conservation of mass). Students run open and closed trials, enter the change in mass, then try the randomized practice problems (iron and sulfur in a closed container, chalk and acid, steel wool burning, a sealed bottle). For M2.S3, follow up with a real closed-bottle trial using small amounts; wear goggles because pressure can pop a cap.

M2.K7 · M2.S5

Three ways to write one reaction

1 · Word equation

hydrogen + oxygen → water

2 · Chemical equation

H₂ + O₂ → H₂O (2 O left, 1 O right: not balanced)

3 · Balanced equation

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (4 H, 2 O on each side)

Balance with coefficients, the numbers in front of formulas. Never change a subscript: that would change the substance.

Science 9 · M2 · Mass and equations

The arrow means “react to make”. Show why the unbalanced equation breaks conservation of mass: an oxygen atom would vanish. Accuracy note (M2.K7, M2.S5): the draft does not say whether balancing is expected. The lab teaches word equations, chemical equations and simple balancing by atom counting, because balancing shows conservation of mass. State symbols are not used. Please confirm against the final curriculum. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7 (one reaction, four ways) toggles between not balanced and balanced with a particle model and an atom count.

Part 3 · M2.K5, K6

Electrons and reactivity

Why do some elements react so easily while others almost never react?

Section opener. Quick recall from M1: atoms gain or lose electrons to become ions. Ask: “Why does sodium form Na⁺ and not Na²⁺?” Keep the question open until the reactivity slide. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 5 and 6; Lab Bench, Investigation 4.

M2.K6 · M2.S7

Full outer shells are stable

Valence electrons are the electrons in the outermost shell. For elements 1 to 20, shells fill as 2, then 8, then 8. A full valence shell, like a noble gas, is the most stable.

Group12131415161718
Valence electrons12345678 (He 2)
Usuallylose 1lose 2lose 3sharegain 3gain 2gain 1stable

Example: magnesium is 2, 8, 2. Losing 2 is easier than gaining 6, so it forms Mg²⁺ with neon’s full shells.

Science 9 · M2 · Electrons and reactivity

Accuracy note (M2.K6): “full” is taught as the noble gas arrangement: 2 in the first shell and 8 in the next shells, using a 2, 8, 8, 2 model for elements 1 to 20. This is a simplification of the octet idea. Group 13 losing 3 fits aluminum; boron usually shares and is left out of the lab’s predictor, as is beryllium. Hydrogen is treated as sharing with non-metals. Group 15 to 17 atoms gain electrons with metals and share with other non-metals. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (shell builder for elements 1 to 20); Lab Bench, Investigation 4 (electron predictor) with the magnesium worked example.

M2.K5 · M2.S7

Sodium meets chlorine

Na 2, 8, 1 → Na⁺ 2, 8

Cl 2, 8, 7 → Cl⁻ 2, 8, 8

Each atom is one electron away from a full shell, so both are very reactive. Sodium gives its 1 valence electron to chlorine. Noble gases, already full, barely react.

Science 9 · M2 · Electrons and reactivity

Green circles are valence electrons; purple are inner electrons. Answer the opener: sodium forms Na⁺ because losing one electron leaves a full shell like neon; losing a second would break into a full shell. The opposite charges of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ attract to form sodium chloride. Accuracy note (M2.K5): the draft says reactivity depends on the number of valence electrons. The lab teaches this as the main factor for elements and adds once that atom size also matters: potassium reacts with water more violently than sodium, although both have 1 valence electron. Reactivity of compounds is not taught. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (press Transfer the electron).

Part 4 · M2.K8, K9

Rate and safety

How to speed a reaction up, and how to stay safe while you do it.

Section opener. Ask: “Why does milk last longer in the fridge than on the counter?” Collect answers, then connect them to collisions. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 8 and 9.

M2.K8

Collisions make reactions happen

Particles react only when they collide hard enough to break their old bonds. More useful collisions means a faster reaction rate.

Higher temperature

Particles move faster, so they collide more often and with more energy. Food spoils slower in a fridge.

Higher concentration

More reactant particles in the same space bump into each other more often. Dilute acid reacts more slowly.

Also: crushing a solid into powder gives more surface area, so it reacts faster.

Science 9 · M2 · Rate and safety

M2.K8 names temperature and concentration as factors that affect how often particles collide. Accuracy note: the lab teaches temperature as raising both how often and how hard particles collide. Surface area is added once as context; catalysts are not taught. Demo idea: a fizzing tablet in hot and cold water. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 8 (collision box). Have students run 0 °C and 80 °C, then low and high concentration, and compare “Collisions that reacted”. The collision box is a simplified model with made-up energy thresholds. Sort It, deck “Faster or slower?”.

M2.K9 · M2.S4

WHMIS and lab safety

Read the label

WHMIS pictograms in red diamonds, a signal word (“Danger” or “Warning”) and safety steps. The safety data sheet (SDS) has full details and first aid.

Protect yourself

Goggles on for the whole lab. Tie back hair. Never taste. Waft, never sniff.

Handle with care

Use small amounts. Never return extra to the stock bottle. Report every spill. Dispose as directed and wash your hands.

Today’s hazards: hydrochloric acid stock is corrosive. Magnesium ribbon and hydrogen gas are flammable.

Science 9 · M2 · Rate and safety

WHMIS is the Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System, Canada’s system for labelling hazardous products at work and at school. Discussion (M2.S4): which rule protects you, and which protects the next class? Accuracy note (M2.K9): safety rules here are general and pictograms in the lab are simplified drawings for learning. Check example hazards (hydrochloric acid stock solution, magnesium ribbon, copper(II) sulfate, calcium chloride) against the SDSs and procedures in your own lab. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 9 (pictograms and the seven-step safe choices walk-through); Sort It, deck “Safe or unsafe?”.

Quick check

Three questions

1

Two clear solutions are mixed and a white solid settles out. What is the solid called, and what does it suggest?

2

Balance: Mg + O₂ → MgO

3

Why is neon unreactive while fluorine reacts easily?

Science 9 · M2 · Review

Answers. 1: a precipitate; it is evidence of a chemical change because a new solid formed from the solutions. 2: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (2 Mg and 2 O on each side). 3: neon has a full valence shell of 8, so it is stable; fluorine has 7 valence electrons and gains (or shares) one electron to fill its shell. Extended response

Design a test of conservation of mass

Design an investigation to show that mass is conserved when baking soda reacts with vinegar. Include:

Your set-up, with the reactants kept apart at first

The measurements you would take

The result you expect, and why

Why an open container gives a different result

Add one safety step.

Science 9 · M2 · Review

This is extended response 1 from the lab’s Written Review. Model answer: put vinegar in a plastic bottle and baking soda in a small bag or cap so they do not touch. Seal the bottle and record its total mass. Tip it to mix. When the fizzing stops, record the mass again without opening it. The masses should be the same, because the carbon dioxide stays inside and atoms are only rearranged. In an open container the mass drops because the gas escapes. Use small amounts, because gas pressure builds up, and wear goggles. Key ideas checklist: closed container with reactants apart; mass before and after; equal masses expected; gas escaping explained; a safety point. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 1.
