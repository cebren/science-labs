Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Mirrors and Lenses Lab

Welcome students. Today’s unit question: how do mirrors and lenses steer light, and how do engineers use that to build cameras, glasses, safety mirrors and medical tools? Part 1 · W3.K2 · W3.K3 · W3.K5

Rays and reflection

How we draw light, how mirrors bounce it, and where a flat mirror puts your image.

Section opener. Part 1 covers the ray model (W3.K2), mirrors reflecting light (W3.K3) and the plane mirror image (W3.K5). Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 to 3.

W3.K2 · The ray model

Light travels in straight lines, so we draw rays

A ray is a straight line with an arrow. It shows the path and direction light travels through one even material, like air or glass.

A ray is a model. Real light spreads as a wave, but a few rays are enough to predict where light will go.

See it: a sunbeam through dusty air, or a laser beam in fog.

Science 9 · W3 · Rays and reflection

Key idea: rays are a model, not a picture of light itself. Light travels in straight lines through ONE even material. Point out that it changes direction at a mirror (reflection) or when it enters a new material (refraction), which is the rest of this unit. Accuracy note (W3.K2): the statement says “light travels in a straight line”; the lab teaches this as straight travel through one even material and shows the path changing at mirrors and boundaries. Discussion prompt: why does a laser dot stay small across a room while a flashlight spot spreads? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 (rays from a bulb, flashlight, laser and the distant Sun).

W3.K3 · Mirrors reflect

The law of reflection

θi

θr

normal

plane mirror

The normal is a line at 90° to the mirror where the ray hits.

Measure both angles from the normal, never from the mirror.

angle of incidence = angle of reflection

Science 9 · W3 · Rays and reflection

Model the vocabulary: incident ray, reflected ray, normal, angle of incidence (θi), angle of reflection (θr). Common error: measuring from the mirror surface. Accuracy note (W3.K3): “mirrors reflect light, whereas lenses refract light” is taught as each device’s main job. Strictly, lenses also reflect a few percent of light at each surface (why glasses have anti-glare coatings), and most household mirrors have a glass front that refracts light before the metal layer reflects it. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (angle slider). Practice: Lab Bench, Investigation 2.

W3.K3 · Smooth and rough surfaces

Why paper shows no image

Regular reflection

Smooth mirror: parallel rays stay together, so an image forms.

Diffuse reflection

Rough paper: each tiny bump faces a new way, so rays scatter.

Science 9 · W3 · Rays and reflection

Both surfaces obey the law of reflection at every point. The difference is the surface: a smooth mirror keeps parallel rays together, so you see an image; the bumps on paper face many directions, so light scatters. Diffuse reflection is why we can see paper from any angle. Discussion prompt: why does a calm lake show a reflection of trees, but a windy lake does not? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (switch between Smooth mirror and Rough paper).

W3.K5 · Images in a flat mirror

A plane mirror image is always the same

Virtual

Cannot be caught on a screen

Upright

Never upside down

Same size

As tall as the object

Same distance

As far behind as the object is in front

Science 9 · W3 · Rays and reflection

Green arrow: the object. Dashed teal arrow: the virtual image. Reflected rays reach the eye and the brain traces them back in straight lines to a point behind the mirror, where no light actually goes, so the image is virtual. The image is also reversed front to back, which is why writing looks backwards. Ask: if you step back 1 m from a mirror, how much farther away does your image get? (Answer: 1 m farther behind the mirror, so 2 m farther from you.) This slide sets up a key precision point for W3.K5: in a flat mirror, moving the object changes the image position but never its size or orientation. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (object distance slider).

Part 2 · W3.K3 · W3.K4

Mirrors and lenses

Curved mirrors reflect, lenses refract, and the shape decides where the light goes.

Section opener. Part 2 covers curved mirrors (W3.K4), refraction (W3.K3) and lens shapes (W3.K4). Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4 to 6.

W3.K4 · Curved mirrors

The shape of a mirror changes where light goes

Concave: converging

Curves inward. Rays meet at the focal point F. Headlights, flashlights, telescopes.

Convex: diverging

Bulges outward. Rays spread for a wide view. Car side mirrors, store safety mirrors.

Science 9 · W3 · Mirrors and lenses

Black dots mark the focal point F. Focal length (f) is the distance from the mirror to F; a more strongly curved mirror has a shorter focal length. Concave mirrors converge light (rays really meet in front). Convex mirrors diverge light (dashed lines trace rays back to a virtual F behind the mirror). Connect to the spoon hook: the inside is concave, the back is convex. Reverse a headlight: a bulb placed at F of a concave mirror sends out a parallel beam. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (switch Plane, Concave, Convex; change the curve).

W3.K3 · Lenses refract

Light bends when it changes material

air

water

Refraction is light bending as it passes at an angle into a new material, because its speed changes.

Slows down: bends toward the normal. Speeds up: bends away. Straight on: no bend.

Context only

n₁ × sin θ₁ = n₂ × sin θ₂

air 1.00 · water 1.33 · glass about 1.5

Science 9 · W3 · Mirrors and lenses

The faint dashed line shows where the ray would have gone without bending. Everyday examples: a pencil looks broken in a glass of water; a fish in Pigeon Lake looks closer to the surface than it is. Snell’s law and refractive indices are context only; students are not asked to memorize them. Light travels about 300 000 km/s in air and about 226 000 km/s in water. Demo idea: a coin in an opaque mug, viewed from just below the rim, appears when water is poured in. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (choose water, glass or diamond; change the angle).

W3.K4 · Lens shapes

Convex and concave lenses

Convex lens: converging

Thicker in the middle. Magnifying glass, camera, your eye.

Concave lens: diverging

Thinner in the middle. Glasses for nearsighted people.

Science 9 · W3 · Mirrors and lenses

A lens refracts light twice: once entering the glass and once leaving it. Diagrams draw the bend at the centre line of a thin lens to keep things simple. A thicker, more curved lens bends light more, so its focal point is closer. Safety: a magnifying glass in sunlight focuses light to a hot spot at F; never aim it near skin, paper or dry grass. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (switch lens shape; change thickness).

Watch the names

A concave mirror converges. A concave lens diverges.

Convex mirrors diverge, but convex lenses converge. Learn mirrors and lenses as opposites.

Science 9 · W3 · Mirrors and lenses

Statement slide. This is the most common mix-up in this unit. Memory hook: the converging pair is concave mirror and convex lens; both bring light to a real focus you could burn paper with (do not try). Quick oral check: “Which converges: a convex mirror or a concave mirror?” (Concave.) “Which converges: a convex lens or a concave lens?” (Convex.) Lab link: Sort It tab, “Converging or diverging?” deck.

Science 9 · W3 · Image and distance

Accuracy note (W3.K5): the statement says the image “can change in size or orientation, depending on the object’s location.” That is true for concave mirrors and convex lenses. For a plane mirror, size and orientation never change. For convex mirrors and concave lenses, the image is always virtual, upright and smaller; only its size and position change. The lab teaches this distinction. Back to the spoon hook: the back of the spoon is convex, so your face never flips. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 1 (record all five shapes at 5, 15, 20 and 30 cm and look for this pattern).

W3.S4 · Lab Bench · Predict the image

Use the lens equation as a tool

The tools

1/f = 1/do + 1/di

m = −di ÷ do

Positive di: real image. Negative di: virtual image. Negative m: upside down.

Worked example

A convex lens has f = 10 cm. The object is 15 cm away.

1/di = 1/10 − 1/15 = 3/30 − 2/30 = 1/30
di = 30 cm, so the image is real
m = −30 ÷ 15 = −2

Image: real, upside down, twice as tall.

Science 9 · W3 · Image and distance

Students do not memorize these equations; they use them as tools. Accuracy note (W3.S4): the equations use the real-is-positive sign convention for an ideal thin lens or a small curved mirror; ray diagrams in the lab are drawn to this ideal, and real lenses blur slightly at the edges. The same equation works for a concave mirror. Check the answer against the zones slide: 15 cm is between f and 2f, so a real, inverted, larger image is expected. Then students try randomized practice. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 3 (new problem, check, show solution). Extension: Investigation 1 records results for all five shapes in a data table.

Part 4 · W3.K1 · W3.S1

Optical technology

Cameras, endoscopes and road signs all put reflection and refraction to work.

Section opener. Part 4 covers technologies that use the behaviour of light (W3.K1) and the skill of investigating one technology (W3.S1). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 8 and Lab Bench, Investigation 4.

W3.K1 · Three families

Technologies that steer light

Optics and imaging

Cameras, telescopes, microscopes, glasses, projectors

Camera: a convex lens focuses a real, upside-down image on the sensor.

Medical applications

Endoscopes, laser eye surgery, pulse oximeters

Endoscope: glass fibres carry light into the body and an image back out.

Security and sensors

Barcode scanners, safety mirrors, road signs, LiDAR

Road sign: tiny beads send headlight beams back to the driver.

Science 9 · W3 · Optical technology

These three families come straight from W3.K1. The lab’s explorer has 14 technologies with how each one uses reflection or refraction. Endoscopes and fibre optics guide light by repeated reflection inside a glass fibre (total internal reflection); name this as context only. Worldwide and local context from the lab: Inuit snow goggles, carved with narrow slits, cut glare from sun on snow and ice; the TELUS World of Science observatory in Edmonton and the dark skies of Jasper. Review flag: the Inuit snow goggles example is described only generally; please review wording and consider a community partner before teaching it in depth. Activity: each pair picks one technology and sorts it into a family with a reason. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 8; Sort It tab, “Which technology family?” deck.

W3.S1 · Lab Bench · Investigate a technology

Build a periscope

Two plane mirrors at 45° each turn the light through 90°. You see over the wall, upright.

Your technology report

  1. What problem does it solve, and who uses it?
  2. Reflection, refraction or both?
  3. What shape are the mirrors or lenses?
  4. Is the image upright? Real or virtual?
  5. How could you improve it?

Science 9 · W3 · Optical technology

In the lab, students start with the mirrors at 30° and 60° and must find the working angles. Expected answers: periscopes let submarine crews, people at parades and engineers inspecting machines see over or around obstacles; they use reflection; two plane mirrors at 45°, parallel to each other; the image is upright and virtual. Improvements: a longer tube to see higher, bigger mirrors for a brighter view, or prisms instead of mirrors. The same five questions work for any technology students research for W3.S1. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 4.

Quick check

Three questions

1

A ray hits a mirror at 20° to the mirror surface. What is the angle of reflection?

2

Which pair both converge light: a concave mirror and a convex lens, or a convex mirror and a concave lens?

3

An object is closer to a magnifying glass than its focal point. Describe the image.

Science 9 · W3 · Optical technology

Answers. 1: 70°. The angle of incidence is 90° − 20° = 70° from the normal, and the angle of reflection equals it. 2: a concave mirror and a convex lens both converge light. 3: virtual, upright and larger, on the same side as the object. Use mini whiteboards or hands up. If many students miss question 1, revisit the law of reflection slide. Lab link: Quiz tab (10 questions drawn from a bank of 24).

Extended response · Decide and defend

A parkade exit meets a busy sidewalk with no clear view. Which mirror should the town install?

Plane

Upright and same size, but how much can drivers see?

Concave

Converges light. What happens to far objects?

Convex

Diverges light. What do people look like in it?

Use ray ideas and image words: real or virtual, upright or upside down, larger or smaller.

Science 9 · W3 · Optical technology

From the lab’s Written Review, extended response 3. Run as a four-corners debate (plane, concave, convex, undecided), then students write their answer. Model answer: a plane mirror gives an upright, same-size image but shows only a narrow area, so drivers might miss someone. A concave mirror converges light; far objects would look upside down and the view would be narrow, which could confuse drivers. A convex mirror diverges light, so it reflects light from a wide area into the driver’s eyes; its image is always virtual, upright and smaller, so people look farther away than they are. Recommend a convex mirror with a sign that objects are closer than they appear. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 3 (key-idea checklist).
