Variation and inheritance

Genetic traits of organisms are determined by DNA.

Genetic variation can lead to evolutionary change in populations.

Genetic inheritance can be used to predict traits from one generation to the next.

Part 1 · LS1 · DNA and traits

Genetic traits of organisms are determined by DNA

Section opener for LS1. Ask: what do you already know about DNA? Note ideas from TV and news, then check them as the section goes on. Lab link: Learn tab, LS1 overview card.

LS1.K1 · LS1.K3

DNA is the instruction manual, shaped like a twisted ladder

Build the organism

Code to make each type of organism

Build each cell

Code for every type of cell in it

Grow and repair

Code for cell growth and repair

Reproduce

Code for cell reproduction

Structure: a double helix. Two long strands twist around each other, joined by paired rungs.

Science 9 · LS1 · DNA

DNA stands for deoxyribonucleic acid. Model it (LS1.S2): students build a DNA model that shows the two twisting strands and the paired rungs. Optional activity from the lab: strawberry DNA extraction. Soap breaks open the cells, salt helps the DNA clump, and cold alcohol makes the long white strands visible. Lab link: Learn tab, LS1, DNA: structure and function.

DNA

Science 9 · LS1 · DNA

Largest to smallest: chromosome, DNA, gene. A chromosome is coiled DNA, and a gene is one segment of that DNA. This is the lab’s quick check: put chromosome, DNA and gene in order. Then follow the arrows from gene to protein to trait (LS1.K5, LS1.S3). Lab link: Learn tab, LS1, DNA, genes, chromosomes and traits.

LS1.K4

23

pairs of chromosomes

Human body cells typically contain 23 pairs, or 46 chromosomes.

One chromosome of each pair comes from each parent.

A karyotype is a picture of a person’s chromosomes lined up in pairs by size. It can show a missing or extra chromosome.

Science 9 · LS1 · DNA

Emphasize “typically”: the statement says human cells typically contain 23 pairs. Some people have a missing or extra chromosome, which a karyotype can show; handle this respectfully, as students may know someone affected. Sex cells carry 23 single chromosomes, which comes back in LS2. Lab link: Learn tab, LS1, DNA, genes, chromosomes and traits.

LS1.K2

A DNA sequence can identify a species or a person

Forensics

DNA profiling compares DNA left at a scene, such as from hair or skin cells, with DNA from known people.

Species ID

Short DNA sequences can check that fish sold in a store is really the species on the label.

The exception

Identical twins come from one fertilized egg, so their DNA is nearly identical.

Science 9 · LS1 · DNA

Quick check from the lab: why can DNA struggle to tell identical twins apart? Their DNA is nearly identical. Note “nearly”: small differences can arise from mutations after the egg splits. Link forward to the ethics slide: who should be allowed to store DNA profiles? Lab link: Learn tab, LS1, DNA as identification.

LS1.K6 · LS1.K7

Alleles vary a trait. Mutations change the code.

Alleles

Different forms of the same gene. One allele of a pea plant’s flower colour gene gives purple flowers; another gives white.

Mutations

A change in the DNA sequence, from copying errors during cell division or environmental factors such as UV light. A mutation can be:

Advantageous: helps survival or reproduction
Harmful: damages how the body works
Neutral: makes no difference

Science 9 · LS1 · Mutations

Mutations can occur naturally during cell division or be caused by environmental factors (LS1.K7). Quick check: a mutation with no effect on survival is neutral. Remind students that many mutations are neutral; “mutation” does not always mean harmful. Lab link: Learn tab, LS1, Alleles and mutations.

LS1.K8 · LS1.S4

Where a mutation happens matters

Where it happensInherited?Possible result
Sex cells (sperm or egg)YesNew genetic traits in offspring
Body cellsNoMay lead to disease in that person, such as skin cancer

Research task

Pick a mutation, such as sickle cell or lactose tolerance. Find its cause, its effect, whether it is helpful, harmful or neutral, and whether it can be inherited.

Science 9 · LS1 · Mutations

Quick check from the lab: UV light causes a mutation in a skin cell. Can the person’s children inherit it? No: only sex cell mutations reach offspring. The research task covers LS1.S4. Accuracy note: the precise name for “lactose tolerance” is lactase persistence (the lactase enzyme keeps being made into adulthood). Sensitive topic: students or their families may live with a genetic condition or cancer. Let students choose their mutation and keep the focus on the science. Lab link: Learn tab, LS1, Sex cell vs body cell mutations; Short Answer question 1.

Part 2 · LS2 · Variation and evolution

Genetic variation can lead to evolutionary change in populations

Section opener for LS2. Big question: how does DNA get passed on, and how can a whole population change over time? Lab link: Learn tab, LS2 overview card.

LS2.K1 · LS2.K2

Asexual reproduction: one parent, identical offspring

Binary fission

A bacterium copies its DNA and splits into two identical cells.

Budding

A small bud grows off the parent and breaks off (yeast, hydra).

Spores

Tough single cells spread by wind, water or animals (moulds, fungi).

Vegetative reproduction

New plants from runners (strawberries), cuttings or tubers (potatoes).

Science 9 · LS2 · Reproduction

Reproduction is how organisms transmit DNA to produce offspring (LS2.K1). Asexual reproduction is efficient: one parent, no mate needed, offspring are genetic copies made by mitosis. The statement names binary fission and budding (highlighted); spores and vegetative reproduction are extra examples from the lab. Quick check: bacteria usually reproduce by binary fission. Lab link: Learn tab, LS2, Reproduction and asexual reproduction.

Science 9 · LS2 · Reproduction

Each parent contributes one chromosome from each of their chromosome pairs (LS2.K3). Quick check from the lab: why do human sex cells carry 23 chromosomes instead of 46? So that two gametes join to restore 23 pairs in the zygote. Extension in the lab: plant and animal fertilization (flowers, conifers, external and internal fertilization). Lab link: Learn tab, LS2, Sexual reproduction and meiosis; Short Answer question 3.

LS2.K4 · LS2.S1 · LS2.S2

Comparing asexual and sexual reproduction

AsexualSexual
ParentsOneTwo
VariationLittle; mainly from mutationsHigh; meiosis and fertilization mix DNA
AdvantagesFast; no mate neededSome individuals may survive change
DisadvantagesA new disease can wipe out the populationNeeds a mate; uses lots of energy

Some species do both: strawberries, potatoes, yeast and aphids.

Science 9 · LS2 · Reproduction

LS2.K4: sexual reproduction results in greater genetic variation because DNA from two parents is mixed during meiosis and fertilization; asexual reproduction relies mainly on mutations. For LS2.S2, assign one species each and have students report whether it reproduces asexually, sexually or both. Extension in the lab: bacterial conjugation swaps DNA but is not reproduction, because the number of cells does not go up. Lab link: Learn tab, LS2, Comparing sexual and asexual reproduction; Sort It; Short Answer question 5.

LS2.K5 · LS2.K6

Variation comes from genes, environment, or both

Genes

Blood type is set by the alleles you inherit.

Environment

A scar comes from an injury, not from DNA.

Both

Height depends on genes and on nutrition.

Genetic variation is the difference in DNA among individuals in a population or species.

Science 9 · LS2 · Variation

Why variation matters: a population with lots of genetic variation is more likely to have some individuals that survive a disease or a change in climate. Quick check: a person’s height is affected by both genes and environment. Only genetic variation can be passed on and acted on by natural selection. Lab link: Learn tab, LS2, Causes of variation.

LS2.K7 · LS2.K8 · LS2.S3

Natural selection makes adaptations more common

1

More offspring than can survive

2

Individuals vary

3

4

5

An adaptation is a genetic trait that gives a survival and reproductive advantage. Example: when antibiotics are used, a few bacteria with a resistance mutation survive and reproduce, so the next population is mostly resistant.

Science 9 · LS2 · Natural selection

Note for review: the lab describes structural and behavioural adaptations (shorebird beaks, migration). The statement (LS2.K7) defines adaptations as genetic traits, so stress that only inherited behaviours count; a learned behaviour is not an adaptation in this sense. In antibiotic resistance, the drug is the selection pressure; nobody chose the bacteria. Lab link: Learn tab, LS2, Adaptations and natural selection; Short Answer question 6.

LS2.K9 · Selection Sim

No moth changed colour. The population did.

Grey and white moths rest on dark bark. Birds spot white moths more easily, so grey moths survive and reproduce more. Over generations, grey becomes common. Change the bark to pale birch and the trend reverses.

Evolution is change in a population over generations. It does not happen within one individual’s lifetime.

Science 9 · LS2 · Natural selection

Main Lab Bench investigation for LS2: the Selection Sim tab. Students first play the bird (eat 12 moths), then let the birds hunt, run 5 generations, then switch the habitat from sooty bark to pale birch. Ask them to sketch the graph of percent grey moths by generation and predict what happens after the switch. Quick check: what changes over generations? The share of each colour in the population, not the colour of any one moth. Extension in the lab: speciation. Simulation results are random each run.

LS2.K10 · LS2.S4

Darwin’s ideas, and how theories change

EARLY 1800s

Lamarck

Changes during life are passed on. A giraffe stretches its neck, so its calf has a longer neck.

1859

Darwin

On the Origin of Species. Based on variation he observed, including finches and tortoises on the Galápagos Islands. Helpful inherited traits survive and spread.

LATER

Genes and DNA

Explained how traits are passed on, supporting Darwin’s theory.

Lamarck’s idea was replaced because traits gained in a lifetime, like a scar, are not inherited.

Science 9 · LS2 · Darwin

LS2.K10: theories of natural selection and evolution are based on Darwin’s observations and ideas. Lamarck is extra context from the lab to show how theories are revised or replaced when new evidence appears; the statement does not require it. Discussion (LS2.S4): what made Darwin’s explanation stronger than Lamarck’s? Lab link: Learn tab, LS2, Darwin and how theories change; Short Answer question 7.

LS2.K11 · LS2.S5

Artificial selection: people choose which organisms breed

The canola story

Canola was bred in Canada from rapeseed in the 1970s to make a healthy edible oil. It is now one of Alberta’s biggest crops.

Other examples

Wheat that handles cold or ripens before the first frost. Cattle bred for meat or milk.

Science 9 · LS2 · Artificial selection

Contrast with natural selection: in artificial selection, people choose the parents; in natural selection, the environment does. Quick check: farmers breeding canola for better oil is artificial selection. Other methods in the lab: cross-breeding, outcrossing between zoos, grafting. Research task (LS2.S5): students pick an Alberta crop or livestock breed and explain which traits were selected. Lab link: Learn tab, LS2, Artificial selection in Alberta; Short Answer question 8.

Part 3 · LS3 · Predicting inheritance

Genetic inheritance can be used to predict traits

Section opener for LS3. Return to the hook: why do two puppies from the same litter look different? Students will be able to answer with alleles and Punnett squares by the end of this section. Lab link: Learn tab, LS3 overview card.

LS3.K1 · LS3.K5 · LS3.S2

Genotype is the alleles. Phenotype is what you see.

Genotype

The set of alleles an organism has.

BB · Bb · bb

Phenotype

The observable trait.

Blue skin · purple skin

Offspring can share a parent’s trait, look different from both parents, or show a blend.

Science 9 · LS3 · Inheritance

The lab uses a made-up creature with one skin colour gene: B (blue) is dominant over b (purple). Quick check: which is a phenotype? Purple skin; Bb and bb are genotypes. LS3.K1 includes blending: offspring can be the same as, different from, or a blend of their parents. Lab link: Learn tab, LS3, Inheritance: genotype and phenotype; Short Answer question 9.

LS3.K2 · LS3.K3 · LS3.S4

Single gene inheritance: dominant and recessive

GenotypeNamePhenotype
BBHomozygous dominantBlue: dominant trait
BbHeterozygousBlue: one dominant allele is enough
bbHomozygous recessivePurple: recessive trait needs two copies

Each parent passes on one allele. The pair decides which trait is expressed.

Science 9 · LS3 · Inheritance

LS3.K3: a dominant trait shows when at least one copy of the dominant allele is present; a recessive trait shows only with two copies of the recessive allele. Practise LS3.S4 aloud: cover the phenotype column and have students call out the trait for each genotype. Quick check: Bb shows the dominant trait. Common misconception: dominant does not mean more common. Lab link: Learn tab, LS3, Dominant and recessive alleles.

Science 9 · LS3 · Punnett squares

Main Lab Bench investigation for LS3: the Punnett Lab tab. B (blue skin) is dominant over b (purple skin) in a made-up creature. Blue boxes show blue offspring; the tan box is purple. Walk through filling each box, then have students try BB × bb in the lab (100% Bb, all blue) and predict before pressing New cross. Remind students that ratios are chances: a litter of four will not always be exactly 3 : 1. Quick check: in Bb × Bb, 25% of offspring are expected to be purple.

LS3.K6 · LS3.K7 · LS3.S1 · LS3.S5

Most traits are complex, and variation comes in two kinds

Complex traits

Eye colour, hair colour and height come from many genes working together, often with the environment. One Punnett square cannot predict them.

Discrete variation

Distinct versions with nothing in between, such as blood type: A, B, AB or O. Graph with a bar graph.

Continuous variation

A spectrum of values: height, hand span, mass. Graph with a histogram.

Science 9 · LS3 · Variation

Accuracy note: the lab’s launch lists tongue rolling and widow’s peak as discrete traits. These are common classroom examples, but research shows they are not simple single gene traits; blood type and earlobes are safer examples for single trait categories, and attached and detached earlobes also vary along a range. Consider using blood type as the main discrete example (as the statement does). For LS3.S1, a class trait survey works well; avoid asking students to share blood type or other personal health data. Quick check: hand span shows continuous variation. Lab link: Learn tab, Unit launch and LS3, Complex multi-gene traits; Sort It.

LS3.K8 · LS3.S6

Genetics raises questions about what we should do

DNA modification

Is it safe and right to edit the DNA of wild or farm organisms, or people?

Ownership

Who owns gene sequences, seed strains and genetic data?

Consent and sharing

Who may store or share your DNA profile: ancestry companies, police databases, insurers?

Science 9 · LS3 · Ethics

Ethical questions ask what we should do, not just what is true. Quick check: “Should a company be allowed to own a gene sequence?” is an ethical question. The lab also compares bacterial overproduction of insulin (few ethical concerns) with transgenic animals (more concerns about animal welfare) and mentions CRISPR as a gene editing tool. Sensitive topic: some families have strong views on genetic testing and modification, and some communities have concerns about how their genetic data has been collected and used. Keep discussion respectful and focused on reasons. Lab link: Learn tab, LS3, Ethics in genetics; Short Answer question 10.

Quick check

Three questions

1. Put these in order from largest to smallest: gene, chromosome, DNA.

2. Why does evolution happen to populations and not to individuals?

3. Two Bb parents have offspring. What percent are expected to show the recessive trait?

Science 9 · LS1–LS3 · Check

Answers. 1: chromosome, DNA, gene. A chromosome is coiled DNA, and a gene is one segment of that DNA. 2: an individual keeps the DNA it was born with; natural selection changes how common each trait is across generations, so the population changes. 3: 25%, the one bb box out of four. Use mini whiteboards. Lab link: Quiz tab, pick a chunk (LS1, LS2 or LS3) or the whole unit.

Debate · LS3.S6

“Should a company be allowed to own a gene sequence?”

Take a side. Give one reason, one consequence for people, and one point the other side would make.

Science 9 · LS3 · Debate

This ethics question comes from the lab’s Ethics in genetics topic. Structure: two minutes of silent writing, then a line debate. Points for ownership: companies invest in research and need to recover costs; ownership can fund new medicines and seed strains. Points against: genes occur naturally; ownership can raise prices of tests and treatments, and limit other researchers. Follow up with Short Answer question 10 in the lab (bacterial overproduction versus transgenic animals) for a written response with a model answer and checklist.
