Rockets drop what they no longer need

On the pad

500 t

Two stages and a satellite. 450 t of this is fuel.

After stage 1 drops

90 t

The empty tank falls away, so a little fuel goes much further.

In orbit

10 t

Only the satellite remains, moving at about 28 000 km/h.

Hot gas pushes down, so the rocket goes up. Computers steer it many times each second and decide when each stage drops.

SP1.K1. The masses are made up and rounded for practice, the same as in the lab’s model. Newton’s third law: the rocket pushes gas out, the gas pushes the rocket forward. Ask: why not build one giant stage? (It would carry an empty heavy tank all the way to orbit.) Mention that some stages now land and fly again, which lowers the cost of a launch. Telescopes, rovers and stations

Space telescopes

Orbit above the air, which blurs starlight and blocks some light. Webb (2021) sees infrared light from early galaxies.

Rovers

Drive on Mars and test rocks for signs of past water and life. Signals take minutes, so rovers steer themselves around rocks.

Space stations

The ISS orbits about 400 km up, circles Earth about every 90 minutes and has had crew since 2000.

Mars is 55 to 400 million km away. One message takes about 3 to 22 minutes.

Science 9 · SP1 · Tools of exploration

SP1.K1. Ask: why can’t someone on Earth drive a Mars rover with a joystick? (By the time the picture arrives, the rover has already hit the rock.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3. Have students drag the Mars distance slider from closest to farthest and read the one-way and round-trip times.

Lab Bench · Signal delay calculator

How long does a message take?

t = d ÷ c    c ≈ 300 000 km/s

Worked example: Mars is 150 million km away.

t = 150 000 000 ÷ 300 000

t = 500 s

500 ÷ 60 ≈ 8.3 min

Your turn: the Moon is about 384 000 km away. How many seconds?

Science 9 · SP1 · Tools of exploration

SP1.S1. Students use the formula but do not need to memorize it. Answer to Your turn: 384 000 ÷ 300 000 = 1.28 s, about 1.3 seconds, so a Moon rover could be driven from Earth with only a short delay. Common error: forgetting to change millions of km into km, or seconds into minutes. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 2 (randomized practice with steps). Investigation 1 (mission planner) is a good follow-up.

Canada’s two specialties

Robotics

Arms that build and fix

Canadarm (1981) on the Space Shuttle. Canadarm2 (2001) helped build the ISS and catches cargo ships. Dextre (2008) does delicate repairs. Canadarm3 is being built for Gateway, a planned station orbiting the Moon.

Satellite technology

Eyes and voices in orbit

Alouette 1 (1962) made Canada one of the first countries to build its own satellite. Anik A1 (1972) brought TV and phone signals to the North. RADARSAT uses radar to see through cloud and darkness.

Part 2

Implications of space exploration

An implication is a result or effect that follows from a choice. Space exploration has benefits and drawbacks.

This section covers SP1.K3 and the skill SP1.S2. Five implications: data that benefits life on Earth, jobs, long-term costs, space debris, and effects on the human body. Ask students to sort each as they appear: benefit, drawback, or both. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 5 to 7.

What we gain: data and jobs

Wildfires

Satellites spot hot spots and smoke over Alberta forests so crews can plan.

Farming

Images show crop health and drought; satellite navigation guides tractors.

Medicine

Space robotics ideas helped create neuroArm at the University of Calgary.

Jobs

Engineers, technicians, coders, data analysts, doctors and lawyers. Many work on the ground.

Radar satellites also map floods and sea ice, and weather satellites feed every Edmonton forecast.

Science 9 · SP1 · Implications

SP1.K3: data that could benefit life on Earth, and employment in the space industry. Accuracy flag: the neuroArm link (University of Calgary with the company that built Canadarm) should be verified before sharing. Career connection: ask students which job here uses skills they already have. No job statistics are given on purpose. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (Alberta, seen from orbit).

SP1.K3: long-term investment and maintenance costs, and space debris. Space debris is human-made junk in orbit: dead satellites, used rocket stages and fragments. Accuracy flag: debris figures are from the ESA Space Environment Report 2025; the ISS cost and retirement date are widely reported estimates. Please check currency before sharing. Kessler syndrome

Every collision makes more debris, and more debris makes more collisions.

Sustainable use of space means bringing satellites down at the end of their life and avoiding events that create debris, so orbits stay usable for future generations.

Run the Lab Bench debris simulator live. Investigation 3: first run the default settings, then raise launches to 5, then drop end-of-life removal to 40%, then tick the weapons test. Ask students to predict each graph before you press Run. Remind them the simulator is a made-up model that shows trends, not a forecast. Question: who should pay for cleanup missions? This connects to the gaps in space law later.

Part 3

Rules for space

A treaty is a written agreement between countries. International space law is the set of treaties that guide what countries do in space.

This section covers SP1.K4, SP1.K5 and the skill SP1.S3. Remind students of their prediction about owning the Moon. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 8 and 9, then Lab Bench Investigation 6 (space law court).

The Outer Space Treaty, 1967

No claiming

No country can claim the Moon, a planet or any part of space as its territory.

Peaceful

No weapons of mass destruction in orbit; no military bases on other worlds.

For everyone

Exploration should benefit all countries, rich or poor.

Clean

Avoid harmful contamination of other worlds, and of Earth.

Canada and more than 110 countries have joined. Later treaties cover rescuing astronauts, paying for damage and registering launches.

Science 9 · SP1 · Space law

SP1.K4. Reveal the hook answer: landing first does not make the Moon yours (Article II). The Apollo flag marked a visit, not ownership. Accuracy flag: the draft wording “prohibit space appropriation” is taught precisely as no national claim of sovereignty; whether companies can own resources they extract is unsettled (next slides). “Reduce contamination” is taught as avoiding harmful contamination of other worlds and of Earth (Article IX). Countries are also responsible for their companies’ space activities. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 8.

“Peaceful” has a precise meaning

Banned by the treaty

Nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass destruction in orbit. Military bases, forts and weapons tests on the Moon and other bodies.

Not banned by the treaty

Military satellites for communication and navigation. Ordinary weapons in orbit. Tests that blow up a satellite are not clearly banned.

Canada and other countries promised in 2022 not to do destructive anti-satellite tests. That promise is voluntary.

Science 9 · SP1 · Space law

SP1.K4 accuracy flag: the draft says treaties “promote peaceful exploration.” The lab teaches what the Outer Space Treaty actually says (Article IV), so students do not think all military activity in space is banned. Discussion: should ordinary weapons in orbit be banned too? Why might countries disagree? Lab link: Lab Bench Investigation 6, cases about a nuclear weapon, a Moon base and a missile test.

Safe · Sustainable · Equitable

New rules are needed so space stays fair for countries that cannot yet afford rockets.

Some countries have their own national laws. Others signed the Artemis Accords (2020), voluntary principles for Moon exploration that Canada signed. Voluntary agreements are not binding treaties.

SP1.K5 and the understanding. Equitable means fair: countries without space programs still share the benefits and the sky. Accuracy flag: the Artemis Accords are taught as a voluntary set of principles, not a treaty, and not every country agrees with them. Ask: is it fair if the first company to reach Moon ice gets to keep it? Who would decide? Lab link: Lab Bench Investigation 4, Moon ice mining mission.

Quick check

Three questions

1. Why must a Mars rover be able to steer itself?

2. Name two implications of space exploration: one benefit and one drawback.

3. A company mines ice on the Moon and sells it. Is this clearly regulated by international space law?

Science 9 · SP1 · Review

Answers. 1: Signals take about 3 to 22 minutes to reach Mars, so a driver on Earth cannot react in time; the rover’s computer chooses a safe path. 2: Benefits include satellite data for wildfires, farming or weather, and jobs; drawbacks include long-term costs, space debris and effects on the human body. 3: No. International law does not clearly say whether a company can own resources it extracts; some national laws allow it and other countries disagree. Science 9 · SP1 · Review

From the lab’s Written Review, extended response 3. Model answer outline: ownership of Moon resources. Rule: companies mine only with a licence from an international body, leave protected science and heritage zones untouched, and pay part of their profits into a fund that helps countries without space programs use space data. Safety: licensing prevents crowding and conflict at one site. Sustainability: protected zones and clean-up rules keep the Moon usable. Equity: the fund shares benefits.
