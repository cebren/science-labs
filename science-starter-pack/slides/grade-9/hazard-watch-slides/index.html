Section 1 · ES2.K1 · ES2.S1

Hazards around the Pacific

Why the edge of the Pacific Ocean, including Canada’s west coast, is one of the most hazardous places on Earth.

This section covers Learn Lessons 1 and 2 and the risk analyzer (Lab Bench, Investigation 1). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

ES2.K1 · Lesson 1

A geological hazard is a natural event caused by processes within Earth that can harm people or property

Earthquakes

Stuck plates suddenly slip and the ground shakes.

Tsunamis

Huge ocean waves made when the sea floor suddenly moves the water above it.

Volcanic activity

Melted rock called magma rises and erupts as lava, ash and gases.

Landslides

Rock and soil move quickly down a steep slope, often after shaking or heavy rain.

Science 9 · ES2 · Hazards around the Pacific

These are the four hazards named in ES2.K1. Ask: which of these could happen in Edmonton? (Small landslides on river valley slopes, and small earthquakes, but our volcano and tsunami hazard is essentially zero.) Keep the definitions short; students meet the causes in more detail on the next two slides. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1; Flashcards tab for the terms.

ES2.K1 · Lesson 1

The Ring of Fire

A horseshoe-shaped belt around the edge of the Pacific Ocean, where several plates meet.

Along most of the ring, an ocean plate slides under another plate. This is subduction. Stuck plates slip in earthquakes. Rock melts deep below and feeds volcanoes.

≈ 90%

of the world’s earthquakes

≈ 3/4

of active volcanoes

Science 9 · ES2 · Hazards around the Pacific

Big idea: hazards cluster where plates meet. The two figures are commonly quoted approximate estimates; teach them as “about.” Accuracy flag for review (ES2.K1): the figures of about 90 percent of earthquakes and about three quarters of active volcanoes should get a currency check. Have students open the Tour the ring map and choose three places, for example Japan (2011 magnitude 9.0 earthquake and tsunami), Chile (1960 magnitude 9.5, the largest ever recorded) and Alaska (1964 magnitude 9.2). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

ES2.K1 · Lesson 2

Canada’s west coast: the Cascadia Subduction Zone

Juan de Fuca Plate

North American Plate

Pacific Ocean

Earthquakes: the plates stick near the coast (star), then suddenly slip.

Tsunamis: the sea floor jumps and lifts the water above it.

Volcanoes: water from the sinking plate helps rock melt; magma rises to volcanoes like Mount Garibaldi.

Landslides: steep mountains slide after shaking or heavy rain.

Science 9 · ES2 · Hazards around the Pacific

Farther north, near Haida Gwaii, the Pacific Plate slides sideways past North America. Canada’s largest recorded earthquake, about magnitude 8.1, happened there in 1949; a magnitude 7.8 earthquake in 2012 made a small tsunami. Great Cascadia earthquakes happen roughly every 500 years on average, but the gaps vary a lot. Mount Meager, north of Whistler, had a huge landslide in 2010. Accuracy flag for review (ES2.K1): Haida Gwaii magnitudes, the 500 year average and the 2010 Mount Meager landslide are general facts worth a quick check. Ask: why is Edmonton’s earthquake and volcano hazard low? (Far from a plate boundary.) Sensitive note: students with family on the coast may feel worried; emphasize preparedness. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2, Cascadia cross-section.

Science 9 · ES2 · Hazards around the Pacific

Hazard: how likely and strong the event is there. Exposure: how many people, homes and roads are in the way. Vulnerability: how easily they are harmed; good buildings, warnings and practice lower it. Worked example from the lab: Metro Vancouver, 3 × 3 × 2 = 18. Edmonton has many people but a low hazard, so 1 × 3 × 2 = 6. Haida Gwaii has a high hazard but few people, so also 6. Discussion: does equal risk score mean equal danger for the people who live there? Have students use the risk analyzer, move the preparedness slider and try the randomized practice questions. The ratings are made up for practice, so label them that way. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 1.

Section 2 · ES2.K2

Reading the past

How historical accounts support scientific understanding of the Cascadia earthquake, the Tseax Cone eruption and the Frank Slide.

This section covers Learn Lessons 3 and 4. Before starting, remind students that oral histories belong to the Nations that keep them. We describe them only in general terms. Accuracy flag for review (ES2.K2): First Nations oral histories are described generally; please review the wording and consider inviting a community voice. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3.

ES2.K2 · Lesson 3

Big events can be centuries apart, so scientists combine kinds of evidence

Oral histories

Passed down by First Nations, who have lived on this land for thousands of years.

Written records

Diaries, newspapers, government reports and photographs. Eyewitness accounts from survivors.

Physical evidence

Clues in rocks, soil, lava and tree rings.

When the stories and the rocks agree, scientists are much more confident about what happened, when, and how big it was.

Science 9 · ES2 · Reading the past

Historical accounts help scientists estimate how often an event happens, which helps communities plan for the next one. Ask: why might one kind of evidence on its own be uncertain? (Stories can lack exact dates; rocks can lack the human details; records only go back so far.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3, Three events, many clues. The tags in the model show which evidence supports each event.

ES2.K2 · Lesson 3

The Cascadia earthquake: January 26, 1700, about magnitude 9

Oral history

First Nations along the coast describe strong shaking and flooding on a winter night.

Written records

Records in Japan describe a tsunami that arrived with no earthquake felt there.

Physical evidence

“Ghost forests” killed when the coast dropped, tsunami sand layers, and tree rings from the winter of 1699 to 1700.

Together, these clues give the date, the time and the size. No single source could.

Science 9 · ES2 · Reading the past

Return to the hook: the “orphan tsunami” in Japan came from this earthquake. Using the Japanese records, scientists traced the wave back across the Pacific, dated the earthquake to the night of January 26, 1700, and estimated it was about magnitude 9. Ghost forests are stands of dead trees killed when the land suddenly sank and salt water flooded their roots. Accuracy flag for review (ES2.K2): the date and magnitude are the widely accepted values; the oral histories are described only generally, and the lab does not name or retell any specific community’s account. If you can, invite a First Nations speaker or use a resource prepared by a Nation. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3, select 1700.

ES2.K2 · Lesson 3

The Tseax Cone eruption: about 300 years ago

A small volcano erupted in the Nass Valley of northwestern British Columbia, in Nisga’a territory.

Lava flowed down the valley and buried villages. Nisga’a oral history remembers the eruption and the many people who died, likely from volcanic gases.

The lava beds are now a protected park managed together with the Nisga’a Nation.

Oral history

What happened and its human cost, kept by the Nisga’a Nation.

Physical evidence

Dating the lava and the trees it buried. Estimates of the date have changed over time.

Science 9 · ES2 · Reading the past

Tseax Cone is one of the deadliest geological disasters known in Canada. There were no written records in the region at the time, so Nisga’a oral history is the main human account, and dating the lava and buried trees gives the timing. Ask: why might scientists’ date estimates change over time? (New dating methods and new samples.) Accuracy flag for review (ES2.K2): the date is taught as about 300 years ago because estimates vary; the number of deaths is often given as about 2000 from Nisga’a oral history and is intentionally not stated on the slide; please review wording with a community source if possible. Sensitive topic: loss of life; treat it with respect. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3, select ~1700.

ES2.K2 · ES2.K3 · Lesson 4

The Frank Slide: April 29, 1903, Crowsnest Pass, Alberta

Millions of tonnes of limestone from Turtle Mountain fell on the town of Frank in less than two minutes. Several causes worked together:

1 · Cracked rock near the top, broken when the Rockies formed

2 · Weak rock such as shale and coal under strong limestone

3 · Water and ice freezing and thawing in the cracks

4 · A coal mine at the base (how much it mattered is still discussed)

Today, sensors watch tiny movements of the cracks so people below can be warned.

Science 9 · ES2 · Reading the past

Somewhere between about 70 and 90 people died; the exact number is not known because records from the time are incomplete. Newspapers, government reports, photographs and survivors’ accounts are the historical accounts; the boulder field and the scar on the mountain, still visible from Highway 3, are the physical evidence. The Alberta Geological Survey monitors Turtle Mountain today, which links to ES2.K3. Accuracy flag for review (ES2.K2): the date is firm; the death toll range and the role of the mine are taught as uncertain. Sensitive topic: loss of life in a local disaster. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4, find all four factors on the diagram.

Section 3 · ES2.K3 · ES2.S2

Watching and protecting

How scientists monitor geological hazards, and how communities minimize their impacts.

This section covers Learn Lessons 5 and 6 and the early warning timer (Lab Bench, Investigation 2). Key distinction to repeat often: monitoring means measuring a hazard again and again to spot changes; minimizing means reducing the harm. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5.

ES2.K3 · Lesson 5

Monitoring: measuring again and again to spot changes early

Seismograph

Records ground shaking. A network finds where an earthquake started and how big it was. A swarm of small quakes can mean magma is moving.

Gas emission monitoring

Measures sulfur dioxide and carbon dioxide from a volcano. A sudden rise can mean magma is rising.

GPS and tiltmeters

Measure tiny changes in the shape of the ground, such as a volcano swelling.

Crack meters

Measure how fast cracks open on an unstable slope, like Turtle Mountain.

Science 9 · ES2 · Watching and protecting

Monitoring cannot stop a hazard. It gives people information and, sometimes, time. Use the Monitoring station model: choose each instrument and switch between a quiet week and a week with warning signs. The traces are made up for practice. Ask: what pattern counts as a warning sign? (A steady reading that suddenly speeds up.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5.

ES2.K3 · ES2.S2 · Lesson 6

Minimizing: we cannot stop earthquakes, but we can reduce the harm

Early warning systems

Detect the first, faster waves and alert people before strong shaking. Tsunami warnings send people to high ground.

Emergency preparedness

Drop, Cover and Hold On. Keep an emergency kit. Practise drills.

Construction standards

Building codes require cross bracing, strong ties and other designs so buildings bend without collapsing.

Land-use planning

Keeps homes, schools and hospitals away from unstable slopes, tsunami zones and soft ground.

Science 9 · ES2 · Watching and protecting

Canada began an earthquake early warning system in British Columbia in 2024. Accuracy flag for review (ES2.K3): please confirm the current status and reach of Canada’s earthquake early warning system before teaching. Try the Shake table: add cross bracing, base isolators, strong ties and solid rock, then shake. The scores are made up for a simple model, not an engineering test. Discussion: which of these four would cost a family nothing? (Preparedness and drills.) Then play Sort It, Monitor or minimize? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6; Sort It tab.

ES2.K3 · ES2.S2 · Lab Bench, Investigation 2

Early warning timer: how many seconds do you get?

P waves are fast and do little damage. S waves are slower and cause strong shaking. Sensors catch the P waves and send an alert.

arrival time = distance ÷ wave speed
warning = S wave arrival − alert time

Simplified model: S waves 3.5 km/s, alert sent 10 s after the earthquake starts.

Worked example: 140 km away

S arrival = 140 ÷ 3.5 = 40 s
Warning = 40 − 10 = 30 s

Closer than 35 km, the S waves arrive before the alert. That is the blind zone: no warning.

Science 9 · ES2 · Watching and protecting

This is the main Lab Bench investigation for the deck. The lab uses a P wave speed of 6 km/s, an S wave speed of 3.5 km/s and an alert 10 seconds after the earthquake starts; real systems vary. Model the worked example, then have students drag the distance slider and watch the green warning bar grow. Ask: why does living farther away give more warning, yet less shaking? Have students complete three randomized practice questions. Check: 70 km gives 20 − 10 = 10 s; 210 km gives 60 − 10 = 50 s. Discussion: what could you do in 10 seconds? (Drop, Cover and Hold On; a surgeon pauses; trains slow down.) Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 2.

Section 4 · ES2.K4 · ES2.S3

Measuring earthquakes

Two scales answer two different questions: how big was it, and how strong did it feel here?

This section covers Learn Lessons 7 and 8 and Lab Bench Investigations 3 and 4. Accuracy flag for review (ES2.K4): the draft says both scales “measure the intensity.” Only the Modified Mercalli scale measures intensity (observed effects at a place). The Richter scale measures magnitude (size at the source, from seismograph records). The lab and this deck teach the difference, and note that scientists now usually report large earthquakes with the moment magnitude scale. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7.

ES2.K4 · Lesson 7 · Lab Bench, Investigation 3

The Richter scale measures magnitude: the size at the source

Created in 1935 by Charles Richter and Beno Gutenberg in California, from seismograph wave heights. It is logarithmic.

10×

taller waves for each whole step

≈32×

more energy for each whole step

Worked example: M7 vs M5

difference = 2
waves: 10² = 100×
energy: 32² ≈ 1000×

Science 9 · ES2 · Measuring earthquakes

One earthquake gets one magnitude number. Formulas are shown so students can follow the pattern; they do not need to memorize them: amplitude ratio = 10 to the power of the difference; energy ratio is about 32 to the power of the difference. Today scientists usually report large earthquakes with the moment magnitude scale, which works like the Richter scale but is more accurate for very big earthquakes; news reports often still say “Richter.” Quick prompt: a magnitude 6 compared to a magnitude 4? (100 times taller waves, about 1000 times the energy.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7, Magnitude ladder; Lab Bench tab, Investigation 3, randomized practice.

ES2.K4 · ES2.S3 · Lesson 8 · Lab Bench, Investigation 4

The Modified Mercalli scale describes intensity: the effects at one place

LevelWhat people notice (selected levels from I to XII)
IIFelt by only a few people at rest, especially on upper floors.
IVFelt indoors by many. Dishes, windows and doors rattle. Parked cars rock.
VIFelt by all. Heavy furniture moves. Some plaster falls. Slight damage.
VIIIConsiderable damage to ordinary buildings. Chimneys and walls fall.
XMost masonry and wood-frame buildings destroyed. Rails bend.
XIIDamage is total. Objects are thrown into the air.

Began with Italian scientist Giuseppe Mercalli and was later modified by American scientists. Roman numerals I to XII.

Science 9 · ES2 · Measuring earthquakes

Intensity is built from observations and felt reports, so it changes from place to place. It is usually highest near the epicentre and drops farther away, and it also depends on the ground and the buildings. Have students do the Intensity survey: six made-up felt reports from a made-up magnitude 6.2 earthquake. Point out Bay Flats: farther away than Wolf Creek, but on soft, wet ground, so its intensity is higher (VIII compared with VII). The lab accepts ratings within one level. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 8; Lab Bench tab, Investigation 4.

ES2.S3 · Compare

One earthquake.
One magnitude.
Many intensities.

Richter magnitude comes from instruments and describes the earthquake’s size. Mercalli intensity comes from observations and describes its effects where you are.

Pause here and have students say the statement back in their own words. Use the One earthquake, many places map in Lesson 8: a made-up magnitude 6.8 earthquake gives intensity VIII in Seal Cove, 5 km away, but only II in Prairie Junction, 300 km away. The magnitude is 6.8 in both places. Then play Sort It, Magnitude or intensity? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 8; Sort It tab.

Debate · Written Review, extended 2

Should students in Edmonton learn about Ring of Fire hazards?

Use these ideas

Hazard, exposure and vulnerability. Edmonton’s low hazard. Albertans who travel or move to BC.

Include

A clear position, an Alberta example such as the Frank Slide, and one opposing view.

Science 9 · ES2 · Review

Run as a four corners debate (strongly agree to strongly disagree), then have students write their response in the Written Review tab. Model answer from the lab: Yes. Edmonton’s own hazard is low, but Albertans travel and move to BC, and a great Cascadia earthquake would affect the whole country. Alberta also has landslides, as the Frank Slide shows. Learning how hazards are monitored and minimized helps people make safe choices anywhere. A reasonable opposing view is that local hazards like river valley slumps and severe weather deserve more time, but the skills of risk analysis apply to both. Key ideas checklist: clear position; uses hazard or exposure ideas; Alberta example; considers another view. Lab link: Written Review tab, Extended response 2.
