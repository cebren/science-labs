Section 1 · CS2.K1

AI at work

Artificial intelligence (AI) means computer systems that do tasks that usually need human thinking.

Define AI: recognizing pictures, understanding speech, making predictions. Stress that AI is a tool built and used by people. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

CS2.K1 · Efficiency

Three ways AI saves time

Information processing

AI sorts 20 000 trail camera photos and flags the ones with animals.

A biologist checks the flagged photos.

Decision making

AI suggests which streets to plow first after a snowfall.

City staff approve the plan and handle emergencies.

Task completion

AI writes captions for a 30 minute science video.

A person fixes names and science words.

AI usually helps people rather than replacing them. A person checks the work and is responsible for it.

Science 8 · CS2 · AI at work

Efficient means the same result with less time, effort or energy. Ask students to add one more example to each column. Worked example from the lab (made-up numbers): 10 000 photos at 3 s each by hand is 500 min. With AI: 10 min plus checking 10% (1 000 photos, 50 min) is 60 min, so 440 min saved. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1, and Lab Bench, Investigation 1 for randomized time-saved practice.

Section 2 · CS2.K2 · CS2.S1

How machines learn

Machine learning is AI that learns patterns from data instead of following only rules a person wrote.

Contrast with Science 7 coding: there, every step was written by a person. In machine learning, the computer finds the pattern from examples. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 2 and 3.

CS2.K2 · Labels or no labels

Sorting leaves two ways

Supervised learning

Labels in, labels out

The AI gets leaves labelled aspen or willow. It labels a new leaf by finding the labelled leaves most like it.

Unsupervised learning

No labels, find groups

The AI gets the same leaves with no labels. It finds two groups by length and width, but cannot name them.

In unsupervised learning, a person decides what each group means.

Science 8 · CS2 · How machines learn

Aspen leaves are roundish (length about equal to width); willow leaves are long and narrow. The leaf measurements in the lab are made up for practice. Demonstrate the lab model: in Supervised mode, move the sliders so the new leaf sits between groups and discuss why the AI’s answer changes. In Unsupervised mode, press Next step until the groups settle. Ask: what would the AI need to name the groups? (Labels from a person.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2.

CS2.K2 · Reinforcement learning

Learning by trial and error

A delivery robot must reach its charger. Nobody tells it the route.

+10

reward for reaching the charger

−10

penalty for the stairs (try ends)

−1

for every move, so short routes win

After many tries it keeps the moves that earn the most reward.

Green: charger. Red: stairs. Grey: wall. Orange line: the learned route.

Science 8 · CS2 · How machines learn

A penalty is a negative reward. Ask: what is the best possible score? The shortest safe route has 8 moves: 7 moves at minus 1 and the last move onto the charger earns 10, so the score is 3. Run the lab model: press Train 1 try a few times (almost random), then Train 25 tries several times and watch the arrows settle. Ask where else trial and error with rewards is used: game-playing AI, robots learning to walk. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3.

Science 8 · CS2 · AI that makes, talks and suggests

In the lab’s training text, “snow falls” is followed by “in” once and “on” once, so each has a 50% chance. Real generators learn from billions of examples, but the idea is the same: learn patterns, then build something new one small piece at a time. Ask students to run the lab model several times and look for a sentence that was not in the training text. Discuss: why can a confident-sounding AI answer still be wrong? CS2.K5 · Chatbots

Chatbots answer in real time

Where is the science fair?

The science fair is in the school gym.

Can I bring my pet snake?

Sorry, I am not sure.

Simple chatbots look for keywords and pick a stored answer.

AI chatbots use a language model trained on huge amounts of text, so they handle many wordings and write new replies.

Neither one understands like a person. Check important answers.

Science 8 · CS2 · AI that makes, talks and suggests

A chatbot simulates a conversation with online users and answers in real time, meaning right away. The example is from the lab’s keyword chatbot; the science fair details are made up. The snake question fails because no keyword matches. Accuracy flag (CS2.K5): the draft says a chatbot can “understand questions”. Teach it as processing text and finding patterns of meaning; chatbots do not understand the way people do, AI chatbots can give confident wrong answers, and some chatbots are rule based with no AI. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6. Have students try an unusual wording to make the bot fail.

CS2.K6 · Recommendation algorithms

Your feed learns from what you do

You act

Watch, click, like, search, stay longer

It predicts

You will probably like more of the same

It shows

Your feed fills with that kind of content

This helps you find things you enjoy. It can also narrow what you see. A feed that shows more and more of the same thing is called a filter bubble.

Science 8 · CS2 · AI that makes, talks and suggests

A recommendation algorithm is an AI program that predicts content a person may like based on that person’s previous online activity. Streaming apps, online stores and social media use them. Accuracy flag (CS2.K6): many recommenders also use the activity of similar users and information about the content itself, and not every recommender uses machine learning; the lab’s feed model uses only the person’s taps. Demonstrate the lab feed: tap only one topic for ten taps and watch the mix. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7.

Worked example: in the final feed of 12 videos, if 5 are nature, 3 music, 2 science and 2 cooking, variety is 4 of 6 and the top share is 5 ÷ 12 × 100% ≈ 42%. Have pairs run each strategy in the lab, record results, then answer the three reflection prompts. Section 4 · CS2.K3 · CS2.K9 · CS2.S2

Fairness and ethics

AI can be unfair to some people. Using AI raises questions about what is right and responsible.

Set norms before this section: we discuss situations and systems, not classmates. Some examples involve accents, privacy and fake videos. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4 and 10.

CS2.K3 · Bias

Four ways bias gets in

Developers

A team tests a new app only on people like themselves and misses that it works worse for others.

Data sets

A voice assistant trained mostly on one accent understands other accents less often.

Algorithms

A program aims for the best average score, so a small group that does poorly gets hidden.

User input

Users rate unfair chatbot answers as helpful, so the chatbot learns to give more of them.

Science 8 · CS2 · Fairness and ethics

Bias in AI means results that are unfair or less accurate for some people or situations. Demonstrate the lab’s voice assistant model: at 85% accent A recordings, accent A is understood about 93% of the time and accent B about 75%; at 50% both are about 86%. These values come from a made-up model. Ask: who decided what recordings to collect? (Developers, so sources often overlap.) Accuracy note (CS2.K3): algorithm bias is taught as design choices in the steps and goals; user input includes feedback loops. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4, and Sort It, source of bias deck.

Science 8 · CS2 · Fairness and ethics

This is the main Lab Bench worked example. Reveal the right column one line at a time. The gap is 20 percentage points. Ask why hiding results for the city, or making the app faster, would not fix the bias. Then students open Lab Bench, Investigation 3: each New data press gives fresh numbers with a different low-accuracy place (forest, farmland or city). They calculate the lowest accuracy, choose the source and the best fix, then check. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 3.

Quick check

Three questions

1

An email filter learns from emails people marked “spam” or “not spam”. Which type of machine learning is this?

2

A voice assistant trained mostly on one accent understands other accents poorly. Where did the bias come from?

3

Why should you check facts in content made by generative AI?

Science 8 · CS2 · Review

Answers. 1: Supervised learning, because each example email has a label. 2: The data set, which had too few recordings of other accents (developers also chose what to collect). 3: Generative AI predicts what is likely, not what is true, so it can make content that sounds right but is wrong. Lab link: Quiz tab draws 10 questions from a bank of 25.

Debate · Written Review extended response 3

“AI will take everyone’s jobs.” Do you agree?

Take a position. Use an example of automation, a new career and a changing skill.

Run as a four corners debate: strongly agree, agree, disagree, strongly disagree. Each corner prepares two reasons using examples from the lab. A strong answer takes a clear position, gives an automation example (self-driving haul trucks, pill counting), a new career example (drone pilot, chatbot designer, fleet technician) and a changing skill example (pharmacists spending more time with patients, designers editing AI drafts), and reasons in a balanced way. Some students may have family members worried about jobs; keep the discussion respectful. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 3.
