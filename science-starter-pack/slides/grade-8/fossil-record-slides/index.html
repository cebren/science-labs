Earth’s history and fossils

Evidence from geological records provides information about Earth’s history.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Fossil Record Lab

Welcome students to the Earth Science unit. Read the understanding statement aloud and ask: what is a “geological record”? Accept ideas such as rock layers, fossils and landforms. Tell students that today they will learn to read rocks and fossils like pages in a history book. Lab link: open the Fossil Record Lab on the Learn tab.

Part 1

Deep time

How scientists organize 4.6 billion years of Earth’s history.

Section opener for ES1.K1 and ES1.S1. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 (Earth’s history on one ruler).

The geological time scale

Eons, eras and periods

Each interval marks big geological events and when life forms appeared and disappeared.

EraTime (Ma)Life forms appearEnds with
Precambrian4600 to 539Microbes (about 3500 Ma), then large complex lifeBurst of animals with hard parts
Paleozoic539 to 252Trilobites, land plants, fish with limb‑like fins, reptilesLargest mass extinction
Mesozoic252 to 66Dinosaurs, small mammals, birds, flowering plantsAsteroid impact: non‑bird dinosaurs and ammonites disappear
Cenozoic66 to todayEarly horses, mammoths, our species (0.3 Ma)Still going

Science 8 · ES1 · Deep time

ES1.K1. Ma means millions of years ago. The biggest intervals are eons, split into eras, split into periods. Example: Alberta’s dinosaurs lived in the Cretaceous period, Mesozoic era, Phanerozoic eon. “Precambrian” is an informal name for the three oldest eons (Hadean, Archean, Proterozoic). Ask: why might boundaries be placed where many fossils suddenly change? (Because big changes in life are easy to recognize in rock everywhere.) There have been five big mass extinctions: about 445, 372, 252, 201 and 66 Ma. Accuracy flag (ES1.K1): dates are rounded from the International Chronostratigraphic Chart (Phanerozoic from about 539 Ma, Earth about 4.6 billion years). Scientists keep refining them. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1; switch between “Whole history” and “Last 600 million years”.

Science 8 · ES1 · Deep time

ES1.S1. Earth formed on January 1 at midnight. Everything before mid‑November is the Precambrian. Students do not need to memorize the formula. Worked example (Lab Bench, Investigation 1): the end of the dinosaurs, 66 Ma. Fraction = 66 ÷ 4600 = 0.0143. Calendar: 0.0143 × 365 = 5.2 days before midnight, so about December 26. On a 100 m strip: 0.0143 × 100 m = 1.43 m from the “today” end. Our species: 0.3 ÷ 4600 × 365 = 0.024 days, about 34 minutes before midnight. Activity idea: give groups a 46 m rope or tape (1 m = 100 million years) and have them peg the events from Lesson 1. Part 2

Reading rock layers

Relative dating puts layers in order from older to younger.

Section opener for ES1.K2 and ES1.S2. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (order the rock face).

Relative dating

Three rules for reading layers

Superposition

In undisturbed layers, the oldest is at the bottom and the youngest is at the top.

Flat first

Layers are laid down flat. Tilted or folded layers were moved later.

Cross‑cutting

A fault or dike is younger than the layers it cuts through.

Relative dating gives the order, not an age in years.

Science 8 · ES1 · Reading rock layers

ES1.K2. Sedimentary rock forms as sand, mud or shells settle in layers; new layers pile on top. “Flat first” is the rule of original horizontality. A fault is a crack where rock has moved; a dike is a sheet of magma that pushed up through the layers and cooled. Accuracy flag (ES1.K2): superposition is stated for undisturbed layers only, since folding can overturn layers. Absolute (radiometric) dating, for example of volcanic ash, gives ages in years and is mentioned as context only. Discussion: a stack of newspapers on a porch. Which paper is oldest? What if someone pulled one out and stuck it back on top? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2.

Science 8 · ES1 · Reading rock layers

ES1.S2, main Lab Bench investigation. Model the thinking aloud: start at the bottom, move up, and place any cutting feature right after the last layer it cuts. Then have students solve three random puzzles each in the lab. Puzzles mix dikes and faults with three to five layers. Ask them to explain one puzzle to a partner using the words superposition and cross‑cutting. Extension: what would it mean if the dike reached all the way to the surface? (It would be the youngest part.) The rock face is a made‑up example for practice. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 2 (rock layer puzzles).

Part 3

Making a fossil

How fossils form, where they are found and how they are lost.

Section opener for ES1.K4, K5, K7 and ES1.S3. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 3 and 4.

How fossils form

Mineralized or compressed

Mineralized

Groundwater carries minerals into the tiny spaces in bone, shell or wood. They harden into a stony copy.

Dinosaur bone, petrified wood

Compressed

A leaf or soft body is squashed under layers. A thin dark carbon film is left behind.

Fern leaves in shale

Impressions

A shell dissolves and leaves a mold. Minerals fill it to make a cast. Footprints are trace fossils.

Molds, casts, tracks, burrows

Most fossils take thousands to millions of years to form.

Science 8 · ES1 · Making a fossil

ES1.K5 and ES1.S3. A fossil is the preserved remains or traces of a living thing from long ago. Accuracy flag (ES1.K5): the draft says “mineralized or compressed”. These are taught as the two main ways. Impressions (molds and casts), trace fossils and amber are added so students can explore the different types in S3. Walk through the six‑step story of the duck‑billed dinosaur in Lesson 3 (death beside a river, fast burial, layers pile up, minerals move in, deep time, uncovered). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3; then Lab Bench, Investigation 3 (fossil factory). Challenge: make all five fossil kinds in the factory.

Specific conditions

Why fossils are found in sedimentary rock

Fast burial

Mud, sand or ash hides remains from scavengers.

Hard parts

Bones, shells, teeth and wood last longest.

Little oxygen

Decay slows down.

Time

Minerals need thousands of years or more.

Rock typeHow it formsFossils?
SedimentaryLayers of sediment bury remains, then hardenYes, almost all fossils
IgneousMelted rock coolsNo: melting destroys remains
MetamorphicGreat heat and pressure change rockRarely: usually crushed or erased

Science 8 · ES1 · Making a fossil

ES1.K4. Most living things never become fossils. Ask: which is more likely to become a fossil, a clam in a muddy delta or a deer on a hilltop? Why? Accuracy flag (ES1.K4): “found in sedimentary rocks” is taught as where fossils are almost always found. Rare fossils occur in volcanic ash deposits and lightly metamorphosed rock (the Burgess Shale is slightly metamorphosed), and amber, ice and tar are not rock. “Thousands to millions of years” is taught as the usual time frame. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4; Sort It, deck “Fossil or not?”.

Science 8 · ES1 · Making a fossil

ES1.K7. Remains and impressions can be destroyed by geological processes: weathering and erosion at the surface, metamorphism (heat and pressure) deep underground, and melting into magma. Point out the irony: erosion is what lets people find fossils, but it also destroys fossils nobody collects in time. That is why Alberta paleontologists patrol the badlands every spring. Discussion: besides destruction, what else leaves gaps? (Soft bodies rarely fossilize; many fossils are still buried.) Counts are from the lab’s simple model and are made up for practice. Part 4

Reading the fossil record

What fossils tell us about past events, climates, environments and living things.

Section opener for ES1.K3, K6 and ES1.S4. The fossil record is all the fossils ever found, together with the rock layers they came from. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 5 and 6.

The fossil record

Alberta through time

Mudstone and coal, 77 to 66 MaFerns, turtlesPast climate: a warm, swampy coastal plain

Science 8 · ES1 · Reading the fossil record

ES1.K3. Read the table from the bottom up, oldest to youngest, as you would read rock layers. For each row ask: is this clue about a past climate or environment, a geological event, or a living or extinct organism? Some fossils tell us more than one thing. An organism is extinct when no members of its species are left alive. An index fossil was common, widespread and lived for a short time, so it helps match layers of the same age in different places. Accuracy flag (ES1.K3): the lab’s Alberta column is a composite (Devonian reef limestone, Borealopelta, Bearpaw‑type shale with ammonites, Late Cretaceous coal‑bearing mudstone, Ice Age gravel with mammoth bones near Edmonton). These layers are not all found in one place. Please verify the local examples. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (tap each fossil); Sort It, deck “What the fossil tells us”.

A geological event in the rocks

Sea fossils sit on top of the Rocky Mountains.

The rock formed under a warm sea. Between about 80 and 55 million years ago, it was pushed up to build the mountains.

Accent statement for ES1.K3 (geological events). Limestone with fossil corals and other sea animals forms many Rocky Mountain peaks. The Burgess Shale in Yoho National Park, British Columbia, holds sea animals about 508 million years old, now high in the mountains. Prompt: what other geological events could fossils record? (A layer where many kinds of fossils suddenly stop can mark a mass extinction; volcanic ash layers record eruptions.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 (event “The Rocky Mountains rise”) and Lesson 5 (coral).

Science 8 · ES1 · Reading the fossil record

ES1.K6 and ES1.S4. Model a good description: name the feature, the direction of change, and the evidence. Then link it to the environment: as forests gave way to grasslands, taller teeth lasted longer on gritty grass, and long legs on one toe suited running on open plains. Scientists call change in living things over many generations evolution. Accuracy flag (ES1.K6): the word evolution is used once in the lab as the scientific term, since the draft avoids it; please review. Horse sizes and dates are rounded, and the horse family is a branching bush with many side branches, not one straight line. Evidence of change

Comparing fossils and living organisms

375 Ma · Ellesmere Island, Nunavut

Tiktaalik

Gills and scales like a fish, but also a neck and sturdy fins with wrist‑like bones.

Living animals today

Matching bones

A human arm, a bat wing and a whale flipper have the same set of bones, arranged in different ways.

Fossils with mixed features, and shared body plans, are evidence that living things have changed over time.

Science 8 · ES1 · Reading the fossil record

ES1.K6. Tiktaalik was found in 2004 on Ellesmere Island in Nunavut, a Canadian discovery. It has features of both fish and four‑legged animals. Have students point to their upper arm, forearm, wrist and finger bones, then compare to a diagram of a bat wing and a whale flipper. Early whales are a second example in the lab: Pakicetus (about 50 Ma) had full legs; later fossils have smaller and smaller hind legs; modern whales keep small hidden hip bones. Dates are rounded. Discussion: why are fossils with mixed features so useful to scientists? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6; Lab Bench, Investigation 4 (Whales data set).

Part 5

Alberta’s badlands

Some of the richest dinosaur fossil beds on Earth.

Section opener for ES1.K8 and ES1.S5. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7 (map and discovery timeline).

Les mauvaises terres à traverser

“Bad lands to cross”

Early French explorers named the dry, steep, gullied land of what is now Alberta. The name badlands stuck.

Carved

Ice Age meltwater cut deep valleys along the Red Deer River.

Exposed

Rain and snowmelt wear soft rock away and uncover new bones every spring.

Renowned

Layers 77 to 66 million years old hold extensive dinosaur fossil beds.

The badlands lie in Treaty 7 territory. First Nations peoples knew these lands and their large bones long before scientists arrived.

Science 8 · ES1 · Alberta’s badlands

ES1.K8. Ask a Francophone student, or the class, to read the French phrase aloud. When these rocks formed, Alberta was a warm coastal plain beside an inland sea. Link back to K7: the same erosion that uncovers fossils will destroy them if they are not found in time. Accuracy flag (ES1.K8): the French phrase is taught as given in the draft. The badlands are noted as Treaty 7 territory, and First Nations knowledge of the bones is mentioned only generally, without specific community knowledge. Please review this wording and consider inviting a community partner. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7 (tap Drumheller, Dinosaur Provincial Park, Fort McMurray and Grande Prairie on the map).

Fossil discoveries in Alberta

Science 8 · ES1 · Alberta’s badlands

ES1.S5. Students choose one discovery and research it in the lab’s research notebook: what was found, who found it and how, which layer and how old, what it tells us, and how erosion helped. They should check each fact in two reliable sources, such as the Royal Tyrrell Museum, Alberta Parks or a university. The lab’s timeline also mentions Peter Fidler (1793), a Hudson’s Bay Company surveyor often credited with the first written record of large bones near the Red Deer River. Accuracy flags (ES1.K8 and S5): the discovery history (Fidler 1793, Tyrrell 1884, the 1910s Dinosaur Rush, Dinosaur Provincial Park 1955 and UNESCO 1979, Royal Tyrrell Museum 1985, Borealopelta 2011, Pipestone Creek bonebed) is summarized from public sources and needs verification. The summary of Alberta fossil rules (Historical Resources Act: fossils belong to the province, surface collecting on private land with the owner’s permission, no digging without a permit, no collecting in parks) needs verification before use. Quick check

Three questions

1

A fault cuts layers 1, 2 and 3. Layer 4 lies unbroken on top. Is the fault older or younger than layer 4?

2

Why are fossils almost never found in igneous rock?

3

You find coral fossils in limestone. What was the environment like when the rock formed?

Science 8 · ES1 · Review

Answers: 1. Older. The fault had to break layers 1 to 3 before layer 4 was laid down across it (cross‑cutting and superposition). 2. Igneous rock forms when melted rock cools. Melting destroys any remains, so fossils cannot survive. 3. A warm, shallow sea. Corals need warm, clear, shallow water. Use mini whiteboards or hand signals. For more practice, students can play a 10‑question round in the lab’s Quiz tab (a bank of 26 questions).

Extended response

Why is the fossil record incomplete?

Use what you know about how fossils form and how they can be destroyed. Give at least one example.

Which conditions does a fossil need?

Which living things rarely become fossils?

Which geological processes destroy fossils?

What about fossils that are still buried?

Science 8 · ES1 · Review

From the lab’s Written Review (extended response 2). Model answer: Most living things never become fossils. They need fast burial, hard parts, low oxygen and long periods of time. Soft‑bodied animals and animals living in dry uplands rarely fossilize. Even when fossils form, erosion can break them up, metamorphism can crush them and melting destroys them. Many fossils are still buried and never found. So the record has gaps, like a book with missing pages. Key ideas checklist: specific conditions are needed; soft bodies and uplands are rarely preserved; geological processes destroy fossils; many fossils are not yet found. Option: run it as a four corners discussion, one corner per prompt card, before students write. Lab link: Written Review tab, Extended response 2.
