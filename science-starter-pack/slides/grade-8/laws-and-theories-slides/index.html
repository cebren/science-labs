Scientific laws and theories

Scientific laws and theories play specific roles in describing and explaining phenomena.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Laws and Theories Lab

Open the unit by reading the understanding statement. Ask: what is the difference between describing something and explaining it? Collect a few everyday examples (describing a sunset versus explaining why the sky turns red). Lab link: open the Laws and Theories Lab on the Learn tab, Lesson 1.

Learning goals

By the end of this unit

I can compare scientific laws to scientific theories.

I can research a scientific law or theory.

Predict

Agree or disagree?

1. When a theory is proven, it becomes a law.

2. A scientific fact can never change.

3. Laws are more important than theories.

Write A or D for each, with one reason. We will come back to these.

Science 8 · SL1 · Predict

All three statements are false, and each is a common misconception. Do not reveal answers yet. 1: theories never become laws (Lesson 7). 2: facts can be revised or rejected with new evidence (Lesson 2). 3: laws and theories are equally important (Lesson 3). Use a quick show of hands or a four-corners activity. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 2, 3 and 7.

Part 1 · Lessons 1 to 3

Kinds of scientific knowledge

Facts, laws, theories and models each do a different job.

Section opener. Ask students to name one thing they are sure is true in science, then ask how they know. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 to 3.

SL1.K1

Four kinds of scientific knowledge

Fact

An observation confirmed again and again

Law

A description of what always happens under certain conditions

Theory

An explanation of why or how it happens

Model

A representation that helps explain or predict

Science 8 · SL1 · Kinds of knowledge

Scientific knowledge is more than a list of facts. Have students give one example of each for a single phenomenon, such as falling objects: fact (dropped objects fall), law (Newton’s law of universal gravitation describes the attraction), theory (general relativity explains gravity as curved space and time), model (a diagram of a falling ball with force arrows). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1. Students choose a phenomenon to see all four kinds of knowledge about it.

SL1.K2

Facts are confirmed, but open to revision

48

Human body cells were said to have 48 chromosomes for about 30 years.

46

In 1956, Joe Hin Tjio and Albert Levan used a better technique and counted 46. Other labs confirmed it.

Observations repeatedly confirmed may be accepted as facts. Additional evidence can still revise or reject them.

Science 8 · SL1 · Kinds of knowledge

Return to prediction statement 2: a fact can change. Stress that this is a strength of science, not a weakness. The better technique spread out the chromosomes so they could be counted clearly. Discussion prompt: why did other labs need to confirm the new count before textbooks changed? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (confirm or revise a fact). Students press the old-method button several times, then the new-method button.

SL1.K3

What laws and theories share

Widely accepted by the scientific community

Supported by large bodies of evidence

Equally important: neither ranks above the other

Used to make predictions about phenomena

Testable: an investigation could show them wrong

In everyday talk, “theory” can mean a hunch. In science, a theory is one of the strongest kinds of knowledge.

Science 8 · SL1 · Kinds of knowledge

Return to prediction statement 3: laws and theories are equally important; they do different jobs. Point out the everyday versus scientific meaning of “theory”. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (check the claim), and Lab Bench, Station 1 table.

Part 2 · Lessons 4, 5 and 7

Laws describe. Theories explain.

Two different jobs, and neither one turns into the other.

Section opener. Quick prompt: describe what happens when you drop a pencil, then try to explain why. Which was easier? Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4, 5 and 7, and Lab Bench, Station 1.

SL1.K4

Scientific laws describe

CAUSE AND EFFECT

Newton’s third law

When A pushes on B, B pushes back on A with an equal force in the opposite direction.

RULE

Law of superposition

In undisturbed rock layers, lower layers are older than the layers above.

EQUATION

Law of reflection

θr = θi

Angle of reflection equals angle of incidence.

Laws are considered universal under their stated conditions: a mirror in Edmonton, a lake in Jasper, a telescope in space.

Science 8 · SL1 · Laws and theories

A law describes what happens every time under stated conditions; it does not explain why. Point out the conditions in each law, such as “undisturbed” layers. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (test the law of reflection). Change the angle and the setting to show the law holds everywhere.

SL1.K5

Theories explain and connect

The theory of plate tectonics says Earth’s outer layer is broken into plates that move slowly over hotter rock beneath. One explanation connects many phenomena:

Earthquakes

Volcanoes

Mountains

Fossils

Continent shapes

Canadian geophysicist J. Tuzo Wilson helped build the theory in the 1960s by explaining transform faults and hot spots.

Science 8 · SL1 · Laws and theories

A theory explains why or how and integrates a wide range of knowledge: many facts, several laws, and evidence from different branches of science. Ask: which of these five phenomena have you seen evidence of in Alberta? (The Rocky Mountains formed as plates pushed together.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (one theory, many lines of evidence).

Comparing laws and theories

FeatureScientific lawScientific theory
Main jobDescribes what happensExplains why or how it happens
Usual formCause and effect statement, rule or equationA detailed explanation, often with models
ScopeUniversal under its stated conditionsIntegrates many facts, laws and fields
SharedWidely accepted, much evidence, equally importantUsed for predictions, testable

Can one become the other? No. A law never becomes a theory, and a theory never becomes a law.

Examples: “Lower rock layers are older” (law). “Plates move over hotter rock, causing earthquakes” (theory). “θr = θi” (law). Then students complete the station’s randomized practice. SL1.K8 · Busting the myth

Theories do not grow up into laws.

Newton’s law of universal gravitation describes how strongly objects attract. Einstein’s general relativity explains gravity as mass curving space and time. Both are still used.

Return to prediction statement 1. The myth says a hypothesis becomes a theory and a theory becomes a law once it is proven. Laws and theories answer different questions, so no amount of evidence turns an explanation into a description. Neither Newton’s law nor Einstein’s theory turned into the other. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7 (myth or science).

Part 3 · Lessons 6 and 8

Models, predictions and careers

Models help scientists explain, predict and test ideas. Many careers use them every day.

Section opener. Ask: what models have you used in science before? (Particle model, food webs, a globe, a circuit diagram.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 6 and 8; Lab Bench, Stations 3 and 4.

SL1.K6

Models explain and predict

A scientific model is a representation that helps explain or predict a phenomenon. Models come in many forms:

Diagrams

Rock layer cross sections

Objects

A balloon with dots

Equations

Charles’s law for gases

Simulations

Computer models of the universe

Every model has limits. In the balloon model, galaxies sit on a 2D surface, but real space has three dimensions.

Science 8 · SL1 · Models and careers

Ask students to name a limitation for each model type. A good model is useful, not perfect. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (balloon model of an expanding universe).

SL1.K7

Models tested the Big Bang theory

1927

Georges Lemaître, a Belgian physicist writing in French, proposes an expanding universe.

1929

Models predict distant galaxies move away faster. Edwin Hubble’s data matches.

1965

Predicted leftover heat is detected: the cosmic microwave background.

The theory explains that the universe began very hot and dense about 13.8 billion years ago and has been expanding ever since.

Science 8 · SL1 · Models and careers

Scientists use models to test hypotheses while developing theories. Hypothesis from the lab: if space itself is expanding, then galaxies farther from us will move away faster. Have students stretch the lab’s balloon model and compare near and far dots. Lemaître was also a Catholic priest; some students may raise questions about science and faith. Keep the focus on evidence and respect students’ beliefs. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6.

Lab Bench · Station 3 · SL1.S3

Predict with a model: a winter balloon

V₂ = V₁ × T₂ ÷ T₁

Charles’s law, at constant pressure. Temperature in kelvin: K = °C + 273.

Worked example

A 3.0 L balloon at 22 °C (295 K) goes outside at −30 °C (243 K).

V₂ = 3.0 L × 243 ÷ 295
V₂ = 2.5 L

Prediction: the balloon shrinks.

The particle model explains why: colder gas particles move slower and push outward less.

Science 8 · SL1 · Models and careers

The particle model of gases is part of kinetic molecular theory; Charles’s law was discovered by French scientist Jacques Charles. Students do not need to memorize the formula; they use it as a model to predict. Ask students to predict before calculating: bigger or smaller? Then check. Extension: what happens when the balloon comes back inside? (It returns to about 3.0 L.) Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Station 3 (gas temperature slider, then the randomized “predict, then test” problems, and the Drumheller rock layer model).

SL1.K9 · SL1.S4 · Lab Bench · Station 4

Careers that use laws and theories

GEOLOGY AND RESOURCES

Geophysicist

Applies wave laws to map rock, groundwater, oil and gas underground.

SUSTAINABILITY

Agronomist

Tests predictions with field trials so crops grow while soil and water stay healthy.

DESIGN CRITERIA

Instrumentation technician

Calibrates sensors against values predicted by gas laws.

Worked example: a sound pulse near Drayton Valley echoes back in 0.80 s through rock at 3000 m/s.

depth = speed × time ÷ 2 = 3000 × 0.80 ÷ 2 = 1200 m

Science 8 · SL1 · Models and careers

Each career advances knowledge by developing, testing and applying laws and theories. In the worked example, ask why we divide by 2 (the pulse goes down and back up). The agronomist case uses canola trials near Vegreville, and the technician case checks a gas tank sensor that reads high as temperature rises. The trial and sensor numbers in the lab are made up for practice. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Station 4 (career case files) and Learn tab, Lesson 8.

Quick check

Three questions

1

“In undisturbed rock layers, lower layers are older.” Is this a law or a theory? How can you tell?

2

Give one example of a scientific model and one of its limitations.

3

Why did the accepted human chromosome count change from 48 to 46?

Science 8 · SL1 · Review

Answers. 1: A law (the law of superposition), written as a rule. It describes what happens but does not explain why. 2: Example: a balloon with dots models the expanding universe; its surface is 2D, but space is 3D. Accept any reasonable model and limitation. 3: In 1956 Tjio and Levan used a better technique and counted 46; other labs confirmed it, so the fact was revised with additional evidence. Extended response

“It’s just a theory”

Your friend says, “Evolution is just a theory. When scientists prove it, it will become a law.”

Write a response that corrects this idea using what you know about facts, laws and theories.

Science 8 · SL1 · Review

From the lab’s Written Review, extended response 1. Key ideas: in science a theory is a well-supported explanation, not a guess; theories are supported by large bodies of evidence; theories cannot become laws; laws describe and theories explain; laws and theories are equally important. Sensitive topic: some families hold religious views about evolution. Keep the focus on what the word “theory” means in science and on the law and theory distinction, not on persuading students about beliefs.
