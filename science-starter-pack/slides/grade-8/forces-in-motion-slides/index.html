Forces and motion

The motion of an object changes because of forces acting on it.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Forces in Motion Lab

Welcome students. Today’s unit question: why does motion change? Ask students to name three things that moved on their way to school and what made them start or stop. Lab link: open the Forces in Motion Lab on the Learn tab so students can try each model as you go. This deck follows the lab’s six lessons in the same order.

Predict

A puck leaves your stick and slides across the rink. Why does it stop?

A

It runs out of the force from the stick.

B

Something on the ice pushes back against it.

C

All moving things stop on their own.

Choose one and write a sentence to defend it. We will come back to this.

Science 8 · F1 · Opening

Take a quick vote with fingers or sticky notes. Do not give the answer yet. Many students choose A or C, which matches the old idea that motion needs a force to keep going. The answer is B: friction is an unbalanced force against the motion. Revisit this on the Newton’s first law and friction slides. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (same shove, different surfaces).

Part 1 · F1.K1 and F1.K2

Forces add up

Every push and pull counts. The total, called the net force, decides what happens next.

Section opener. Ask: when two people push a stalled car from the same side, what happens to the total push? What if one pushes from the front? This sets up adding and subtracting forces.

F1.K1 · What is a force?

A force is a push or a pull

Newtons

Forces are measured in newtons (N). A small apple pushes on your hand with about 1 N.

Magnitude

The size of the force. A longer arrow means a bigger force.

Direction

The way the force points. The arrowhead shows it.

Science 8 · F1 · Forces add up

Pass around a small apple or a 100 g mass so students can feel about 1 N. Stress that every force needs both a size and a direction, which is why we draw arrows. Discussion: why would “a force of 50 N” not be enough information to predict what a box does? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

F1.K1 · Net force

Net force is the sum of all forces

Three students pull a sled

60 N right

40 N right

70 N left

Same direction: add. Opposite directions: subtract.

(60 + 40) − 70
= 30 N right

The net force points toward the bigger side.

Science 8 · F1 · Forces add up

Model the worked example, then change one number and have students solve on whiteboards: what if the left pull became 100 N? (Net 0 N, balanced.) What if it became 130 N? (30 N left.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 tug of war model, then Lab Bench Investigation 1 for randomized practice.

F1.K2 · Balanced forces

Zero net force: motion does not change

At rest

Stays at rest

A book on a desk. Gravity pulls down 15 N and the desk pushes up 15 N. A tied tug of war works the same way.

Already moving

Keeps the same speed and direction

A car at a steady 100 km/h. The road’s push on the tires equals air resistance and friction.

Science 8 · F1 · Forces add up

Accuracy note for F1.K2: the draft says the object’s motion “will not change.” Teach both cases explicitly: at rest stays at rest, and moving keeps the same speed and direction. Ask students to draw a force diagram for each card: a box with one arrow per force, equal lengths for equal forces. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (balanced force diagrams, four examples including a canoe gliding steadily).

Common mistake

Balanced forces do not mean an object is still.

A canoe gliding at a steady speed has balanced forces. So does a parked truck.

Pause here for a think, pair, share. Ask students to name another moving object with balanced forces (an elevator rising at constant speed, a bike coasting steadily on flat ground). Then ask for one object at rest with balanced forces. This misconception returns on the quiz and in Sort It (balanced or unbalanced deck).

Part 2 · F1.K3, F1.K4 and F1.K5

Forces change motion

Unbalanced forces speed things up, slow them down or turn them. Mass decides how hard that is.

Section opener. Quick prompt: think of a time you had to push something heavy, like a loaded wagon or a car stuck in snow. Was it harder to start or to stop? Keep the answers for the mass and inertia slide.

F1.K3 · Unbalanced forces

The net force decides the change

Speeds up

Net force points the same way as the motion.

Slows down

Net force points against the motion. It may stop, then reverse.

Turns

Net force acts at an angle to the motion.

Blue arrows show motion. Ember arrows show the net force. A bigger net force makes a bigger change.

Science 8 · F1 · Forces change motion

Accuracy note for F1.K3 and F1.S2: net force problems stay on one horizontal line. The sideways card only shows that a force at an angle changes direction; students do not add forces at angles. Also note that a backward net force slows an object, which may stop and then reverse if the force keeps acting. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (hockey puck motion map); Sort It, “What happens to the motion?” deck.

Reading a puck’s motion map

Each dot shows where the puck is every quarter second, like a strobe photo. The puck moves to the right.

No net force: even gaps

Forward net force: gaps grow

Backward net force: gaps shrink

Ask: which row shows the puck speeding up, and how can you tell without numbers? (Bigger gaps in equal time steps mean more distance each step.) Have students sketch a motion map for a puck that slows, stops and then slides back. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3; try each push and the push size slider. F1.K4 · Newton’s first law

The law of inertia

An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion keeps moving in a straight line at a constant speed, unless an unbalanced force acts on it.

Inertia

The tendency of an object to resist changes in its motion. It is a property of the object, not a force.

1687

Isaac Newton published his three laws of motion. This is the first one.

Science 8 · F1 · Forces change motion

Accuracy note for F1.K4: inertia is taught as a property of matter, not a force. Avoid phrases like “the force of inertia.” Have students restate the law in their own words in two sentences: one for objects at rest, one for moving objects. The 1687 date (Principia) is context only. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4; Flashcards include Newton’s first law and law of inertia.

F1.K4 · Why things stop

Friction is the unbalanced force

Surface (puck at 5 m/s)Slides about
Rough asphalt2.6 m
Gym floor5.1 m
Outdoor rink25.5 m
Freshly flooded ice63.8 m
No friction (imagined)never stops

The puck does not run out of motion. Friction and air resistance push against it until it stops.

Less friction means a longer slide. With no friction at all, it would keep going forever.

Distances are estimates for a smooth puck.

Science 8 · F1 · Forces change motion

Return to the hook question: the answer is B. Friction from the ice is an unbalanced force against the motion. Ask why curling and hockey rinks are flooded and resurfaced (smoother ice, less friction). The distances come from the lab’s model and are estimates, not measurements. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (same shove, different surfaces).

F1.K5 · Mass and inertia

More mass, more inertia

Same 80 N push for 1 sSpeed after
Empty toboggan, 10 kg8 m/s
With gear, 20 kg4 m/s
One rider, 40 kg2 m/s
Two riders, 80 kg1 m/s

Double the mass, half the change in speed. A bigger mass needs a bigger force for the same change.

Mass is the amount of matter. Weight is the pull of gravity. Mass, and so inertia, is the same on Earth and in space.

Model values with friction ignored.

Science 8 · F1 · Forces change motion

Accuracy note for F1.K5: mass is kept separate from weight. The halving pattern is shown as data only; F = ma is not taught at this grade. Connect to the opener: a loaded toboggan is harder to start and harder to stop. Context: toboggans were developed by First Nations peoples for hauling loads over snow. This is stated in general terms only; please review the wording and avoid attributing it to a specific Nation without a community source. Part 3 · F1.K6

Measuring motion

To describe how motion changes, we first need to measure it. Speed links distance and time.

Section opener. Ask: how would you prove who is faster, a friend on a bike or a friend on a scooter? Students usually suggest racing over the same distance and timing. That is the idea behind v = d ÷ t.

F1.K6 · Speed

Speed is distance divided by time

v = d ÷ t

speed = distance ÷ time

Units: metres per second (m/s) or kilometres per hour (km/h).

Convert: m/s × 3.6 = km/h.

Average speed: the formula gives the overall rate for a whole trip, even if the speed changes along the way.

Science 8 · F1 · Measuring motion

Accuracy note for F1.K6: the draft writes v = d/t. The letter v usually stands for velocity, which includes direction. In this unit it means average speed only, with no direction. Students do not need to memorize the formula or its rearrangements (d = v × t, t = d ÷ v). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (distance and time graph: walker, runner, cyclist).

F1.K6 · Worked example

A cyclist on a river valley trail rides 1200 m in 240 s

1 · Formula

v = d ÷ t

2 · Substitute

1200 m ÷ 240 s

3 · Answer

v = 5 m/s

4 · Convert

5 × 3.6 = 18 km/h

Your turn: a train moves at 15 m/s for 60 s. How far does it go? Use d = v × t.

Science 8 · F1 · Measuring motion

Model the four steps aloud and insist on units at every step. Your turn answer: d = 15 m/s × 60 s = 900 m. Remind students they can look up the rearranged forms; the skill is choosing and substituting correctly. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 3 (speed calculator with steps, then randomized practice problems).

Walk through the plan before the data: independent variable distance, dependent variable time, controlled variables car, speed setting and batteries, floor surface. Ask why each trial is repeated three times (to average out stopwatch reaction time). Quick check

Three questions

1

Forces of 40 N right, 25 N right and 65 N left act on a cart that is at rest. What is the net force, and what does the cart do?

2

The same push is given to a 20 kg toboggan and a 40 kg toboggan. Which one gets the bigger change in speed, and why?

3

A runner covers 200 m in 40 s. What is the runner’s average speed?

Science 8 · F1 · Review

Answers. 1: (40 + 25) − 65 = 0 N. The forces are balanced, so the cart stays at rest. 2: the 20 kg toboggan gets about twice the change in speed, because it has less mass and so less inertia. 3: v = 200 m ÷ 40 s = 5 m/s. Use mini whiteboards. If many miss question 1, revisit the balanced forces slide; if many miss 2, revisit mass and inertia. Lab link: Quiz tab for ten more questions from a bank of 24.

Extended response

Which grocery cart is harder to stop?

A full grocery cart and an empty one roll toward a wall at the same speed. Use Newton’s first law and inertia to explain which is harder to stop, and why.

A strong answer includes

Newton’s first law · stopping needs an unbalanced force · more mass means more inertia · a clear comparison

Science 8 · F1 · Review

Give 8 to 10 minutes of quiet writing, then peer review against the checklist. Model answer: both carts keep rolling because of inertia; by Newton’s first law they keep moving unless an unbalanced force acts. Stopping either one needs a force against the motion. The full cart has more mass and so more inertia, so it needs a bigger force, or the same force for longer, to stop.
