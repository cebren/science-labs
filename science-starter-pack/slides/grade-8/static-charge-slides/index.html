Science 8 · Energy: Stores, Static and Waves · EN2

Static electricity

Static electricity accumulates and can be discharged, which results in the transfer of energy.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Static Charge Lab

Welcome students to the static electricity understanding. Read the understanding statement aloud and ask: what does “discharged” mean in everyday life (a battery, a hospital patient)? Here it means charge leaving an object. The three dots stand for the three ideas in the lab colours: red for positive charge, violet for electrons (negative), amber for sparks. Lab link: open the Static Charge Lab on the Learn tab before class so students can follow along.

Predict

Why do doorknobs zap you in January but almost never in July?

January in Edmonton

Cold, dry air. Wool socks, a fleece toque and a carpet hallway.

July in Edmonton

Warm air with more moisture in it. Same carpet, same doorknob.

Write a prediction: what is different, and where does the zap come from?

Science 8 · EN2 · Hook

Give students two minutes to write a prediction, then collect a few ideas without correcting them. Accept any reasonable answer for now. The answer they will reach by Lesson 5: walking moves electrons onto you, and in dry winter air that charge cannot leak away, so it builds until it sparks to the metal knob. Humid summer air carries charge away slowly. Revisit these predictions on the discharge slides. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (doorknob spark model with dry and humid air).

Part 1 · EN2.K1 · EN2.K2

Building up charge

What charge is, how rubbing moves electrons, and how to count the result.

Section opener. Ask: have you ever seen your hair stand up after pulling off a toque? Tell students that by the end of this part they will be able to explain it with the word electron. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 and 2.

EN2.K1 · Charge imbalance

Static electricity is a charge imbalance on a surface

4 p · 4 e

Neutral

Equal protons and electrons. The charges cancel.

4 p · 6 e

Negative

Extra electrons were gained.

4 p · 2 e

Positive

Electrons were lost. Protons never move.

Static means staying still: the charge stays on the surface until something lets it move.

Science 8 · EN2 · Building up charge

Key teaching move: protons are locked in the centre of atoms, so only electrons move. Negative means extra electrons; positive means missing electrons. Common error to address: “it became positive because it gained protons.” Ask students to explain why that is wrong. Accuracy flag (EN2.K1): the draft defines static electricity as “the accumulation of electrons on the surface of a material.” The lab teaches it more precisely as a charge imbalance, since a positively charged surface has missing electrons, not accumulated ones. The p and e counts are made up for practice. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 (atom charge count model).

EN2.K2 · Rubbing

Rubbing moves electrons from one surface to another

LOSES ELECTRONS

Your hair

Becomes positively charged.

GAINS ELECTRONS

A rubber balloon

Becomes negatively charged.

The arrow shows the balloon pulling electrons from the hair. The two charges are equal in size: no charge is made or destroyed, it only moves.

Science 8 · EN2 · Building up charge

Demonstrate with a real balloon and a volunteer’s hair if you can (dry days work best). Ask: which way did the electrons move? (From the hair to the balloon.) Emphasize conservation: the balloon’s negative charge equals the hair’s positive charge. Accuracy flag (EN2.K2): the lab teaches that touching and pulling apart moves electrons, and rubbing adds many more points of contact. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (the rubbing test), choose Human hair and Rubber balloon.

EN2.K2 · The rubbing series

The lower material takes the electrons

TENDS TO LOSE ELECTRONS (+)

Dry skin · Glass · Human hair · Nylon · Wool · Silk · Paper

TENDS TO GAIN ELECTRONS (−)

Cotton · Rubber balloon · Polyester · Plastic wrap · Vinyl (PVC) · Teflon

Glass rubbed with silk

Silk is lower, so silk gains electrons and becomes negative.

Glass loses electrons and becomes positive.

Results change with humidity and dirt. Use the list as a guide.

Science 8 · EN2 · Building up charge

Read the series as one list from top (Dry skin) to bottom (Teflon). Practice: wool and vinyl (vinyl becomes negative); hair and balloon (balloon negative); nylon and Teflon (Teflon negative). Two of the same material: almost no transfer. Accuracy flag (EN2.K2): this is a common classroom order. Sources disagree on some positions, and real results depend on humidity and surface condition, so treat it as a model. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 1 (the rubbing test, with predictions and a data table).

Lab Bench · Investigation 3 · Count the charge

Worked example: a balloon on hair

net charge = protons − electrons

A balloon has 6000 protons and 6000 electrons. Rubbed on hair, it gains 250 electrons.

Made-up practice numbers. Real objects have trillions of particles.

electrons = 6000 + 250 = 6250

net = 6000 − 6250

−250

Negative: extra electrons. The hair is now +250.

Science 8 · EN2 · Building up charge

Walk through the steps aloud. Stress the sign: gaining electrons gives a negative answer, losing electrons gives a positive answer. Try one together: a glass rod with 4000 protons and 4000 electrons loses 150 electrons when rubbed with silk. Answer: electrons = 3850, net charge = +150. Formula is shown so students can reason with it, not to memorize. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 3 (count the charge) gives randomized practice.

Part 2 · EN2.K3 · EN2.S2

Push and pull

How charged materials interact, and why a charged balloon sticks to a neutral wall.

Section opener. Quick demo idea: hang two balloons rubbed on the same hair from strings and let students observe them push apart. Ask for a prediction before you release them. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 3 and 4.

EN2.K3 · Three rules

Charged objects push or pull without touching

+ ← → +

Like charges repel

Two balloons rubbed on the same hair push apart.

+ → ← −

Unlike charges attract

The balloon and the hair pull together.

− → ← 0

Charged attracts neutral

A charged comb picks up bits of paper, by induction.

The force gets stronger as the objects get closer.

Science 8 · EN2 · Push and pull

Have students act out the rules in pairs with + and − cards. Ask: if object A attracts object B, can you be sure B is charged? (No, B could be neutral.) This sets up the tape test. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (choose two charges) and Sort It, attract, repel or nothing deck.

EN2.K3 · Induction

Induction rearranges charge in a neutral object

The balloon’s extra electrons push electrons in the can to the far side. The near side is left positive.

The near side is closer, so the pull beats the push. The can rolls toward the balloon.

The can is still neutral overall. In paper or a wall, charge only shifts a tiny bit inside each atom.

Left: negative balloon. Right: neutral metal can, near side positive, far side negative. Arrow: the can is pulled toward the balloon.

Science 8 · EN2 · Push and pull

Demo: roll an empty pop can across a desk with a charged balloon. Then stick a balloon to the wall. Ask: is the can charged? (No, its charge was only rearranged.) Accuracy flag (EN2.K3): the draft calls attraction to neutral objects “induction.” The lab teaches it as induced charge separation: electrons move across a conductor, while in an insulator charge shifts within atoms and molecules (polarization, named once). The object stays neutral overall. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (bring the balloon closer); switch between paper and metal can.

Lab Bench · Investigation 2 · EN2.S2

The sticky tape test

Stick a bottom tape (B) to the desk and a top tape (T) on it. Rip them apart quickly. T and B now have opposite charges.

Hang them from a ruler. A paper strip (P) is neutral. Predict, then test.

PairResultWhy
T and TRepelSame charge
B and BRepelSame charge
T and BAttractOpposite charges
T and PAttractInduction
P and PNothingBoth neutral

Science 8 · EN2 · Push and pull

Run the real investigation if you have tape: each pair of students makes two T and two B strips and records results in a table like this one. Cover the Result column first and have students predict. Discussion: which result proves both strips are charged? (Repelling.) Results in the lab are a model; real tape can lose its charge in humid air within minutes. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 2 (sticky tape test, randomized trials).

The sure test for charge

Anything charged can attract. Only like charges repel.

If it repels a charged object, you know it is charged.

Pause here and ask students to explain the statement in their own words to a partner. Link back to the tape test: T repelling T proves both strips are charged, while T attracting P does not prove P is charged. Lab link: Written Review, short answer 6.

Part 3 · EN2.K4 · EN2.S1 · EN2.K1

Letting it go

How a spark discharges static, where the energy goes, and how machines put static to work.

Section opener. Return to the hook predictions from the start of the lesson: which ones mentioned electrons moving or dry air? Tell students they will now test those ideas. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 5 and 6.

EN2.K4 · EN2.S1 · Discharge

A spark sends electrons back and transfers energy

After shuffling across a carpet you carry extra electrons. Just before your finger touches a metal knob, they jump across the tiny air gap.

Light

The blue flash you see in a dark room.

Sound

The snap you hear.

Heating

The air in the gap warms up.

The energy came from the separated charges. Afterward you are neutral again.

Science 8 · EN2 · Letting it go

Connect to the understanding statement: discharge results in the transfer of energy. Ask: why does a second touch of the knob not shock you? (You already lost the extra charge.) You also feel the shock because moving charge stimulates nerves. Accuracy flag (EN2.K4): the draft says the spark happens “when a conductor touches the material.” The lab teaches that the spark jumps across a small air gap just before contact. Carpet charging is modelled as the person gaining electrons, which depends on the shoe and carpet materials. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (the doorknob spark model).

EN2.K4 · Quiet discharge

Charge can also leave without a spark

Damp air

Moist summer air carries charge away slowly. Dry Alberta winters let it build.

Grounding

A conductor connected to the Earth lets charge flow away safely.

Safety at the gas station

Touch a metal part of the car away from the nozzle before you pick up the pump. You discharge safely, far from fuel vapour.

Science 8 · EN2 · Letting it go

Answer the hook: in July the moist air carries charge away before it can build, so shocks are rare. Ask: why might a hospital or an electronics factory use grounding straps? (To keep charge from building and sparking near sensitive equipment.) Accuracy flag (EN2.K4): the lab adds quiet discharge through humid air and grounding, which the draft does not name. The gas station advice reflects common Canadian safety guidance; please verify current wording. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5; switch between Dry winter day and Humid summer day.

EN2.K1 · Static at work

Charge the particles, then attract them

Laser printer

A laser draws the page as a pattern of charge on a drum. Charged toner sticks to the pattern, is pulled onto paper, then melted in.

Paint sprayer

Droplets get the same charge and spread out evenly. The grounded object attracts them, so less paint is wasted.

Air filter

Dust and smoke get a charge, then stick to oppositely charged plates. Clean air flows out.

Science 8 · EN2 · Letting it go

Jigsaw idea: assign each table one machine and have them explain it to the class using the words charge, attract and repel. Ask: which machine uses like charges repelling as well as unlike charges attracting? (The paint sprayer.) These descriptions are simplified from the lab. Big air filters, called electrostatic precipitators, clean smoke from factory smokestacks. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (step through a machine).

Part 4 · EN2.K5 · EN2.S3

Nature’s biggest spark

How a summer thundercloud over the prairie separates charge, and what happens when it lets go.

Section opener. Sensitive topic: some students may have had frightening experiences with storms, lightning fires or injuries. Keep the tone factual and focused on safety. Ask: what do you already know about where lightning comes from? Record ideas to check against research. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7 (inside a thunderstorm).

EN2.K5 · Charging a cloud

A thundercloud separates charge

1

Collide

Rising air carries small ice crystals up. Heavier soft hail, called graupel, falls. When they bump, electrons move.

2

Separate

Crystals, usually positive, rise to the top. Graupel, usually negative, gathers lower down.

3

Induce

The negative cloud base repels electrons in the ground, so the ground below becomes positive.

Science 8 · EN2 · Nature’s biggest spark

Link each step to earlier ideas: step 1 is like rubbing (contact moves electrons), step 3 is induction on a giant scale. Accuracy flag (EN2.K5): the ice crystal and graupel collision model is the leading explanation and is still being researched. Cloud tops are usually positive and the lower cloud usually negative; real storms are more complex. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7, stages 1 to 4.

EN2.K5 · The strike

Lightning is a giant static discharge

When the charge difference is big enough, the air can no longer insulate.

A faint leader steps down from the cloud. A streamer rises from a tall object to meet it. Then the bright return stroke flashes along the path.

Most lightning stays inside a cloud or jumps between clouds.

ABOUT

30 000 °C

Hotter than the surface of the Sun. The air expands so fast that it booms: that is thunder.

Energy is transferred as light, heating and sound.

Science 8 · EN2 · Nature’s biggest spark

Compare with the doorknob spark: same idea, much bigger scale. Ask students to name the three energy transfers in a lightning strike and match each to the doorknob spark. Accuracy flag (EN2.K5): lightning temperature is rounded to about 30 000 °C; sources give roughly 20 000 °C to 30 000 °C. Leader, streamer and return stroke are named for research only. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7, stages 5 and 6.

Lab Bench · Investigation 4 · EN2.S3

Flash to bang: how far is the storm?

d = v × t  (v ≈ 340 m/s)

Light arrives almost instantly. Thunder travels at the speed of sound. Quick rule: about 3 seconds for every kilometre.

You count 9 seconds from flash to thunder.

d = 340 m/s × 9 s = 3060 m

≈ 3.1 km

When thunder roars, go indoors. Stay inside until 30 minutes after the last thunder.

Science 8 · EN2 · Nature’s biggest spark

Try two more together: 6 seconds is about 2.0 km (2040 m); 15 seconds is about 5.1 km (5100 m). The formula is shown for reasoning, not memorizing. Safety: if you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck. The 30 minute rule matches Environment and Climate Change Canada guidance; please verify current wording. Quick check

Three questions

1

A balloon rubbed on hair becomes negative. What charge does the hair have, and why?

2

A charged rod attracts a small object. Can you be sure the object is charged?

3

Name the three ways energy is transferred when a spark jumps to a doorknob.

Science 8 · EN2 · Review

Answers. 1: Positive. The hair lost electrons to the balloon; protons did not move. 2: No. A charged object also attracts neutral objects by induction. Only repelling proves the object is charged. 3: Light (the flash), sound (the snap) and heating of the air. Use mini whiteboards or a quick show of hands. Extended response · Debate

“Static electricity is useless. It only gives people shocks.”

Agree or disagree? Defend your position with evidence.

Use at least

Two technology examples and one safety example.

Use the words

Charge, attract, repel, discharge, energy transfer.

Science 8 · EN2 · Review

Run as a four corners debate (strongly agree to strongly disagree), then have students write a paragraph. A strong answer disagrees: laser printers place charged toner, paint sprayers charge droplets so less paint is wasted, and air filters charge smoke particles and collect them on plates. Safety example: touching a metal part of the car before using a gas pump so no spark happens near fuel vapour. A balanced conclusion admits static can be a nuisance or a danger but is useful when it is controlled. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 4, with a model answer and key idea checklist.
