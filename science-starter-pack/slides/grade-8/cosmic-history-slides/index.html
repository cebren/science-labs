Science 8 · Space · SP1

Origin of the universe

Understandings of the universe have changed over time.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Cosmic History Lab

Welcome students and introduce the unit. Today’s big idea: our picture of the universe has changed a lot, and it changed because of new tools and new evidence. The companion lab is the Cosmic History Lab; students can follow along in the Learn tab, which has seven lessons in the same order as these slides. Progress in the lab saves only in the student’s browser.

Learning goals

By the end of this lab, I can…

examine how planets, solar systems, galaxies and the universe are related.

explore how understandings, theories and models of the universe have changed over time.

describe the origin and evolution of the universe according to the Big Bang theory.

compare the visibility of various stars in the Milky Way.

explore the life cycle of a star.

Science 8 · SP1 · Learning goals

These five goals are the SP1 skills statements (S1 to S5) written as “I can” goals. Ask students to rate themselves 1 to 3 on each goal now, and again on the wrap-up slide. Lab link: the Coverage tab lists each goal and where the lab teaches it.

Predict

On a dark night, are the stars you see in our galaxy or in other galaxies?

Write your prediction and one reason. We will come back to it.

Give students one minute to write a prediction and a reason. Many will guess “other galaxies” or “both”. Do not give the answer yet: Lesson 2 shows that almost all naked-eye stars are in the Milky Way, and the one easy exception (the Andromeda Galaxy) is a faint smudge, not single stars. Follow-up prompt: “How could anyone measure how far away a star is?” Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2.

Science 8 · SP1 · Our place in the universe

Return to the hook prediction: almost every star you can see with no telescope is in the Milky Way, mostly within a few thousand light-years. The Milky Way band is the blended glow of millions of distant stars in our galaxy’s disc, seen edge on because we live inside it. The Andromeda Galaxy is the main exception: a faint smudge of blended light from about 2.5 million light-years away, not single stars. Local link: Elk Island National Park is part of the Beaver Hills Dark Sky Preserve; Jasper is also a dark-sky preserve. Review flag: star counts per sky type are rough values for the half of the sky above you. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (sky darkness slider).

Section 2

Changing ideas

How understandings, theories and models of the universe changed over time.

Section opener for Lesson 3. Define model (a simpler picture that helps explain or predict) and theory (a well-tested explanation supported by many lines of evidence). Ask: “What would make you change your mind about something you were sure of?” Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3.

SP1.K4 · SP1.S2

Early ideas: Earth at the centre

About 1600 to 300 BCE

Babylonia

Recorded the Moon and planets on clay tablets for centuries. Found repeating patterns and predicted eclipses.

About 350 to 270 BCE

Ancient Greece

Aristotle placed Earth at the centre and used Earth’s curved shadow to show it is round. Aristarchus suggested the Sun was at the centre, but few agreed.

About 150 CE

Ptolemy

Built an Earth-centred model with small circles called epicycles. It predicted planet positions for about 1400 years.

Each idea was built on the best observations of its time.

Science 8 · SP1 · Changing ideas

Teach K4’s named examples: Babylonia, ancient Greece and Ptolemy. Stress that Ptolemy’s model was useful: it predicted well even though its picture was wrong. Also mention that astronomers in the Islamic world, India, China and elsewhere improved observations (Chinese astronomers recorded the 1054 supernova). Sensitive content and review flag (SP1.K4): First Nations, Métis and Inuit peoples have long traditions of reading the sky for seasons, travel and teachings; the lab and deck acknowledge this generally without specific community knowledge. Invite a Knowledge Keeper if possible. Historical dates are approximate and need a spot check. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (timeline of ideas).

SP1.K4 · SP1.S2

New tools, new evidence

1543

Copernicus

A full model with the Sun at the centre.

1609 to 1610

Galileo

With a telescope: four moons of Jupiter, a full set of phases of Venus, and a Milky Way made of countless stars.

1912 to 1929

Leavitt, Hubble, Lemaître

Andromeda is a separate galaxy. Galaxies are moving apart.

1965 to today

Modern astronomy

A faint microwave glow from every direction. Space telescopes see early galaxies.

Science 8 · SP1 · Changing ideas

Galileo is K4’s named astronomer. Ask: “Which of Galileo’s observations did NOT fit Ptolemy’s model?” Answer: the full set of Venus phases (only possible if Venus circles the Sun) and moons circling Jupiter (not everything orbits Earth). Then the 1900s: Henrietta Leavitt’s distance method, Edwin Hubble’s measurement of Andromeda, Georges Lemaître and Hubble on expansion, and Penzias and Wilson’s 1965 detection of the microwave glow. Canada built parts of the James Webb Space Telescope. Review flag: dates are approximate. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3; Lab Bench, Investigation 2 (evidence check).

The pattern

New tools bring new evidence. New evidence changes our models.

Old models were not foolish. They fit the evidence people had.

Pause for discussion: “Is the Big Bang theory the final answer?” Guide students to see that scientific theories are well supported but still tested; new evidence could refine them. Quick pair task: name one tool and the change it caused (telescope and Galileo; distance measurements and Hubble; microwave detectors and the cosmic microwave background). Lab link: Sort It tab, “Who added the idea?” deck.

Section 3

The Big Bang theory

The origin of the universe, and the path from hydrogen and helium to our solar system.

Section opener for Lessons 4 and 5. Quick poll: “What do you picture when you hear ‘Big Bang’?” Many students picture an explosion at one spot in empty space; the next slide corrects that idea. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4 and 5.

SP1.K5 · SP1.S3

A universe that is stretching

About 13.8 billion years ago, everything we can now observe was packed into an extremely small, hot and dense state, with enormous temperature and pressure.

Since then the universe has been expanding and cooling.

It was not an explosion at one spot. Space itself stretches everywhere, so there is no centre.

Left: before. Right: space stretched 2 times. The gold galaxy is “home”. Every other galaxy moved away, and the far ones moved the most.

Science 8 · SP1 · The Big Bang theory

Review flag (SP1.K5): the draft says the universe started “from a dense, single point state”. Teach it more precisely: an extremely small, hot and dense state of everything we can now observe, about 13.8 billion years ago. It was not an explosion at one point in empty space; space expands everywhere and has no centre. Science does not yet describe the very first instant. Analogy: raisins in rising bread dough, or dots on an inflating balloon. Every raisin sees all others moving away. Diagram positions are made up. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (tap a galaxy to make it home, then drag the stretch slider).

SP1.K5 · SP1.S3

Evidence for the Big Bang

1

Galaxies moving apart

Light from distant galaxies is stretched toward red. Farther galaxies move away faster.

2

A leftover glow

A faint microwave glow comes from every direction. It is light left over from the hot early universe.

3

Mostly hydrogen and helium

Ordinary matter is about three quarters hydrogen and one quarter helium by mass, as the theory predicts.

Science 8 · SP1 · The Big Bang theory

These three lines of evidence are brief context beyond the statement wording; students do not need the details, only the idea that a theory rests on evidence. Ask: “Which piece of evidence would surprise you most, and why?” Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 2 (evidence check) includes these observations.

Science 8 · SP1 · The Big Bang theory

Walk the chain of K6 in order: hydrogen and helium, first stars, heavier elements, solar system. Review flag (SP1.K6): hydrogen and helium nuclei form in the first minutes and full atoms at about 380 000 years; a trace of lithium also forms. Heavier elements are made mainly in stars and spread by star deaths. Times and temperatures are rounded. Discussion: “Why couldn’t the first stars have rocky planets like Earth?” (Little or no heavier elements yet.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (cosmic history strip).

SP1.S3 · Lab Bench

The cosmic calendar

Squeeze 13.8 billion years into one year. The Big Bang is January 1. Today is the last moment of December 31.

day of the year = (time after the Big Bang ÷ 13.8 billion years) × 365

First stars · 0.2 billion years

January 6

Solar system · 9.2 billion years

September 1

Dinosaurs die out · 66 million years ago

December 30

Science 8 · SP1 · The Big Bang theory

Worked example for the solar system: 13.8 − 4.6 = 9.2 billion years after the Big Bang; 9.2 ÷ 13.8 = 0.667; 0.667 × 365 ≈ 243.3 days, so after 243 full days the date is about September 1. First stars: 0.2 ÷ 13.8 × 365 ≈ day 5.3, about January 6. Dinosaurs: 13.734 ÷ 13.8 × 365 ≈ day 363.3, about December 30. The dinosaur date is context added for scale. Students do not need to memorize the formula. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 3 (calendar converter with practice).

Section 4

Stars

Why some stars look brighter than others, and how a star is born, changes and dies.

Section opener for Lessons 6 and 7. Ask: “Is the brightest star in the sky the most powerful star?” Collect guesses; the next two slides answer it. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 6 and 7.

SP1.K7 · SP1.S4

How bright a star looks

Luminosity

The total light energy a star gives off each second. More luminous looks brighter.

Distance

Farther looks dimmer, fast. Twice as far looks one quarter as bright. Three times as far looks one ninth as bright.

apparent brightness ∝ L ÷ d²

StarLuminosity (Suns)Distance (light-years)How it looks
Siriusabout 25about 8.6brightest night star

Science 8 · SP1 · Stars

Apparent brightness depends on luminosity and distance (K7). The full form b = L ÷ (4π d²) is context only; students should remember the idea, not the formula. Sirius looks brightest mainly because it is close; Deneb is about 8000 times as luminous but looks dimmer because it is about 300 times as far. Review flag (SP1.K7/S4): luminosities and distances are rounded estimates, and some are uncertain; the model ignores dust. Ask: “Could a dim star that is close look brighter than a powerful star far away?” Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (two-star test, Polaris vs Deneb preset).

SP1.S4 · Lab Bench investigation

Brightness calculator: a worked example

brightness ratio A ÷ B = (LA ÷ LB) × (dB ÷ dA)²

Step 1 · The question

Star A has 100 times the luminosity of star B. Star A is 5 times as far away.

Step 2 · Luminosity part

100

A gives off 100 times the light.

Step 3 · Distance part

(1 ÷ 5)² = 1/25

5 times as far means 25 times dimmer.

Step 4 · Answer

100 ÷ 25 = 4

Star A looks 4 times as bright as star B.

Science 8 · SP1 · Stars

Model the calculation, then have students try the lab’s randomized practice. Common mistake: dividing by the distance instead of the distance squared (100 ÷ 5 = 20). These are made-up practice stars. Students are not asked to memorize the formula. Extension: “How much farther would star A need to be to look exactly as bright as star B?” (10 times as far: 100 ÷ 100 = 1.) Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 4 (brightness calculator and New practice button).

Science 8 · SP1 · Stars

K8: formation, changes in mass, size and brightness, and star death. Both paths begin with a nebula and a protostar and spend most of their lives on the main sequence, fusing hydrogen into helium. Stars above about 8 Suns end in a supernova; above about 20 Suns they usually leave a black hole. Red dwarfs live for trillions of years, so none has died yet; their ending as a white dwarf is predicted. Review flag (SP1.K8/S5): mass boundaries (about 0.5, 8 and 20 Suns) and lifetimes are rounded. Quick check

Three questions

1

Put these in order from smallest to largest: Milky Way, Earth, universe, solar system.

2

Which observation by Galileo did not fit Ptolemy’s Earth-centred model?

3

Two stars give off the same light. Star A is twice as far away as star B. How bright does star A look compared with B?

Science 8 · SP1 · Review

Answers: (1) Earth, solar system, Milky Way, universe. (2) Venus showing a full set of phases, or moons orbiting Jupiter; either shows not everything circles Earth. (3) One quarter as bright, because (1 ÷ 2)² = 1/4. Have students answer on mini whiteboards, then discuss misconceptions. Lab link: Quiz tab (10 questions per round from a bank of 25).

Debate

“The brightest stars in the night sky must be the most powerful stars in the galaxy.”

Agree or disagree. Use luminosity and distance, with at least one real star as an example.

This is Extended response 4 in the lab’s Written Review. Model answer: disagree. Apparent brightness depends on luminosity and distance. Sirius looks brightest because it is close (about 8.6 light-years) even though it is only about 25 times as luminous as the Sun. Deneb is around 200 000 times as luminous but looks dimmer because it is roughly 2600 light-years away. Key ideas: takes a position, names both factors, uses a real example, explains that twice as far means one quarter as bright. Run as a four-corners debate or a written exit slip.
