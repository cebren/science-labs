Design criteria and testing

Design criteria and testing can help turn ideas into solutions.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Design Criteria Lab

Welcome. Today’s understanding: design criteria and testing can help turn ideas into solutions. Students will design, test and improve a syringe hydraulic arm, a build many Alberta Grade 8 classes make in the fluids unit. This deck follows the Design Criteria Lab in teaching order. Predict

Two teams say their arm is “strong.” Whose arm is better?

Team 1

“Our arm is really strong. Everyone who tried it agreed.”

Team 2

“Our arm is very strong. It lifted lots of stuff.”

What would you need to know to decide?

Science 8 · CS3 · Hook

Think, pair, share for two minutes. Collect ideas on the board. Steer toward: how much mass, how high, how many tries, under the same conditions. The point is that “strong” cannot be checked; we need specific criteria and a fair test. Return to this slide after Lesson 3. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

Part 1 · CS3.K1

Design criteria

What does success look like, in words we can check?

Section opener. The class need for the whole lesson: a hydraulic arm that can move a small load from one desk spot to another. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

CS3.K1

Criteria are the requirements a solution must meet

NEED

Move a small load from one desk spot to another.

DESIGN CRITERION

The arm lifts a 100 g load at least 10 cm.

Criteria say what success looks like, so the need, want or problem is really addressed.

Science 8 · CS3 · Design criteria

Definition from CS3.K1: design criteria are the specific requirements a solution must meet to ensure needs, wants or problems are addressed. A need on its own is too vague to build or test. Ask: what would we measure to know the arm works? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

CS3.K1 · Make it measurable

Vague words become numbers

VagueSpecific and measurable
“It should be strong”Lifts a 100 g load at least 10 cm
“It should be fast”Moves a paper cup 20 cm sideways in under 30 s
“It should not leak”Loses no more than 5 drops of water after 20 open and close cycles
“It should fit”Base fits inside 30 cm × 30 cm on a desk

Science 8 · CS3 · Design criteria

Cover the right column and have pairs rewrite each vague criterion before revealing. Good criteria name a number and a unit and can be checked with a test. These four are the criteria used throughout the lab. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1, Make it measurable. Sort It deck: Measurable or vague.

CS3.K1 · Needs and wants

Some criteria are musts, some are wants

Must

The solution fails without it.

Lifts 100 g at least 10 cm. Moves the cup in under 30 s. Loses 5 drops or fewer. Fits on a desk.

Want

Nice to have, not required.

Looks tidy enough to show at the open house.

Science 8 · CS3 · Design criteria

Accuracy flag for review (CS3.K1): the must and want split is a common classroom extension (sometimes called requirements and desirables). It is not in the draft statement, which names needs, wants and problems. Use it to help students rank criteria. Ask: which criterion would you give up first if time ran short? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

Part 2 · CS3.K2

Constraints

What limits do we have to work within?

Section opener. Criteria say what the solution must do. Constraints say what you have to do it with. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2.

CS3.K2

A constraint is a limit you must work within

Budget

$10 per team for parts

Materials

Only what is in the supply bin

Time

Built and tested in four class periods

Safety

No hot glue without a teacher present

Size

Base fits inside 30 cm × 30 cm

Tools

Scissors, rulers and hole punches only

Science 8 · CS3 · Constraints

Accuracy flag for review (CS3.K2): the line between criteria and constraints is not sharp in practice; some engineering sources list constraints as a kind of requirement. The lab teaches a working distinction: criteria say what success looks like, constraints limit how you can reach it. Note that size appears both as a must criterion (fits on a desk) and as a constraint: a good discussion point. Ask students which constraint would affect their arm most. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2, Constraint explorer. Sort It deck: Criterion or constraint.

CS3.K2 · Impacts on innovation

One constraint, two possible effects

Constraint: each team may spend only $5.

Negative impact

Cheaper, weaker parts. The arm may bend, break or leak sooner and miss its criteria.

Positive impact

Teams invent clever, low-cost ideas, like a recycled cardboard frame and a clothespin gripper.

Science 8 · CS3 · Constraints

CS3.K2 says constraints can have positive or negative impacts on innovation. Ask for one more example of each from students’ own lives (a phone with limited storage, a recipe with missing ingredients). Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 1 (Budget build). Try the $5 budget: no build meets all four criteria, which shows the negative side; the free materials students reach for show the positive side. Prices in the lab are made up for practice.

CS3.K2 · Apollo 13, April 1970

Only the items on board. A working air filter adapter anyway.

The crew sheltered in the small lunar module after an explosion damaged their spacecraft. The command module’s square filter canisters did not fit the lunar module’s round openings. Engineers designed an adapter from bags, cardboard, hoses and tape. The crew built it and made it home safely.

Science 8 · CS3 · Constraints

A positive impact of a constraint on innovation (CS3.K2). Accuracy flag for review: the story is described generally; please check the wording suits your class. Carbon dioxide was building up because the lunar module’s filters were not meant for three people for that long. Ask: what were the criteria (remove carbon dioxide, safe to build in space) and what were the constraints (only items on board, limited time)? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2.

Part 3 · CS3.K3

Testing

How do we know if a criterion was met?

Section opener. CS3.K3: solutions can be tested to determine whether design criteria were met. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3.

CS3.K3 · Fair test

Change only what you are testing

MEASURE

Height the load is lifted, in cm. It matches the criterion: lifts 100 g at least 10 cm.

KEEP THE SAME (CONTROLLED VARIABLES)

Same 100 g load
Same starting position
Same amount of water in the syringes
Same operator, after a practice run

Science 8 · CS3 · Testing

A good test measures exactly what the criterion names, with its unit. Controlled variables are kept the same in every trial. Ask: what would go wrong if Team 2 used a heavier load than Team 1? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3; Sort It deck: Fair test or not.

Science 8 · CS3 · Testing · Made-up practice data

These trial results are made up for practice. Ask: if we had stopped after trial 1, what would we have concluded? Results vary because of small differences in how each trial is done. The average of several trials is more reliable. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3, Lift test. Students press Run a trial and watch the average settle.

Lab Bench · Investigation 2 · CS3.S2

Worked example: a test plan

Plan stepExample
CriterionLoses no more than 5 drops of water after 20 open and close cycles
MeasureDrops of water lost after 20 full cycles, caught in a tray
Keep the sameWater in each syringe, speed of each cycle, operator, tubing
TrialsThree, refilling to the same level each time
DecidePass if the average loss is 5 drops or fewer

Science 8 · CS3 · Testing

Model the five steps, then have pairs plan a test for the cup move time criterion (measure time to move the cup 20 cm; keep the same cup, start position and operator; three trials; pass if the average is under 30 s). Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 2, Test planner. The Check my plan button flags unfair choices such as using a heavier load after a good trial.

Part 4 · CS3.K4

Analyze and improve

What does the data say is strong, and what needs work?

Section opener. CS3.K4: data can be analyzed to identify potential strengths and weaknesses of a design. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4 and 5.

CS3.K4 · Strengths and weaknesses

Compare every average with its target

Criterion (target)Prototype APrototype BPrototype C
Lift height (at least 10 cm, higher is better)10.6 cm, meets12.8 cm, meets8.9 cm, misses
Cup move time (under 30 s, lower is better)27 s, meets41 s, misses24 s, meets

Science 8 · CS3 · Analyze · Made-up practice data

Averages are made up for practice and match the lab. Ask: which prototype has no weaknesses (A)? Which is the best lifter but too slow (B)? Which is fastest but fails two criteria (C)? Point out the direction of each target: higher is better for lift, lower is better for time and leaks. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4, target line graph.

Lab Bench · Investigation 3 · CS3.S3

Worked example: strength or weakness?

PROTOTYPE C · CUP MOVE TIME

Target: under 30 s
Trials: 26 s, 29 s, 32 s

(26 + 29 + 32) ÷ 3 = 87 ÷ 3 = 29 s

29 s is under 30 s, so the criterion is met. One trial failed, so it is a borderline strength worth watching.

Science 8 · CS3 · Analyze · Made-up practice data

This is the worked example from Investigation 3. Steps: add the trials, divide by the number of trials, compare with the target, decide strength or weakness. Then the lab asks which improvement targets a weakness. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 3, Data analyzer; New problem gives random practice.

CS3.S3 · Improve and retest

Fix the weakness, then check the trade-off

Prototype BTargetBeforeAfter
Lift heightat least 10 cm12.8 cm10.4 cm, still meets
Cup move timeunder 30 s41 s, misses28 s, meets
Water lost5 drops or fewer1 drop1 drop

Change: a smaller output syringe on the base joint. Each push moves the arm farther, but with less force.

Science 8 · CS3 · Analyze · Made-up practice data

A trade-off: gaining speed cost some lift. That is why we retest after every change. Accuracy note: the force and distance trade-off in syringe hydraulics is stated qualitatively only, borrowed from the fluids unit. Ask students why the other lab options failed: a third joint made it slower and leakier; air squashes under pressure so the arm lost lifting power; paint helped only a want. Quick check

Three questions

1. Is “each team may spend no more than $10” a criterion or a constraint?

2. Cup move times are 28 s, 31 s and 34 s. The target is under 30 s. Is the criterion met?

3. Why should you retest after making an improvement?

Science 8 · CS3 · Check

Answers. 1: a constraint, because a budget limits how you build. 2: no. The average is (28 + 31 + 34) ÷ 3 = 93 ÷ 3 = 31 s, which misses the target, even though one trial passed. 3: a change can affect other criteria (a trade-off), so you check every criterion again. Use mini whiteboards. Lab link: Quiz tab for ten more questions from a bank of 24.

Debate · Written Review

“A bigger budget always leads to a better design.”

Agree or disagree. Use evidence from the lab.

Science 8 · CS3 · Debate

Extended response 4 from the Written Review. Have students stand on a line from agree to disagree, then defend their spot. Strong answers: a $10 arm with craft sticks and a clothespin met all four criteria in the budget build, while the costly 3D printed claw only helped control a little; constraints can spark innovation, as on Apollo 13; but a budget that is too small can stop a design from working at all.
