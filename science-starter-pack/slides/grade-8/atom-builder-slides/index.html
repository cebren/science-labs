Atomic structure

Atoms can be defined by their structure.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Atom Builder Lab

Welcome students. Big idea: every atom has a tiny nucleus of protons and neutrons, with electrons around it, and the number of protons tells you which element it is. Have the Atom Builder Lab open: Learn tab, Lesson 1. This deck follows the lab’s five lessons and four Lab Bench stations.

Lesson 1 · M1.K1

All matter is made of atoms

Matter

Anything with mass that takes up space: your desk, the air, the North Saskatchewan River.

Atom

The smallest piece of an element that still has that element’s properties.

Element

A pure substance made of only one kind of atom, such as carbon, oxygen or gold.

One drop of water holds more than a thousand billion billion atoms.

Science 8 · M1 · Everything is atoms

Accuracy flag (M1.K1): taught as all ordinary matter. Dark matter is not made of atoms as far as scientists know; keep that caveat for curious students or the teacher note rather than the main lesson. Prompt: name three things in the room that are not made of atoms. (Trick question for matter: light and sound are not matter.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

Lesson 2 · M1.K2

Can this tool see an atom?

NO · LIMIT ABOUT 200 NM

Light microscope

Visible light waves are too long. Atoms are 0.1 to 0.5 nm wide.

YES · ABOUT 0.1 NM

Scanning tunnelling microscope

A sharp tip measures a tiny electric current to map single atoms.

YES · IN GOOD CONDITIONS

Atomic force microscope

A tiny tip on a flexible arm feels the surface, like reading braille.

YES · THE BEST ONES

Transmission electron microscope

Electrons pass through a very thin slice to show rows of atoms.

Science 8 · M1 · Everything is atoms

The scanning tunnelling microscope was invented in 1981 by Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer, who won a Nobel Prize for it; the atomic force microscope followed in 1986. Researchers at the University of Alberta in Edmonton use these microscopes to study and even move single atoms on silicon surfaces. Accuracy flag (M1.S2): Canadian research examples need a name check before sharing. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (can this tool see an atom?).

Tiny centre, almost all the mass

IF THE NUCLEUS WERE

a blueberry

THE WHOLE ATOM WOULD BE

Most of an atom is empty space. Protons and neutrons are each about 1836 times heavier than an electron, so the nucleus holds almost all the mass.

Science 8 · M1 · Inside an atom

The blueberry comparison is the lab’s scale analogy. Discussion: if atoms are mostly empty space, why can’t you walk through a wall? (The electrons of the wall’s atoms push back on the electrons in your hand; you can mention this briefly without more detail.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3; Written Review short answer 8.

Accuracy flag (M1.K3): neutrality is taught as equal numbers of protons and electrons. Atomic model

A theoretical representation of an atom’s structure.

Theoretical

Based on evidence and reasoning, not on a direct photo. It changes when new evidence appears.

Science 8 · M1 · Atomic models

Connect to the Grade 8 launch unit on laws and theories: models are tools for explaining evidence. Ask: what other models do you use in science? (Globe, food web, particle model of matter.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4.

Lesson 4 · M1.K5

How the model changed

1803 · DALTON

Solid, indivisible spheres.

1904 · THOMSON

Plum pudding: electrons in a ball of positive charge. He found the electron in 1897.

1911 · RUTHERFORD

Gold foil: a few particles bounced back. A tiny, dense, positive nucleus.

1913 · BOHR

Electrons in shells at set distances. Explained hydrogen’s colours of light.

1926 · ELECTRON CLOUD

Electrons behave like waves. A cloud of where they are likely to be.

1932 · CHADWICK

The neutron is discovered. The basic structure is complete.

Science 8 · M1 · Atomic models

For each card ask: what evidence made scientists change the model? Gold foil (1909): Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, working with Ernest Rutherford. Canadian connection from the lab: Rutherford did his first prize-winning work on radioactivity at McGill University in Montréal, where Harriet Brooks, one of Canada’s first nuclear physicists, worked with him. Rutherford predicted a neutral particle in 1920 before Chadwick found it. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (atomic model timeline). Written Review extended 2.

Key idea · M1

The number of protons decides the element.

Change the neutrons or electrons and it is still the same element.

Example: take one electron from sulfur and it is still sulfur, now with a +1 charge. Add a neutron to carbon and it is still carbon. This sets up the Periodic Table lab (M2), where elements are ordered by atomic number. Lab link: Lab Bench, Station 1.

Quick check

Three questions

1. Which two subatomic particles are found in the nucleus?

2. Nitrogen-14 has atomic number 7. How many protons, neutrons and electrons does a neutral atom have?

3. Why can’t a light microscope show a single atom?

Science 8 · M1 · Check understanding

Answers. 1: protons and neutrons. 2: 7 protons, 7 neutrons (14 − 7), 7 electrons; arrangement 2, 5. 3: visible light cannot show details smaller than about 200 nm, and atoms are only about 0.1 to 0.5 nm wide. Use mini whiteboards. Lab link: Quiz tab for a 10 question round.

Extended response

Neon-20 (Z = 10) and sodium-23 (Z = 11): what makes them different elements?

Count each atom’s protons, neutrons and electrons.

Draw or describe each electron arrangement.

Explain which number decides the element.

Science 8 · M1 · Check understanding

Model answer from the lab: Neon-20 has 10 protons, 10 neutrons (20 − 10) and 10 electrons, arranged 2, 8. Sodium-23 has 11 protons, 12 neutrons (23 − 11) and 11 electrons, arranged 2, 8, 1. Neon’s two shells are both full. Sodium has one extra electron, which must start a third shell. They are different elements because they have different numbers of protons.
