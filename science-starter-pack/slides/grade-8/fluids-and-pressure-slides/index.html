PREDICT FIRST

Which plunger moves in more?

Syringe A: air

The tip is sealed. You push the plunger.

Syringe B: water

The tip is sealed. You push with the same force.

Make a prediction. Write one sentence that says which plunger moves in more, and why.

Science 8 · F2 · Getting started

Hook. Give students one minute to write a prediction alone, then share with a partner. Do not give the answer yet. Push for a reason: “What is inside each syringe? What could the plunger squeeze?” Take a quick hand vote: A moves more, B moves more, or both the same. SECTION 1 · LEARN LESSONS 1 AND 2

Particles and pressure

Why fluids flow, and where the push of pressure comes from.

Section 1 covers F2.K1, F2.K2 (pressure) and F2.K3. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 (What is a fluid?) and Lesson 2 (Where pressure comes from). Ask students to keep the particle model in mind for the whole unit: every later idea (pressure, viscosity, compressibility, hydraulics) is explained by how particles are arranged and how they move.

LESSON 1 · F2.K1

Fluids flow and have no fixed shape

Solid: not a fluid

Particles are packed tightly and held in place. They can only vibrate.

Liquid: a fluid

Particles are close together, but they slide past each other. A liquid takes the shape of its container and keeps its volume.

Gas: a fluid

Particles are far apart and move freely in every direction. A gas spreads out to fill the whole container.

Science 8 · F2 · Particles and pressure

F2.K1. Key sentence: a fluid is any substance that can flow and has no fixed shape. Liquids and gases are both fluids. Teaching move: have three groups of students act out solid (arms linked, swaying in place), liquid (close, shuffling past each other) and gas (spread out, walking freely). Ask: “Which groups can take the shape of the room? Which group keeps its size?” Answer: liquid and gas take the shape of the container; the liquid keeps its volume, and the gas spreads to fill the whole space. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1, “Model: particles in three states”.

REMEMBER · F2.K1

“Fluid” does not mean “liquid.”

Liquids and gases are both fluids. The air in your classroom is a fluid too.

Science 8 · F2 · Particles and pressure

Common misconception: many students use “fluid” to mean “liquid”. Ask: “Name three fluids in this room right now.” Push for air as an answer. Then ask: “Is sand a fluid? It pours.” (No. Each grain is a solid with a fixed shape; the grains just tumble.) Practice: Sort It tab, “Fluid or not?” deck, which includes local examples such as water in the North Saskatchewan River. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

LESSON 2 · F2.K3

Pressure comes from collisions

Orange arrow: the wall pushes back on the particle, so it bounces off.

1

Gas particles are always moving. Each hit on the wall is a tiny push.

2

The wall pushes back with an equal but opposite force. The particle bounces off.

3

Millions of hits each second add up. That push spread over an area is pressure.

Science 8 · F2 · Particles and pressure

F2.K3. Walk through the three steps. Discussion prompt: “If the wall did not push back, what would happen to the particle?” (It would not bounce; it would keep going.) Run the gas box model live and point out the orange flashes, which show the wall pushing back on a particle. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2, “Model: gas particles in a box”. REVIEW FLAG (F2.K3): the lab teaches the collision model with a gas in a box, showing the wall’s equal but opposite push on each bounce. A short note adds that liquid pressure also grows with depth, because of the weight of liquid above (next slide). Temperature is taught as harder, more frequent hits so students can predict pressure changes.

Science 8 · F2 · Particles and pressure

Pressure goes up when there are more hits or harder hits. Use the gas box model: raise the number of particles, then shrink the box, and have students watch the “wall hits per second” and “pressure (relative)” readouts. Prediction prompt: “A sealed bag of chips sits in a hot car. What happens to the pressure inside? Why?” (It goes up, because warmer particles move faster and hit harder and more often.) Quiz items that match this slide: squeezing gas into a smaller box (pressure increases) and heating a sealed box (pressure increases because particles move faster). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2. SECTION 2 · LEARN LESSON 3 · LAB BENCH INVESTIGATION 1

Pressure, force and area

The same push can sink you into the snow or keep you on top of it.

Section 2 covers F2.K2 (pressure as force over an area), F2.K5 and the skills F2.S1 and F2.S2. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (Pressure, force and area, with the “standing on snow” model), then Lab Bench, Investigation 1 (pressure calculator and practice). Quick opener: ask who has walked through deep snow in boots, and what helped them stay on top.

LESSON 3 · F2.K2 · F2.K5 · F2.S1

Pressure is force over area

P = F / A

Pressure is the force pushing perpendicular (straight into) a surface, divided by the area of that surface.

P

pressure, in pascals (Pa)

F

force, in newtons (N)

A

area, in square metres (m²)

1 Pa is one newton on each square metre. That is small, so we often use kilopascals. 1 kPa = 1000 Pa.

The unit is named after Blaise Pascal, a French scientist and mathematician of the 1600s who studied fluids and air pressure.

Science 8 · F2 · Pressure, force and area

Read the formula as a sentence: “pressure equals force divided by area.” Stress “perpendicular”: the push that counts goes straight into the surface. Check for understanding: “If the force doubles and the area stays the same, what happens to the pressure?” (It doubles.) “If the area doubles and the force stays the same?” (It halves.) Context from the lab’s Flashcards: air pressure is about 101 kPa at sea level and about 94 kPa in Edmonton; these are rounded values. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3. REVIEW FLAG (F2.K5): the draft curriculum calls P = F/A “Pascal’s law”. In physics, P = F/A is the definition of pressure. Pascal’s law (pressure added to an enclosed fluid is passed on equally throughout) is taught separately, in Lesson 6. The lab keeps the formula symbolic. The pascal unit is named for Blaise Pascal (France, 1600s); this is the lab’s Francophone contribution, flagged for Lance to review. REVIEW FLAG (F2.K2): pressure is taught as the force exerted over an area, as in the draft.

LESSON 3 · F2.S1

Same force, different area

Snowshoes

Large area, so small pressure. You stay on top of deep snow.

Boots

Smaller area, so bigger pressure. You sink into the snow.

Skate blades

Tiny area, so a very large pressure. The blade cuts into the ice.

Snowshoes were developed by Indigenous peoples of North America for winter travel.

Science 8 · F2 · Pressure, force and area

The same force on a smaller area makes a bigger pressure. The orange arrow is the same size in all three cards: only the area changes. Prompt: “Why do snowshoes keep you from sinking?” (They spread your weight over a larger area, lowering the pressure.) “Why does a skate blade cut into ice more than a boot?” (Its small area makes a very large pressure.) Use the “standing on snow” model and change the force slider while switching between snowshoes, boots and skate blades. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3, “Model: standing on snow”. SENSITIVE TOPIC AND REVIEW FLAG (examples): the snowshoe sentence describes Indigenous knowledge in general terms, as the lab does (“developed by Indigenous peoples of North America”). The wording needs Lance’s review. Do not add claims about a specific Nation unless you have a trusted local source, and consider inviting local knowledge keepers to share their own history of snowshoe making.

Science 8 · F2 · Pressure, force and area

Worked example from Lesson 3: a student pushes down with 600 N on boots with a total area of 0.06 m². Model the four steps: know, formula, substitute with units, answer with units. Point out that 10 000 Pa is the same as 10 kPa because 1 kPa = 1000 Pa. Then try the lab’s practice problems together. 1) A box pushes down with 300 N on an area of 0.5 m². Pressure? (P = 300 ÷ 0.5 = 600 Pa.) 2) A pressure of 2000 Pa acts on 0.3 m². Force? (F = P × A = 2000 × 0.3 = 600 N.) 3) Written Review: a force of 1500 N on 0.25 m². (P = 6000 Pa = 6 kPa.) LESSON 4 · F2.K2 · F2.S3

Viscosity: resistance to flowing

Honey

Water

High viscosity: honey

Honey pours slowly. A marble sinks slowly through it. A viscous fluid also resists objects moving through it.

Low viscosity: water

Water pours quickly. A marble sinks quickly through it.

Why? Look at the particles

In a thick liquid, the particles tug on each other more strongly, or are tangled. They do not slide past each other easily.

Science 8 · F2 · Viscosity and compressibility

Viscosity is a fluid’s resistance to flowing. Ask students to rank honey, water, vegetable oil and air from most to least viscous before you run the model. (Honey has the highest viscosity of these at room temperature.) The picture shows both tubes at the same moment after the drop: the marble in honey has barely moved. Run the marble drop test model and stress that its times are simulated for practice. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4, “Model: marble drop test”. REVIEW FLAG (F2.K2): the draft curriculum defines viscosity as “a fluid’s resistance to motion”. The lab teaches it as resistance to flowing, and adds that a viscous fluid also resists objects moving through it (the marble drop test). The warming effect is taught for liquids only (next slide).

LAB BENCH INVESTIGATION 2 · LESSON 4 · F2.S3

A fair test for viscosity

Time a steel marble falling 20 cm through each fluid. Run three trials, then average. A longer time means a more viscous fluid.

KEEP THE SAME

The same marble

KEEP THE SAME

The same tube and fall distance

KEEP THE SAME

The same temperature for every fluid

Warming a liquid

Warming most liquids lowers their viscosity. The particles move faster and slide apart more easily. Cold syrup pours slowly. Warm syrup runs.

Alberta example

Bitumen from the oil sands is so viscous that it is mixed with a thinner liquid, called diluent, before it can flow through a pipeline.

Science 8 · F2 · Viscosity and compressibility

Investigation 2 asks: how long does a steel marble take to fall 20 cm through different fluids at room temperature? Before running trials, students tick the controlled variables in the lab. The trap option is “a different fluid each time”: that is the variable we change on purpose, not one we keep the same. Then “Run all trials” and complete the Conclude question. Extension: test honey at 5 °C, 20 °C and 40 °C to see how temperature changes viscosity. Discussion: “Why do we run three trials and average?” (Times include small stopwatch errors.) Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 2 (viscosity test). REVIEW FLAG (examples): all viscosity times in the lab are simulated practice data, not real measurements; tell students this. The bitumen and diluent example is rounded and simplified. REVIEW FLAG (F2.K2): warming lowers viscosity for liquids only, as the lab teaches; do not extend the rule to gases.

LESSON 5 · F2.K2 · F2.K4 · F2.S4

How much can a fluid squeeze?

Compressibility is how much a fluid’s volume changes when pressure pushes on it.

Gases: highly compressible

Gas particles have lots of empty space between them. A push moves them closer together, so the volume shrinks a lot. That is how a bike tire or a scuba tank holds so much air.

Liquids: nearly incompressible

Liquid particles are already touching or nearly touching. There is almost no space to squeeze out. Water’s volume changes by only about 0.005% for each extra 100 kPa of pressure.

Science 8 · F2 · Viscosity and compressibility

Return to the hook prediction. Answer: the air plunger moves in a lot; the water plunger hardly moves at all. The reason is the space between particles (F2.K4). Run the sealed syringe model (plunger area 3 cm², starting volume 60 mL, air starts at about 100 kPa) and push on the plunger with air, then with water. Then do Lab Bench Investigation 3, the squeeze test: two sealed 60 mL syringes stand upright, one with air and one with water; add 10 N weights one at a time and record the volume. Ask which statement fits the data. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5, then Lab Bench tab, Investigation 3. REVIEW FLAG (F2.K4): liquids are taught as nearly incompressible, not perfectly incompressible. The figure of about 0.005% per extra 100 kPa for water is a rounded reference value. REVIEW FLAG (examples): syringe volumes and plunger areas are simulated or approximate practice data.

SECTION 4 · LEARN LESSON 6

Pascal’s law and hydraulics

How a small push on a trapped liquid can lift a car.

Section 4 covers F2.K5 (Pascal’s law, taught separately from P = F/A), F2.K6 (technologies) and the skills F2.S1, F2.S2 and F2.S5. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (Pascal’s law and hydraulics) with the hydraulic lift model and the “Pascal’s law in technology” panel. Opener question: “Have you seen a car lifted at a garage or a tire shop? How could one person’s push raise a car?”

LESSON 6 · F2.K5 · F2.S1

Pascal’s law

When you add pressure to a fluid trapped in a closed container, that pressure is passed on equally to every part of the fluid and the container walls.

Small piston: force F₁ on area A₁

Large piston: force F₂ on area A₂

P = F₁ / A₁ = F₂ / A₂

F₂ = F₁ × A₂ / A₁

A small force on a small piston makes a pressure in the oil. The pressure is the same at the big piston, so the big piston pushes with a big force.

Science 8 · F2 · Pascal’s law and hydraulics

Read Pascal’s law aloud, then trace the push on the diagram: orange arrow down on the small piston, the same pressure everywhere in the oil, a bigger orange arrow up at the large piston. A hydraulic system uses a trapped liquid, usually oil. Prompt: “Why does the big piston push harder if the pressure is the same?” (Pressure is force per area; a bigger area at the same pressure gives a bigger force.) Use the hydraulic lift model: change the input force and the two piston areas, and select a part of the lift to learn what it does. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6, “Model: hydraulic lift”. REVIEW FLAG (F2.K5): the draft curriculum calls P = F/A “Pascal’s law”. The lab teaches P = F/A as the definition of pressure (Lesson 3) and teaches Pascal’s law separately here: pressure added to an enclosed fluid is passed on equally throughout the fluid. The formula stays symbolic. The pascal unit is named for Blaise Pascal (France, 1600s), a Francophone contribution for Lance to review.

LESSON 6 WORKED EXAMPLE · F2.S2

Worked example: a hydraulic lift

1 · KNOW

F₁ = 50 N
A₁ = 5 cm²
A₂ = 100 cm²

2 · FIND THE PRESSURE

P = 50 N ÷ 5 cm²
= 10 N/cm²

3 · SAME PRESSURE

F₂ = 10 N/cm² × 100 cm²

4 · ANSWER

F₂ = 1000 N

The trade-off

The big piston moves a shorter distance than the small one. The work in and the work out stay the same.

Science 8 · F2 · Pascal’s law and hydraulics

Worked example from Lesson 6: 50 N on a 5 cm² piston gives P = 10 N/cm². On a 100 cm² piston, F₂ = 10 N/cm² × 100 cm² = 1000 N. Point out that we kept the area in cm², so the pressure is in N/cm², not pascals; that is fine as long as both areas use the same unit. The large piston has 20 times the area, so it gives 20 times the force, but it moves only one twentieth of the distance. Practice from the lab’s quiz: 20 N pushes a 2 cm² piston. What force acts on a 50 cm² piston? (P = 20 ÷ 2 = 10 N/cm²; F = 10 × 50 = 500 N.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6, “Model: hydraulic lift”; try these numbers in the model.

LESSON 6 · F2.K6

Fluids at work

Car brakes

Hydraulic

Your foot pushes a small piston. Brake fluid passes the pressure to bigger pistons at each wheel, which squeeze the brake pads.

Hospital bed

Can be hydraulic

Many can be raised with a hydraulic lift. A pedal pumps a small piston, and oil pushes a larger piston. Many newer beds use electric motors instead.

Jackhammer

Usually pneumatic

Compressed air pushes a piston up and down many times a second. The piston hammers the chisel into concrete.

Excavator arm

Hydraulic

A pump pushes oil into long cylinders on the arm. The pistons push out with huge forces to dig and lift.

Hydraulic: a trapped liquid, usually oil. Best for lifting, because liquids do not squeeze. Pneumatic: a trapped gas, usually compressed air. Useful for a springy, quick action.

Science 8 · F2 · Pascal’s law and hydraulics

F2.K6: Pascal’s law applies in technologies that use fluid to produce motion. For each card, ask: “Is the trapped fluid a liquid or a gas? What moves?” Brakes connect to compressibility: air bubbles in brake lines make brakes feel spongy, because air compresses. The excavator is a common sight on construction sites and farms across Alberta. Quiz items that fit: why car brakes use a liquid (liquids barely compress, so the push passes on fully) and what a compressed air jackhammer is (a pneumatic system). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6, “Pascal’s law in technology” panel. REVIEW FLAG (F2.K6): automotive brakes are taught as hydraulic. Hospital beds are taught as one example that can use a hydraulic lift (many modern beds are electric). Jackhammers are taught as usually pneumatic (compressed air), so they show a trapped gas moving a piston rather than a liquid.

LAB BENCH INVESTIGATION 4 · F2.S5 · F2.S2

Design challenge: the syringe lift

The rulesValue
Load to lift60 N
Your largest push20 N
Push distance5 cm
Box must riseat least 1 cm

Try this design

Push a 10 mL syringe (1.6 cm²). Lift with a 60 mL syringe (5.5 cm²). Fill the system with water. Plunger areas are approximate practice data.

Test it with water

P = 20 N ÷ 1.6 cm² = 12.5 N/cm²
F₂ = 12.5 N/cm² × 5.5 cm² = 68.75 N
Push volume = 1.6 cm² × 5 cm = 8 cm³
Rise = 8 cm³ ÷ 5.5 cm² ≈ 1.45 cm

68.75 N beats 60 N. 1.45 cm beats 1 cm. The box lifts.

Swap in air: it fails

The air squeezes instead of passing on the motion, so the box does not rise. Air compresses. Water does not.

Science 8 · F2 · Apply and review

Main Lab Bench investigation. Goal: lift a 60 N box of books using two syringes joined by a tube. You can push with at most 20 N and you push your plunger 5 cm. The box must rise at least 1 cm. Let groups try designs in the lab before showing the worked example. Choices: fluid (water, vegetable oil or air) and syringe sizes (5 mL 1.1 cm², 10 mL 1.6 cm², 20 mL 2.8 cm², 60 mL 5.5 cm²). Worked example: pressure at 20 N is 20 ÷ 1.6 = 12.5 N/cm²; largest output force 12.5 × 5.5 = 68.75 N, enough for 60 N; the 8 cm³ of liquid pushed lifts the 5.5 cm² piston about 1.45 cm. With air, the same syringes fail: the air squeezes instead of passing on the motion, so the lift feels spongy. Discussion: “Why not use the smallest push syringe and the biggest lift syringe every time?” (A bigger output piston gives more force but less lift height. Work in equals work out, ignoring friction.) Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 4 (syringe lift design challenge). REVIEW FLAG (examples): syringe volumes and plunger areas are simulated or approximate practice data. Each syringe starts with 20 mL of fluid in the system.

QUICK CHECK · F2.S2 · F2.S4 · F2.K5

Quick check

1

A box pushes down with 300 N on an area of 0.5 m². What is the pressure?

2

You seal and push two syringes. One is full of air. One is full of water. What happens, and why?

3

In a hydraulic lift, 20 N pushes a 2 cm² piston. What force acts on a 50 cm² piston?

Show your work. Write the formula, put in the numbers with units, then give the answer with units.

Science 8 · F2 · Apply and review

Give students three minutes, working alone, on mini whiteboards or exit slips. Answers: 1) P = F / A = 300 N ÷ 0.5 m² = 600 Pa. 2) The air syringe moves in a lot; the water syringe barely moves. Air is highly compressible because its particles have lots of space between them. Water is nearly incompressible because its particles are already close together. 3) P = 20 N ÷ 2 cm² = 10 N/cm². F = 10 N/cm² × 50 cm² = 500 N. Common errors: multiplying force by area in question 1 (gives 150, wrong), or dividing the areas the wrong way in question 3. All three come from the lab’s quiz bank. WRITTEN REVIEW · EXTENDED RESPONSE · F2.S1 · F2.S5

A student says, “A hydraulic lift gives you something for nothing, because a small force lifts a heavy car.”

Do you agree? Evaluate the claim. Use all four ideas in your answer.

Pressure

Force

Area

Distance

Science 8 · F2 · Apply and review

Run this as a quick four corners debate (strongly agree, agree, disagree, strongly disagree), then have students write their answer. Model answer from the lab: The claim is wrong. The lift does multiply force, because the same pressure acts on a larger area: F₂ = F₁ × A₂ / A₁. But the large piston moves a much shorter distance than the small piston. If the large piston has 20 times the area, it gives 20 times the force but moves only one twentieth of the distance. Work in equals work out (ignoring friction), so no energy is gained. You trade a longer push for a bigger force. Key ideas to tick: force is multiplied by the area ratio; same pressure throughout (Pascal’s law); large piston moves a shorter distance; work in equals work out; claim rejected with reasons. Other extended prompts in the lab: spongy brakes with air in the lines, and designing a syringe system to lift a toy.
