Part 1 · EN3.K1 and K2

What is a wave?

Energy on the move, and the parts we can measure.

Section opener. In this part students learn the definition of a wave and its five measurable properties: wavelength, frequency, amplitude, period and speed. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 and 2; Lab Bench, Investigation 1.

EN3.K1 · What a wave is

A wave carries energy, not matter

Amber arrow: energy moves toward the post. Red bead: it only moved up and back down.

A wave is a disturbance or oscillation that transfers energy from one point to another without transporting matter.

Each piece of rope moves up, then back to its resting place. The energy travels all the way to the post.

Science 8 · EN3 · What a wave is

Demo first if you can: tie a rope or skipping rope to a chair and flick a single pulse. Tape a piece of ribbon to one point. Students watch the ribbon go up and down, not along. Then show the Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 (Send a pulse, then Make a steady wave; the red bead never reaches the post). Return to the hook: the duck is answer B. Accuracy note for teachers (EN3.K1): without transporting matter is taught as no overall transport; particles oscillate and return. Breaking waves at a beach are the exception students will raise.

Science 8 · EN3 · What a wave is

Point to each label. Crest: highest point. Trough: lowest point. Rest position: where the medium sits with no wave. Wavelength: crest to the next crest (any point to the matching point). Amplitude: from the rest position up to a crest, NOT crest to trough. Common error: students measure amplitude from trough to crest, which is twice the amplitude. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (move the sliders and watch the labels follow).

Science 8 · EN3 · What a wave is

Waves repeat, so these five properties describe any steady wave. 1 Hz means one wave each second. Show the two relationships as tools students can look up; they are not asked to memorize them. Quick oral check: if 2 waves pass each second, what is the period? (0.5 s.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 shows the period and speed update live as the sliders move. EN3.S2 · Lab Bench, Investigation 1

Worked example: how fast is the lake wave?

Given

Crests are 4 m apart. A float bobs once every 2 s.

Step 1 · frequency

f = 1 ÷ T
= 1 ÷ 2 s
= 0.5 Hz

Step 2 · speed

v = f × λ
= 0.5 Hz × 4 m
= 2 m/s

Numbers are made up for practice. Your turn: a wave has f = 5 Hz and λ = 0.8 m. Find v and T.

Science 8 · EN3 · What a wave is

Work through the Sylvan Lake example (made-up numbers, same as the lab). Then students try the your turn problem on whiteboards. Answer: v = 5 Hz × 0.8 m = 4 m/s; T = 1 ÷ 5 Hz = 0.2 s. This is Written Review short answer 3 in the lab. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 1 (calculator plus randomized practice; answers within 2% are accepted). Units matter: Hz times m gives m/s.

Part 2 · EN3.K3 · Energy in waves

Double the amplitude, and the wave carries about 4 times the energy.

energy each second ∝ A² × f²

Same kind of wave, same material. Higher frequency adds energy too.

Accent statement. For the same wave type in the same medium, energy carried each second goes with amplitude squared and frequency squared: double A gives about 4 times, double f gives about 4 times. The ∝ sign means goes up with. This proportionality is context only; students do not need to memorize it. Accuracy flag (EN3.K3): the draft says higher frequency and amplitude transfer a greater amount of energy. The lab teaches it as energy carried each second, for the same wave type and medium. For light, frequency sets the energy of each packet and brightness depends on how many arrive (photons are not named). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (energy meter).

EN3.S1 · Energy in waves

A wave moves energy from one store to another

Store

Kinetic store of your moving hand

Transfer

The wave travels along the rope or water

Store

Kinetic and gravitational stores of the float as it rises

Swinging your hand farther (bigger amplitude) or more often (higher frequency) takes more energy from you, so more arrives at the far end.

Science 8 · EN3 · Energy in waves

Connect to EN1 energy stores from earlier in the unit. Ask: where does the energy end up when a boat’s wake reaches a dock? (Kinetic and gravitational stores of the dock and anything floating there; some is absorbed by the shore and warms things slightly.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (energy chain under the meter). This is Written Review extended response 1 in the lab.

EN3.S1 · Lab Bench, Investigation 2

Ripple tank: what makes a wave deliver more energy?

AmplitudeFrequencyEnergy (units)
1 cm1 Hz5.1
2 cm1 Hz19.8
3 cm1 Hz45.2
1 cm2 Hz20.3

Made-up practice data. Energy reaching the float in 10 s.

Worked analysis

1 cm to 2 cm: 19.8 ÷ 5.1 ≈ 3.9, about 4 times.

1 Hz to 2 Hz: 20.3 ÷ 5.1 ≈ 4.0, about 4 times.

Change one variable at a time. Run each setting twice.

Science 8 · EN3 · Energy in waves

This is the main Lab Bench investigation. Before showing the table, have students predict: if the amplitude doubles, what happens to the energy? (Choices in the lab: same, doubles, about 4 times, goes down.) Then reveal the made-up data and model the ratio calculation. Ask: why change only one variable at a time? (So we know which change caused the result: a fair test.) Why run trials twice? (Measurements wobble a little; the model adds about 4% random variation.) Extension: 3 cm gives about 9 times (45.2 ÷ 5.1). Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 2 (students run their own trials and the analysis box reports the ratios). Written Review extended response 2 asks students to design this investigation.

Part 3 · EN3.K4 and K5

Does it need a medium?

Mechanical waves, electromagnetic waves, and what the particles do.

Section opener. Define medium before moving on: the material a wave travels through (water for a lake wave, air for most sounds, rock for seismic waves). Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4 and 5.

EN3.K4 · EN3.S3 · Kinds of waves

Two kinds of waves

Mechanical

Need a medium. They travel by making particles of the medium move.

Sound · water waves · waves on a string · seismic waves

Electromagnetic

Do not need a medium. They can cross empty space, and can pass through some materials.

Radio · microwaves · visible light · X-rays

Science 8 · EN3 · Kinds of waves

Medium = the material a wave travels through. Seismic and water waves are mechanical; radio and microwaves are electromagnetic (the draft’s own examples). Accuracy flag (EN3.K4): do not require a medium is taught as can travel through empty space; EM waves can also travel through some materials, like light through glass. Quick sort aloud: ultrasound at the doctor (mechanical, it is sound), Wi-Fi (electromagnetic, radio waves). Lab link: Sort It tab, Mechanical or electromagnetic deck.

EN3.K4 · EN3.S3 · Kinds of waves

Evidence from the Sun

8 min

About how long sunlight takes to cross about 150 million km of nearly empty space to reach Edmonton.

Light arrives

Light is electromagnetic, so it needs no medium.

No sound arrives

Sound is mechanical. With almost no particles to push, it cannot cross space.

Science 8 · EN3 · Kinds of waves

Values are rounded: about 150 million km, about 8 minutes (closer to 8 minutes 20 seconds). Bell jar connection: a ringing bell in a jar gets quieter as air is pumped out, but the light inside stays just as bright. In a real demo some sound still leaks through the bell’s supports, and a pump never removes every particle. Ask: why can astronauts on a spacewalk not hear each other shout? (No air to carry sound; they use radios, which send electromagnetic waves.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (bell jar slider).

EN3.K5 · Kinds of waves

Particles oscillate while energy moves on

Across: transverse

Particles move up and down. Rope waves and S waves in rock.

Along: longitudinal

Particles squeeze together and spread apart. Sound and P waves.

Red: marked particles and how far they move. Amber arrow: the direction the wave and its energy travel.

Science 8 · EN3 · Kinds of waves

EN3.K5: mechanical waves oscillate the particles of a medium while transferring energy in the direction the wave travels. Each particle is pushed by its neighbour, moves, and passes the push along. Demo with a slinky on the floor: shake side to side (transverse), then push and pull along its length (longitudinal). Tie a ribbon to one coil. Note for teachers: transverse and longitudinal are not named in the draft; they are added as brief context for the two directions particles can oscillate. P waves are faster than S waves; no speeds are given. Seismometers in Alberta record both. Earthquakes can be a sensitive topic for students with family in affected regions. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5.

Meeting a new medium

What happens when a wave reaches water, glass, a mirror or a wall?

Section opener. Ask students to list places they have seen a wave hit something new: light on a lake, sound off a gym wall, sunlight through a window. Keep the list for the next slide. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 6, 7 and 8; Lab Bench, Investigations 3 and 4.

Science 8 · EN3 · Meeting a new medium

Go through each behaviour with its example. Accuracy flag (EN3.K6): dispersed is ambiguous in the draft. The lab teaches dispersion as a mix of frequencies spreading apart because each travels at a slightly different speed in the new medium (prism, rainbow). Scattering from rough surfaces is mentioned only under reflection. Violet light bends most and red least in a prism. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (boundary explorer); Sort It tab, Which behaviour deck.

EN3.S4 · Meeting a new medium

Most of the time, several happen at once

SituationWhat the wave does
Sunlight hits a windowMostly transmitted. A little reflected and absorbed.
Light enters a lakeRefracted (bends toward the normal). Some reflected as glare.
Sound hits thick curtainsMostly absorbed. A little reflected and transmitted.
Sound hits a rock cliffMostly reflected: you hear an echo.
Lake waves reach shallow waterSlow down and refract, turning toward the beach.
Seismic waves reach a new rock layerPartly refracted, partly reflected, some absorbed.

Science 8 · EN3 · Meeting a new medium

Interpret, do not just name. Ask: how do you know some light reflects from a window? (You see your reflection at night, when it is dark outside.) Why does a band room have soft panels? (They absorb sound so it does not echo.) Scientists use reflected seismic waves to map rock layers underground. An echo sounder uses reflected sound to measure lake depth: depth = speed × time ÷ 2, with sound about 1480 m/s in water (rounded reference value). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (nine situations); Lab Bench, Investigation 4 (echo sounder).

EN3.K7 · EN3.S5 · Meeting a new medium

The law of reflection

i = 40°

r = 40°

normal

mirror

i = r

The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.

Measure both from the normal, a line at 90° to the surface, not from the mirror.

Science 8 · EN3 · Meeting a new medium

Model drawing it: draw the mirror, draw the normal with a protractor where the ray hits, measure i from the normal, then measure the same angle on the other side of the normal. Accuracy flag (EN3.K7): angles are taught as measured from the normal. Rough surfaces, like paper, scatter light in many directions, but each tiny piece of the surface still follows the law. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7 (move the slider or focus the red light source and use arrow keys).

EN3.S5 · Lab Bench, Investigation 3

Watch for the surface trap

Problem

A ray hits a mirror 25° from the surface. What is the angle of reflection?

Step 1 · use the normal

i = 90° − 25°
= 65°

Step 2 · apply the law

r = i
= 65°

Your turn: a ray hits a mirror 30° from the surface. Find i and r.

Science 8 · EN3 · Meeting a new medium

The most common error is answering 25°, the angle from the surface. Your turn answer: i = 90° − 30° = 60°, so r = 60°. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 3 (mirror protractor). About 45% of its randomized rays give the angle from the surface; a wrong answer that matches the surface angle gets a specific hint, and the reflected ray is drawn after each check.

EN3.K8 · Meeting a new medium

Refraction: a change in speed bends the wave

Air · about 300 000 km/s

Water · about 225 000 km/s

normal

Slows down: bends toward the normal.

Speeds up: bends away from the normal.

Straight on: speed changes, no bending.

Science 8 · EN3 · Meeting a new medium

Analogy: a wagon rolling at an angle from a sidewalk onto grass. The wheel that reaches the grass first slows first, so the wagon turns. Light in glass is about 200 000 km/s, so glass bends light more than water does. Real-world links: a straw looks broken in water; lake waves slow in shallow water and turn toward the beach. Accuracy flag (EN3.K8): refraction is taught as bending when a wave enters a new medium at an angle; a wave entering along the normal changes speed without bending. The lab model uses indices of refraction (air 1.00, water 1.33, glass 1.50) to draw rays, but students only see speeds and the toward or away rule. Speeds are rounded. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 8.

Quick check

Three questions

1

A wave has a frequency of 4 Hz. What is its period?

2

Why can astronauts on a spacewalk not hear each other shout?

3

Light passes at an angle from air into water and slows down. Which way does it bend?

Science 8 · EN3 · Quick check

Answers. 1: T = 1 ÷ f = 1 ÷ 4 Hz = 0.25 s. 2: There is almost no air in space, and sound is a mechanical wave that needs a medium. 3: Toward the normal, because it slows down. All three are from the lab’s Quiz bank. Lab link: Quiz tab (10 questions drawn from 24, with explanations).

Extended response · Debate

“Light and sound are both waves, so they must travel the same way.” Do you agree?

Use what you know about mechanical and electromagnetic waves, and about how waves behave at a new medium. Give at least one similarity and one difference.

Science 8 · EN3 · Extended response

Four corners or a quick pair-share, then write. A strong answer partly agrees: both transfer energy, have wavelengths and frequencies, and can be reflected, refracted and absorbed (both echo or bounce). But sound is mechanical and needs a medium, so it cannot cross space, while light is electromagnetic and can travel from the Sun. Speeds differ hugely: sound about 343 m/s in air, light about 300 000 km each second. Key ideas checklist: clear position; similarities in energy and properties; both reflect, refract and absorb; difference in medium; evidence such as speed or sunlight.
