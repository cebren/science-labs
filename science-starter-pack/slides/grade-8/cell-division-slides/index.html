DNA and Cell Division

Cell division is essential for survival and diversity.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Cell Division Lab

Welcome students to the second cells understanding. Read the statement aloud: cell division is essential for survival and diversity. Ask what “survival” and “diversity” might mean for a living thing, and collect a few ideas without correcting them yet. Tell students they will use the Cell Division Lab alongside these slides. Science 8 · LS2 · Opening

Give students two minutes to write a prediction for each question, then share with a partner. Section 1

DNA

What it is, where cells keep it, and why your body cells share it.

This section covers LS2.K1 to K3 and the skill S1. It matches Lessons 1 to 3 on the Learn tab and Investigation 1 (DNA locator) on the Lab Bench tab.

LS2.K1

DNA carries the cell’s instructions

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is a very long molecule that carries the cell’s genetic information. Zoom in from a whole cell:

1

Cell

In plant and animal cells, DNA sits in the nucleus.

2

Nucleus

Long, thin threads of DNA spread out like loose yarn.

3

Chromosome

DNA wound tightly into a package before the cell divides.

4

DNA

A twisted ladder written in four chemical letters.

5

Gene

A section of DNA with instructions for one job.

Why it matters: the cell reads its genes to build the proteins it needs to live. Without DNA, a cell cannot survive for long.

Science 8 · LS2 · DNA

Use the instruction book comparison: DNA is the whole book, chromosomes are the volumes, genes are the recipes. Ask: if a cell lost its DNA, what could it no longer do? (Make new proteins, repair itself, divide.) Accuracy flag (LS2.K1): the draft says DNA “is the genetic information”; the lab teaches it more precisely as the molecule that carries the genetic information, coded in genes, and links the instructions to the survival of the whole organism as well as the cell. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 (zoom into a cell).

LS2.K1

The twisted ladder

A T G C

Four chemical letters. A pairs with T and G pairs with C, so DNA can be copied exactly.

46

Chromosomes in most human body cells, in 23 pairs.

≈20 000

Genes in human DNA, each a section of the ladder.

≈2 m

Length of the DNA in one human cell if it were stretched out.

Science 8 · LS2 · DNA

Point out the shape: two strands twist around each other, joined by rungs. This shape is called a double helix. History in one line: in 1953, Rosalind Franklin’s X-ray images helped James Watson and Francis Crick work out this shape (context only, not a curriculum statement; please check against your resource). Ask: how could 2 metres of DNA fit inside a cell too small to see? (It is extremely thin and wound tightly into chromosomes.) The numbers are rounded reference values. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1, pick the DNA and Gene levels.

LS2.K2 · LS2.S1

Where do cells keep their DNA?

CellNucleus?Where the DNA is
Animal cellYesIn the nucleus, plus a little in each mitochondrion
Plant cellYesIn the nucleus, plus a little in mitochondria and chloroplasts
Bacterial cellNoIn the cytoplasm: one large loop, often with small rings called plasmids

The rule: no nucleus means the DNA stays in the cytoplasm. Almost all of a plant or animal cell’s instructions are in its nucleus.

Science 8 · LS2 · DNA

Have students sketch the three cells and mark the DNA in red. Ask: a yeast cell is unicellular. Where is its DNA? (In a nucleus. Being one cell does not mean having no nucleus.) Accuracy flag (LS2.K2): the draft says bacteria “may” store DNA in the cytoplasm; the lab teaches that bacteria always keep their DNA in the cytoplasm because they have no nucleus, and notes that mitochondria and chloroplasts hold a small amount of their own DNA. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (find the DNA), then Lab Bench, Investigation 1 (DNA locator).

LS2.K3

Same DNA, different genes in use

You started as one cell. Every division copied the DNA, so your body cells all hold the same DNA. Each kind of cell switches on different genes.

Body cellSame DNA?Genes it uses most
Skin cellYes, full setKeratin (a tough protein) and skin pigment
Muscle cellYes, full setMuscle proteins that pull and shorten
Nerve cellYes, full setProteins for sending electrical signals
Stomach cellYes, full setProteins that break down food

Science 8 · LS2 · DNA

Comparison: every cell has the whole cookbook, but a muscle cell only opens the muscle recipes. Ask: a muscle cell has the keratin gene. Why does it not grow tough skin protein? (That gene is switched off in muscle cells.) Accuracy flag (LS2.K3): the draft says cells in an organism “contain the same DNA”; the lab teaches almost all body cells, with the exceptions on the next slide. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (gene switchboard), pick each body cell.

LS2.K3

Three exceptions to the rule

Half

Sex cells

Sperm and egg cells carry only one chromosome from each pair: 23 instead of 46 in humans.

None

Mature red blood cells

They push out their nucleus to make room for more hemoglobin, so they have no DNA and cannot divide.

Rare

Copying errors

Once in a while, a mistake when DNA is copied changes one cell’s DNA a little.

So: almost every body cell holds the same DNA.

Science 8 · LS2 · DNA

These exceptions make the statement precise. Ask: why might it help a red blood cell to have no nucleus? (More room for hemoglobin, which carries oxygen.) Mature red blood cells last about four months and are replaced by new ones made in bone marrow. Accuracy flag (LS2.K3): taught as almost all body cells sharing the same DNA, with sex cells (half), mature red blood cells (none) and rare copying errors named. Sensitive topic note: sperm and egg cells are named at the cell level only. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3, pick Mature red blood cell and Sex cell (press Sex cell again to see a new mix).

Section 2

Dividing cells

Why cells divide, how mitosis copies them, and how meiosis mixes them up.

This section covers LS2.K4 to K6. It matches Lessons 4 to 6 on the Learn tab. Return to the hook: the first question (one cell to trillions) is answered by mitosis, and the second (why siblings differ) by meiosis.

LS2.K4

Four jobs of cell division

One parent cell splits to make new daughter cells.

Growth

A white spruce seedling grows taller as cells at its tips divide.

Repair

New skin cells fill in a scraped knee.

Reproduction

A yeast cell in bread dough divides into two yeast cells. Trembling aspen sprout new trees from roots.

Variation

Calves from the same parents look different because their sex cells shuffled DNA.

Bacteria split in two by a simpler process called binary fission: they copy their DNA loop and pinch in half.

Science 8 · LS2 · Dividing cells

Ask students to think of one more Alberta example for each job (a canola seedling growing, a broken bone mending, bacteria in yogurt, puppies in a litter). Point out that one example can do two jobs: aspen root sprouts are growth and asexual reproduction. Accuracy flag (LS2.K4): variation is taught as coming from meiosis together with fertilization; reproduction includes asexual examples (yeast, aspen); bacteria divide by binary fission, not mitosis. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (what is the division for?), and the Sort It deck “Why is the cell dividing?”.

LS2.K5

Mitosis: copy and split

1 · Copy

Every chromosome is copied. Each now has two identical halves.

2 · Line up

The chromosomes line up single file across the middle.

3 · Pull apart

The two halves of each chromosome move to opposite ends.

4 · Split

The cell pinches in two. Each new cell has a full set.

Mitosis makes 2 identical daughter cells for growth and repair.

Science 8 · LS2 · Dividing cells

Walk through the four steps, then have students act them out: four students are chromosomes, each holding hands with a “copy” partner, line up across the room, then split to opposite walls. Orange chromosomes came from parent 1 and teal from parent 2. Scientists name the stages interphase, prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase; students do not need to memorize these. Accuracy flag (LS2.K5): strictly, mitosis divides the nucleus and then the cytoplasm splits (cytokinesis); the lab treats them together as the draft does. Daughter cells are identical apart from rare copying errors. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (mitosis step by step).

LS2.K5 · Mitosis in numbers

46

→

46 + 46

One human body cell becomes two identical cells with the full set.

Copy it, then split it: 1 cell → 2 → 4 → 8. After 10 rounds, one cell becomes 1024.

Science 8 · LS2 · Dividing cells

Use this slide to answer the first hook question: one cell became trillions through many rounds of mitosis, and each round copied the DNA, so the cells share it. Show the doubling formula from the lab: cells after n rounds = starting cells × 2 to the power n. Students are not asked to memorize it. Ask: how many cells after 5 rounds? (32.) Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 4 (slide the rounds of mitosis, then try the practice questions).

LS2.K6

Meiosis: copy, shuffle, split twice

1 · Copy

Every chromosome is copied, just like in mitosis.

2 · Pair and swap

Matching pairs line up side by side and trade pieces: crossing over.

3 · First split

The pairs separate. Which side each goes to is random.

4 · Second split

The copies separate. Four cells, each with half the chromosomes.

Meiosis makes 4 non-identical daughter cells used in sexual reproduction.

Science 8 · LS2 · Dividing cells

Compare each step with mitosis: step 1 is the same; step 2 is new (pairs and crossing over); there are two splits instead of one. Ask students to find the swapped tips in step 2 and follow them into the final cells. Meiosis happens only in reproductive organs: in animals it makes sperm and egg cells, and in flowering plants the sex cells form inside pollen and ovules. Accuracy flag (LS2.K6): the four daughter cells are taught as having half the chromosomes; in human females meiosis gives one egg cell and three small cells that break down, and the lab keeps the general four-cell model without detailing this. Sensitive topic note: sperm and egg are named at the cell level only. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (meiosis step by step; press Shuffle to rerun with a new mix).

LS2.K4 · LS2.K6 · Where variation comes from

Every sex cell gets a different mix

Crossing over

Matching chromosomes trade pieces, making new combinations of genes.

Random line-up

Which chromosome of each pair goes to which cell is random.

Fertilization

A sperm and egg join. The fertilized egg gets a full set and a new combination.

23 + 23 = 46

Science 8 · LS2 · Dividing cells

This answers the second hook question: siblings differ because each came from a different sperm and egg, each with its own mix of DNA. Variation matters for survival of a species: when conditions change, some individuals may be better suited. Discussion prompt: why might a canola grower care about variation in seeds? Accuracy flag (LS2.K4): variation is taught as coming from meiosis (crossing over and random line-up) together with fertilization. Sensitive topic note: fertilization is described at the cell level only; check your school’s parental notice rules for human sexuality content. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (Shuffle) and Lesson 7 (chromosome counter).

Lab Bench · LS2.S2 · LS2.K5

Investigation: the onion root tip count

Root tips grow fast, so many cells are in mitosis. Count the cells on a stained slide and find the percent that are dividing.

percent dividing = (dividing cells ÷ total cells) × 100

Clue: a dividing cell shows dark chromosomes instead of a round nucleus.

Worked example

A slide has 40 cells. 8 are dividing.

8 ÷ 40 = 0.2
0.2 × 100 = 20%

20% of the cells are dividing.

Made-up practice data.

Science 8 · LS2 · Compare and investigate

Model the worked example, then send students to the lab. Each new slide has 40 cells with a random number dividing (6 to 14). Students tap the cells they think are dividing, press Check, then calculate the percent using the real count. Extension: if 10 of 40 are dividing, 25%. Ask why a growing root tip would have more dividing cells than a mature leaf. The slides in the lab are drawings with randomized practice data, not real micrographs. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 3 (onion root tip count); Investigation 2 (name that division) gives more practice identifying division from a representation.

LS2.S2

Name that division

Diagram A

Parent: 6 chromosomes. Daughter cells: 6 each.

Diagram B

Parent: 6 chromosomes. Daughter cells: 3 each.

Your turn: which diagram shows mitosis and which shows meiosis? Give two clues for each.

Science 8 · LS2 · Compare and investigate

Answers: Diagram A is mitosis (2 daughter cells; each has all 6 chromosomes; matching colours, so identical). Diagram B is meiosis (4 daughter cells; each has 3, half of 6; different colour mixes, so not identical; one cell shows a crossed-over tip). Representations can also be descriptions: “cells in a canola flower’s anthers make pollen” is meiosis; “a cut on your finger heals” is mitosis. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 2 (name that division) gives randomized diagrams and descriptions.

Quick check

Three questions

1

An E. coli bacterium has no nucleus. Where is its DNA kept?

2

A skin cell and a nerve cell from the same person: do they have the same DNA? Why do they look different?

3

A bread wheat body cell has 42 chromosomes. How many are in each cell made by mitosis, and in each sex cell made by meiosis?

Science 8 · LS2 · Check and wrap-up

Answers. 1: in the cytoplasm, as one large loop (often with small plasmids), because bacteria have no nucleus. 2: yes, the same DNA, because both came from one fertilized egg by mitosis; they look different because each uses different genes. 3: mitosis gives 42 in each daughter cell; meiosis gives 42 ÷ 2 = 21 in each sex cell. Use mini whiteboards or fingers for question 3. Lab link: Quiz tab (10 questions drawn from a bank of 26) for more practice.

Extended response · LS2.K4 · LS2.S3

Roots or seeds: how should an aspen grove grow?

Trembling aspen can sprout new trees from their roots, and can also grow from seeds made in flowers.

Root sprouts

Which type of cell division? How are the new trees related to the parent?

Seeds

Which type of cell division makes the sex cells? How are the seedlings different?

Decide: which way gives the grove a better chance if a new disease arrives? Explain.

Science 8 · LS2 · Check and wrap-up

Model answer: root sprouts grow by mitosis, so the new trees have the same DNA as the parent; a whole grove can share one set of DNA. Seeds come from sex cells made by meiosis in the flowers, joined by fertilization, so seed-grown trees have new mixes of DNA from two parents and differ from each other. Sprouting is fast and keeps a set of DNA that already works; seeds add variation, which can help some trees survive a new disease or a changing climate. Key ideas to look for: mitosis for sprouts, identical offspring, meiosis and fertilization for seeds, variation, an advantage of each. Run it as a four-corners debate before writing.
