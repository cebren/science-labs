Cells

Cells support life in organisms of varying complexity.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Cell Explorer Lab

Welcome students to the cells unit. Read the understanding statement aloud: cells support life in organisms of varying complexity. Ask: what is the smallest living thing you can think of? Section 1

Cells and microscopes

What a cell is, how big cells are, and how microscopes revealed them.

This section covers LS1.K1, K2 and K3 and the skills S1 and S2. It matches Lessons 1 to 3 on the Learn tab and Investigations 1 and 2 on the Lab Bench tab.

LS1.K1 · LS1.K4

The cell is the basic unit of life

A cell is the smallest unit that can carry out all the processes of life: take in materials, release energy, grow, respond and make more cells.

ONE CELL

Unicellular

The whole organism is one cell. A bacterium or a yeast cell is a complete living thing.

MANY CELLS

Multicellular

Many cells share the work. A spruce tree, a moose and you are all multicellular.

Science 8 · LS1 · Cells and microscopes

Ask: is a yeast cell alive? Is one of your skin cells alive? Both are, because each cell carries out life processes. The difference is that the yeast cell is a whole organism, while your skin cell depends on the rest of your body. Note for discussion: organelles (Lesson 4) are found in the cells of both unicellular and multicellular organisms (LS1.K4). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

Sizes are rounded typical values from the lab; real cells vary. Ask: which of these could you see without a microscope? Only the human egg cell, just barely, at about 0.1 mm. The nerve cell is very long but very thin, so you still could not see one cell by eye. Discussion prompt: why would a red blood cell shaped like a long box be a bad design? LS1.K2

Microscopes revealed cells

1665

Robert Hooke sees boxes in cork and names them cells.

1670s

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek sees living bacteria and protists.

1838 to 1839

Schleiden and Schwann: plants and animals are made of cells.

1850s

Remak and Virchow: new cells come from existing cells.

1931

Ruska and Knoll build the electron microscope.

1938

Hillier and Prebus in Toronto build North America’s first practical electron microscope.

Today, glowing dyes and computers let scientists watch living cells in 3D.

Science 8 · LS1 · Cells and microscopes

Return to the hook predictions: cells stayed hidden until lenses were good enough. Each better microscope let scientists see smaller things. Hooke was seeing the empty walls of dead cork cells. Van Leeuwenhoek’s single-lens microscopes magnified more than 200 times. The Toronto team worked with Professor E. F. Burton at the University of Toronto, a Canadian connection worth pointing out. Accuracy flag (LS1.K2): timeline dates are well known but should be checked against your resource; Remak is named so Virchow is not credited alone. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (microscope timeline).

The cell theory

1. All living things are made of one or more cells.

2. The cell is the basic unit of life.

3. New cells come from existing cells.

Science 8 · LS1 · Cells and microscopes

The cell theory is added as context for LS1.K2; it shows what microscopes made possible. Ask students to match each statement to a timeline event: statement 1 to Schleiden and Schwann, statement 2 to the idea that a single cell carries out all life processes, statement 3 to Remak and Virchow. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2.

LS1.K3 · LS1.S1

The compound light microscope

Eyepiece: the lens you look through, usually 10×.

Objective lenses: just above the slide, often 4×, 10× and 40×.

Stage and clips: hold the slide over a hole for light.

Diaphragm: controls how much light reaches the slide.

Coarse focus: big moves. Use it only on low power.

Fine focus: small moves. The only knob on high power.

Safe steps: carry with two hands, start on low power, center the cells, then switch up.

Science 8 · LS1 · Cells and microscopes

Demonstrate with a real microscope if you have one. Explain why coarse focus is unsafe on high power: the 40× objective sits very close to the slide and can crash into it. Mention that a stain such as iodine or methylene blue makes cell parts easier to see. Accuracy flag (LS1.K3): lens values are typical and vary between microscopes. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (clickable microscope), then Lab Bench, Investigation 1 (virtual microscope with focus, stain and the coarse focus warning).

LS1.K3 · LS1.S2

Total magnification is a product

total magnification = eyepiece × objective

40×

10 × 4 · low power

100×

10 × 10 · medium power

400×

10 × 40 · high power

At 400×, a cell looks 400 times wider than it really is. You do not need to memorize the formula.

Science 8 · LS1 · Cells and microscopes

Practice: a 15× eyepiece with a 10× objective gives 150×. Reverse problem: total 100× with a 10× eyepiece means a 10× objective. Extension from the lab: estimate cell size by dividing the field of view by the number of cells across it (typical fields of view are about 4.5 mm at 40×, 1.8 mm at 100× and 0.45 mm at 400×; these vary by microscope). Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 2 (calculator and randomized practice).

Section 2

Inside the cell

Organelles, plant and animal cells, bacteria, and microbes that affect health.

This section covers LS1.K4 to K8 and the skills S3 to S6. It matches Lessons 4 to 7 on the Learn tab and Investigations 3 and 6 on the Lab Bench tab.

LS1.K4 · LS1.K5 · LS1.S5

Each organelle has a job

Cell membrane

Thin outer layer. Controls what enters and leaves.

Nucleus

Holds the genetic instructions. Controls the cell.

Cytoplasm

Jelly-like fluid that fills the cell and holds organelles.

Mitochondria

Release energy from food by cellular respiration.

Ribosomes

Tiny structures that build proteins.

Vacuoles

Sacs that store water, food and wastes.

All six are found in both plant and animal cells, in unicellular and multicellular organisms.

Science 8 · LS1 · Inside the cell

Use the factory comparison: the nucleus is the office with the plans, mitochondria are the power plant, ribosomes are the assembly line, the membrane is the security gate, vacuoles are storage rooms and cytoplasm is the factory floor. Ask students to invent their own comparison. Accuracy flag (LS1.K5): cytoplasm is taught as the fluid that holds the organelles, since it is not an organelle in the strict sense; ribosomes are tiny structures without a membrane; animal cells have small vacuoles, while plant cells usually have one large central vacuole. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (tap each organelle on the animal cell).

The wall supports the cell. Chloroplasts use light energy to make sugar by photosynthesis.

Science 8 · LS1 · Inside the cell

Ask: why does an onion bulb cell have no chloroplasts? It grows underground with no light, like a root cell. Ask: why can a full central vacuole keep a plant upright? It pushes out against the wall. Accuracy flag (LS1.K6): “only in plant cells” is taught as in plant cells but not animal cells; fungi and bacteria also have cell walls, algae also have chloroplasts, and plant cells that get no light have no chloroplasts. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (plant and animal toggle), then Lab Bench, Investigation 3 (label a random cell).

LS1.K7

Bacteria have no nucleus

Bacterial DNA sits in a loop in the cytoplasm. There is no nucleus, and no mitochondria or chloroplasts.

Bacteria still have a membrane, cytoplasm and ribosomes. Most have a cell wall. Some swim with a flagellum.

A bacterium is about 1 to 2 µm long. About 30 fit across one cheek cell.

Red loop: DNA. Dark dots: ribosomes. Teal: membrane. Tan: cell wall. Tail: flagellum.

Science 8 · LS1 · Inside the cell

Ask: if a bacterium has no nucleus, where are its genetic instructions? In a loop in the cytoplasm (this links to LS2). Point out that yeasts, amoebas and other protists are unicellular too, but they do have a nucleus. Challenge question: a mature human red blood cell also has no nucleus. It loses it as it matures to make room for carrying oxygen, but it is part of a multicellular organism. Accuracy flag (LS1.K7): “some unicellular organisms, including bacteria” is taught as all bacteria having no nucleus. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (bacterial cell), and Sort It, nucleus or no nucleus deck.

LS1.K8 · LS1.S6

Tiny organisms, big effects

BACTERIUM · HUMANS

Strep bacteria

Infect the throat: strep throat and fever.

PROTIST · PEOPLE, ANIMALS

Giardia

Untreated lake water. Harms the intestine.

FUNGUS · HUMANS

Candida yeast

Usually harmless. Overgrowth causes thrush.

PROTIST-LIKE · CANOLA

Clubroot

Roots swell into clubs. The plant wilts.

PROTIST · HUMANS

Plasmodium

Malaria. Spread by mosquitoes in warm regions.

HELPFUL BACTERIUM

Rhizobium

Helps peas and lentils get nitrogen.

Most microbes are harmless, and many are helpful.

Science 8 · LS1 · Inside the cell

Sensitive topic: some students may have personal or family experience with these illnesses. Keep the focus on how the microbe affects cells and organs, and remind students this is science, not medical advice. Alberta connections: boil, filter or treat backcountry water to avoid Giardia; clubroot was found in Alberta canola fields in the early 2000s and spreads in soil on equipment; many Alberta farmers coat pulse seeds with Rhizobium. Accuracy flag (LS1.K8): fungi are taught through unicellular yeasts, since most fungi are multicellular; the clubroot organism is described as protist-like; Alberta details should be checked. Section 3

Keeping the inside steady

Four processes and a semi-permeable membrane keep a cell alive.

This section covers LS1.K9 and K10 and the skills S7 and S8. It matches Lessons 8 and 9 on the Learn tab and Investigations 4, 5 and 7 on the Lab Bench tab.

LS1.K9 · LS1.S8

Four processes keep a cell alive

Cellular respiration

Provides energy. Happens in the mitochondria.

Diffusion

Moves vital substances, such as oxygen in and carbon dioxide out.

Osmosis

Moves water into or out of the cell.

Protein formation

Ribosomes build proteins that do specific jobs.

glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy

Science 8 · LS1 · Keeping the inside steady

Explain that “regulate the internal environment” means keeping conditions inside the cell steady even when the outside changes. Walk through the word equation for cellular respiration. For protein formation, instructions from the nucleus travel to a ribosome, which joins building blocks into a chain that folds into a protein; DNA itself comes in LS2. Quick check: a yeast cell in bread dough gives off carbon dioxide. Which process? Cellular respiration. Accuracy flag (LS1.K9): respiration taught as a word equation in the mitochondria; protein formation taught at the ribosomes using instructions from the nucleus. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 8 (cell at work), then Lab Bench, Investigation 7 (process detective).

LS1.K10 · LS1.S7

A semi-permeable membrane

Blue: water passes the gaps. Orange: sugar is too big. Water moves toward the sugar side.

Diffusion

Particles move from more concentrated to less concentrated until they are spread evenly.

Osmosis

Diffusion of water across a semi-permeable membrane, toward the side with more dissolved particles.

Science 8 · LS1 · Keeping the inside steady

Semi-permeable means some particles pass and others do not; it is also called selectively permeable. Small particles such as water, oxygen and carbon dioxide pass easily; large ones such as starch or sugar do not. Run the particle tank in the lab twice: first diffusion with oxygen on the left, then osmosis with sugar on the right, and have students record the counts on each side. Accuracy flag (LS1.K10): the gap model is a simple picture; real membranes also move particles through special proteins. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 9 (particles at a membrane).

Have students predict each row before you reveal it. Real-world links: a wilted lettuce leaf crisps up in fresh water; salt sprinkled on a slug draws water out. Lab Bench · Investigation 5 · LS1.S7

Potato core osmosis

Manipulated: salt concentration. Responding: percent change in mass. Controlled: core size, potato, time, temperature.

% change = (final − start) ÷ start × 100

Worked example

Start 2.50 g, final 2.80 g.

(2.80 − 2.50) ÷ 2.50 × 100 = +12%. The core gained water.

Science 8 · LS1 · Keeping the inside steady

Answers for the practice table: 0% salt, (2.70 − 2.40) ÷ 2.40 × 100 = +12.5%; 1% salt, (2.18 − 2.20) ÷ 2.20 × 100 = −0.9%; 3% salt, (2.29 − 2.60) ÷ 2.60 × 100 = −11.9%; 5% salt, (2.05 − 2.50) ÷ 2.50 × 100 = −18.0%. The data are made up for practice, as are the lab’s simulated trials. Explain: in weak solutions water moves into the potato cells by osmosis; in strong solutions water moves out. If you run the real experiment, use the same potato and pat cores dry the same way each time. Quick check

Three questions

1. An eyepiece is 10× and the objective is 40×. What is the total magnification?

2. Name two parts found in plant cells but not in animal cells.

3. A potato core sits in very salty water. Does its mass go up or down? Why?

Science 8 · LS1 · Check and wrap-up

Answers: 1. 10 × 40 = 400×. 2. The cell wall and chloroplasts. 3. Down, because water leaves the potato cells by osmosis toward the side with more dissolved particles. Debate · Extended response

A classmate says, “All microbes are dangerous, so we should get rid of them.” Do you agree?

Use at least two microbes and their effects on other organisms as evidence.

Science 8 · LS1 · Check and wrap-up

Model answer from the lab: disagree. Some microbes cause disease, such as strep bacteria (strep throat) and clubroot (damages canola roots). But most microbes are harmless and many are helpful: Rhizobium helps peas and lentils get nitrogen, gut bacteria help us digest food and yeast makes bread rise. Getting rid of all microbes would harm people, crops and ecosystems; prevention through safe water, hand washing and good farming practices is better. Key ideas: a clear position, a harmful example with its effect, a helpful example with its effect, prevention instead of removal. Run it as a four corners debate (strongly agree to strongly disagree). Sensitive topic: keep illness examples general. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 2.
