Resource extraction

Geological resource extraction methods change Earth’s surface.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Resource Extraction Lab

Welcome students and introduce the unit thread: we already studied fossils and Earth’s history (ES1). Now we ask how that history left resources in the ground, and what happens when we take them out. Part 1 · ES2.K1 and ES2.K2

What resources are, and why they are where they are

Four tests for a resource, then a trip down through Alberta’s layers.

Section opener. Ask: name something you think is a geological resource. Keep the list for the next slide. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 and 2.

ES2.K1 · Definition

A geological resource passes four tests

1

Natural

Formed by Earth’s processes, not made by people.

2

On or below the surface

Part of Earth’s crust: rock, sediment, fluids.

3

Economic value

People will pay for it or for what is made from it.

4

Can be extracted

People can reach it and take it out for use.

Examples: fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas), metal ores (copper, iron ore), industrial minerals and rock (gravel, limestone, salt).

Science 8 · ES2 · What resources are

Teach the definition from ES2.K1: naturally occurring materials on or below Earth’s surface that have economic value and can be extracted for human use. Check the students’ list from the opener against the four tests. Point out that “worth extracting” can change: a rise in price or new technology can turn a deposit into a resource. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

ES2.K2 · Uneven distribution

Resources form only where past processes made them

Alberta has plenty

Ancient seas, swamps and glaciers left oil, natural gas, oil sands, coal, salt, limestone and gravel in flat sedimentary layers.

Alberta has few

Metal ores like copper form from hot fluids in cracked rock near old mountains or volcanoes. Most come from British Columbia, Quebec and Labrador.

Science 8 · ES2 · What resources are

Key idea: geological resources are unevenly distributed because of past geological processes. Ask: why does Saskatchewan have potash but Alberta has more oil sands? (Different ancient seas and sediments.) The far northeast corner of Alberta is part of the Canadian Shield and is mostly unexplored for metals. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 1 (Alberta resource atlas): students open each resource and read how it formed.

Part 3 · ES2.K4, ES2.K5 and ES2.S2

Finding and extracting

How geologists locate a deposit, and how mines change Earth’s surface.

Section opener. Ask: if you were looking for buried gravel or copper, what clues would you use? Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4 and 5, then Lab Bench, Investigations 2 and 3.

ES2.K5 · Exploration

Start wide and cheap, end narrow and costly

Step 1

Surface survey

Map rock outcrops, collect rock and soil samples, look for clues.

Step 2

Geophysical survey

Measure magnetism or gravity, or record sound echoes (seismic).

Step 3

Core drilling

A hollow bit cuts a cylinder of rock showing every layer and its depth.

Step 4

Lab analysis

Crushed core is tested for grade, the percentage of useful resource.

Only a few explored places ever become mines.

Science 8 · ES2 · Finding and extracting

ES2.K5 names surveying Earth’s surface and core sample drilling; geophysical surveys and lab analysis are added as context. Note that exploration itself changes the surface: seismic lines through forests leave long cleared strips. Discussion: why not just drill everywhere? (Drilling is expensive.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (exploration steps with core samples).

ES2.K4 · Changing the surface

Depth decides the kind of mine

Surface mine

Removes the overburden over a shallow deposit. Clears a wide area and leaves pits and waste piles until reclaimed.

Usually: gravel, Alberta coal, shallow oil sands, limestone, big shallow copper and iron deposits.

Underground mine

Shafts and tunnels reach a deep deposit. Less land is disturbed, but it costs more and can leave waste piles and sinking ground.

Usually: deep copper, iron and gold deposits, and some historic Alberta coal.

Science 8 · ES2 · Finding and extracting

Define overburden: the soil and rock on top of a deposit. Ask: which kind of mine changes more of the surface? (Surface mine.) Accuracy flag (ES2.K4): the draft pairs coal and gravel with surface mines and copper and iron with underground mines. This is taught more precisely: the method depends mainly on depth and shape. Copper and iron come from both, and many of the largest copper and iron mines (for example in British Columbia and the Labrador Trough) are open pits. Alberta also has a history of underground coal mining. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (depth slider: watch the pit turn into a shaft as the deposit gets deeper).

ES2.K4 · Not every resource is dug

Wells, steam and water

Wells

Oil and natural gas flow up drilled wells from porous rock deep below.

In situ

“In place.” Steam heats deep oil sands so bitumen can be pumped up, as near Cold Lake.

Solution mining

Water dissolves deep salt beds and the brine is pumped up, as near Fort Saskatchewan.

These change the surface less in one spot, but wells, pads, pipelines and roads spread across a large area.

Science 8 · ES2 · Finding and extracting

Context beyond ES2.K4, added so students do not think every resource comes from a mine. Most oil sands are too deep to mine and are recovered in situ; only the shallow deposits near Fort McMurray are surface mined. Discussion: how could many small well pads affect wildlife compared with one big mine? (Linear disturbances such as roads and cleared lines break up habitat; woodland caribou are especially affected.) Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 1 atlas (oil sands, oil and gas, salt).

Lab Bench · Investigation 2 · ES2.S2

Core drilling explorer

Your mission

Budget: 100 units. Magnetic survey: 20. Each core hole: 15.

Survey, drill near the bump, then estimate the depth to ore and choose a mine.

Model rule: stripping ratio 4 or less points to a surface mine.

Worked example (made-up site)

Cores show ore from 200 m to 240 m.

overburden ÷ ore
= 200 m ÷ 40 m = 5

5 is more than 4, so an underground mine is likely.

Science 8 · ES2 · Finding and extracting

Demonstrate one site on the projector, then let pairs explore new sites. Ask students to explain their drilling strategy: why drill near the highest part of the survey line? A narrow, tall bump suggests shallow ore; a low, wide bump suggests deep ore. Stress that the rule of 4 is a simplified model; real decisions also depend on ore grade, prices, costs and environmental impacts. All site numbers are made up for practice. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 2 (core drilling explorer).

Lab Bench · Investigation 3 · ES2.S3

Ore math: is it worth mining?

Three formulas

metal = ore × grade ÷ 100
stripping ratio = waste ÷ ore
profit = revenue − costs

You do not need to memorize these.

Worked example (made-up numbers)

2 000 t ore at 0.5% copper → 10 t copper

10 t × $10 000 per tonne → $100 000 revenue

6 000 t waste ÷ 2 000 t ore → ratio 3

Costs 2 000 × $40 = $80 000 → profit $20 000

Science 8 · ES2 · Finding and extracting

Walk through the worked example line by line. Ask: what would happen to profit if the grade dropped to 0.25%? (5 t copper, $50 000 revenue, a $30 000 loss.) This shows why low-grade deposits need very large, cheap mines, often open pits. The price is a made-up practice value. Then have students do three randomized practice problems in the lab. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 3 (ore math practice).

Part 4 · ES2.K6 and ES2.S3

Responsible extraction and its impacts

Extraction always has impacts. Responsible practices make them smaller.

Section opener. Ask: name one way a mine or well could harm the environment, and one way it helps people. Keep both lists for the impacts slide. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 6 and 7, and Lab Bench, Investigation 4.

Science 8 · ES2 · Responsible extraction

Go through each practice and ask students to name the impact it targets. Examples: the Quest project near Fort Saskatchewan stores about one million tonnes of carbon dioxide a year (since 2015); methane traps about 80 times more heat than the same mass of carbon dioxide over 20 years; steam-based oil sands facilities recycle most (often over 90%) of their water. Accuracy flag (ES2.K6): these figures are general and should be checked against current sources before sharing. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (four practices with diagrams).

ES2.K6 · Tailings and reclamation

The job is not done until the land is reclaimed.

Store tailings safely

Help fine clay settle

Recycle the water

Reshape and replant

Reclamation returns disturbed land to forest, wetland or farmland. Alberta law requires it.

Science 8 · ES2 · Responsible extraction

Define tailings: the water, sand, fine clay and traces of bitumen or chemicals left after the resource is separated. The fine clay settles very slowly, which is why tailings ponds last for years. Reclamation is added to complete tailings management. Local connection: old gravel pits around Edmonton and other towns have been turned into lakes and parks. Ask: what would “success” look like for a reclaimed site? (Stable soil, native plants, wildlife returning, clean water.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (Tailings management), and Sort It, “Find, extract or reclaim?” deck.

ES2.S3 · Weighing the impacts

Two kinds of impact

Economic

Jobs, local businesses, taxes and royalties: fees paid to the resource owner. Most Alberta mineral rights belong to the province, so royalties help pay for schools and hospitals. Prices rise and fall.

Environmental

Cleared land, lost habitat, water use, dust, noise, waste and greenhouse gas emissions.

Different people feel different impacts. First Nations and Métis communities must be consulted about projects that may affect their Treaty and Aboriginal rights.

Science 8 · ES2 · Responsible extraction

Sort the opener’s lists into economic and environmental. Then demonstrate the impact analyzer: choose the oil sands mine, switch on all four practices, and watch the environmental bars shrink while the company’s costs go up. Scores are made up for comparison only. Sensitive topic: resource development is a live debate in many families and communities, and Edmonton is on Treaty 6 territory. Keep the discussion respectful and evidence based. Accuracy flag (Lesson 7): the statement that most Alberta mineral rights are owned by the province (about 81%) is context and should be verified; First Nations and Métis content is described generally and should be reviewed, ideally with a community partner. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7 (Who is affected?), and Lab Bench, Investigation 4 (impact analyzer).

Quick check

Three questions

1

Iron in Earth’s core is natural. Why is it not a geological resource?

2

A copper deposit starts 30 m down in a huge, low-grade body. Surface or underground mine? Why?

3

A pit has 3 000 t of ore at 2% copper. How many tonnes of copper is that?

Science 8 · ES2 · Review

Answers. 1: It cannot be extracted; it is about 2 900 km down and no one can reach it. 2: Surface mine (open pit). It is shallow and large, so removing the overburden is cheaper than tunnelling; this is how many big copper mines work. 3: 3 000 × 2 ÷ 100 = 60 t of copper. Use mini whiteboards or a thumbs vote. Others say the environmental costs are too high. Take a position and defend it with evidence.

Use evidence

Name practices

Which responsible practices would change your answer?

Whose view is missing from the debate?

Science 8 · ES2 · Review

This is extended response 4 from the Written Review. Run it as a four-corners or line-up debate, then have students write. A strong answer takes a clear position, uses economic evidence (royalties, jobs, products such as medical plastics) and environmental evidence (cleared land, water use, tailings, greenhouse gases), names responsible practices (carbon capture, methane reduction, water recycling, tailings management, reclamation), and includes the voices of First Nations and Métis communities and others affected. Sensitive topic: many students have family members who work in the resource sector, and others may have strong environmental concerns. Set norms so every evidence-based position is respected.
