B

LEARN 1 · SL2.K1

Three kinds of relationship

Positive

As x goes up, y tends to go up.

Example: cricket chirps and air temperature.

Negative

As x goes up, y tends to go down.

Example: elevation and air temperature near Jasper.

No relationship

Points scatter with no up or down direction.

Example: shoe size and quiz score.

Science 8 · SL2 · Patterns between variables

If the points form a shape, the two variables have a relationship. Use the four practice data sets in Learn Lesson 1: cricket chirps (positive), elevation and temperature on a trail near Jasper (negative), one student’s screen time before bed and hours of sleep (negative), and shoe size and quiz score for nine Grade 8 students (no relationship). Teaching move: students show thumbs up (positive), thumbs down (negative) or flat hand (no relationship) as you click through each data set in the lab. Discussion: “The shoe size trend line is almost flat. Why would a prediction from it be unreliable?” Sensitive topic: the screen time and sleep set is one student’s log. Keep the talk about the data, not about students’ own habits. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1, then Sort It tab, Round 1 “Which relationship?” for practice.

Science 8 · SL2 · Patterns between variables

Once you know how two variables are related, the pattern becomes a tool. Its first use: predict trends, which means estimating a value you did not measure. Ask: “About what temperature would you expect at 1600 m?” (Between 12.9 and 11.4 °C, about 12 °C.) “Is that interpolation or extrapolation?” (Interpolation.) Then: “What about 3500 m?” (Extrapolation. Weather, wind or time of day could break the pattern there. Treat it as a rough guess.) Science 8 · SL2 · Patterns between variables

Two more uses of a pattern: identify risks (notice when one variable is reaching a level linked to harm) and make decisions (choose an action based on what the pattern says is likely). The Air Quality Health Index (AQHI) runs from 1 upward. Canada labels 7 to 10 as high risk. Ask: “What is the relationship?” (Positive. Visits climb fastest above 7.) “Using the pattern, about how many visits would you expect at AQHI 6.5?” (About 8, from the Written Review model answer.) Discussion: “What is one limitation of this data?” (It comes from one clinic, so it may not represent the whole city. Other factors like pollen could also affect visits.) Sensitive topic: some students have asthma or have lived through wildfire smoke or evacuations. Keep the focus on planning and safety, and do not ask students to share health details. Five questions to ask about any pattern

TRY IT

Ask all five about the ice cream and sunburn pattern. Which question catches the problem?

Science 8 · SL2 · Thinking critically

The full wording from Learn Lesson 3: 1. Is there really a pattern? Look at the whole cloud of points, not one or two. A trend line helps, but a few points can fool you. 2. How strong is it? If points sit close to the trend line, the pattern is strong. If they are spread far from it, predictions will be rough. 3. Could a third variable explain it? Two variables can rise together because something else drives both, like hot weather driving ice cream sales and sunburns. A pattern alone does not prove that one variable causes the other. 4. Is the data fair and big enough? Ask who or what was measured, how many, and how they were picked. A small or one-sided sample can show a pattern that is not real. 5. Does it hold outside the data? A pattern is only known inside the range you measured. Beyond it, the pattern might bend, level off or stop. Try it answer: question 3 catches the ice cream problem. Teaching move: assign each table group one question. They report how it applies to the AQHI data from Lesson 2. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3, the five questions (expand each one). Written Review short answer 6 asks for two of these.

Science 8 · SL2 · How bias misleads

Route A: errors in reasoning. These are thinking mistakes, such as noticing only evidence that agrees with you (confirmation bias), judging a whole group from a few examples (hasty generalization), or deciding one thing causes another just because they change together. Reveal in two steps: show only the left table first and ask, “Is Sam right?” Sam remembers the two rowdy full moon days because they fit the idea. That feels like proof. Then show the full record. With every day counted, rowdy days happen at the same rate either way. Made-up practice data. Extension (Written Review, extended response 2): design a fair test. Record every school day for several months with the same rowdiness checklist, decided before starting. Ideally the person scoring would not know the moon phase. Compare the share of rowdy days with and without a full moon. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4, Route A “Try it” buttons (What Sam remembers / All 30 days).

Science 8 · SL2 · How bias misleads

Route B: misinterpretation and inaccurate analysis. These are mistakes in handling the data, such as picking only the part that supports your idea (cherry-picking), using a misleading graph scale, or stretching a trend line too far. The Grade 8 step challenge ran for 12 weeks (practice data from 6,200 steps in week 1 to 8,300 in week 12). Choosing a window that supports a claim and hiding the rest is cherry-picking. Teaching move: in the lab, set the window to weeks 5 to 7 and read the feedback, then set it back to 1 to 12. Ask students to find another window that would mislead, and one that happens to agree. Other Route B cases from Station 3: a survey posted on a gaming website, a trend line from 40 days of canola growth stretched to day 150, and a fertilizer test where four of six runs were thrown out. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4, Route B “Try it” (From week / To week sliders). Lab Bench Station 3: Bias Investigator.

Science 8 · SL2 · How bias misleads

An energy drink ad claims: “Scores TRIPLED after drinking our energy drink!” The ad’s bar graph starts its y-axis at 90. Scores went from 92 to 96 (made-up practice numbers from the Bias Investigator case file). Work it through: 96 ÷ 92 is about 1.04, a 4% change. Starting the axis at 90 makes the bars look 3 times different: (96 − 90) ÷ (92 − 90) = 6 ÷ 2 = 3. That is a misleading scale, a Route B bias. Station 2 formulas: actual ratio = B ÷ A. Looks like = (B − start) ÷ (A − start). Teaching move: in Station 2, slide the axis start up slowly and have students call out when the gap starts to look “huge”. Then use “New numbers” for practice. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Station 2 Graph Makeover, and Station 3 case “Scores TRIPLED”.

3 · FLAWED DECISION

Science 8 · SL2 · How bias misleads

Route C: flawed decision making. Follow the chain left to right. Ask at each arrow: “Where did the bias get in?” (In the data: one stormy day does not represent normal ridership.) Discussion: “Who is harmed by this decision?” Then: “How should the town fix its process?” Model answer from Written Review extended response 3: count riders on many days across seasons, check the numbers with drivers and riders, and review the conclusion before deciding. Second example from Station 3: “Students want more sports drinks, so the cafeteria ordered 500.” Only members of the school sports teams were asked, so spending money on that conclusion is a flawed decision. SECTION 4 · LEARN LESSON 5 · UNDERSTANDING · SL2.K2 · SL2.K3

Unbiased data predicts better

Data is unbiased when it fairly represents the whole situation. It does not guarantee a perfect prediction, but it makes the pattern far more likely to show what is really happening.

Section opener. This lesson ties the whole unit to the understanding: “Unbiased data can help accurately predict trends.” Ask: “If bias pushes results one way, what would fair data look like?” Collect ideas, then compare them with the lab’s list on the next slide. Science 8 · SL2 · Unbiased data

Your school has 400 students. In the lab, blue rings mark students who bike to school. Students estimate the percentage who bike using two survey methods: random from the school list, or at the bike rack. Teaching move: before revealing the true value, have pairs take several samples with each method and record their estimates. Then press “Reveal true value” (35%). Random samples land near the true value, and bigger samples land closer. Bike rack samples land too high every time, no matter how big they are. More biased data does not fix bias. Full list from Lesson 5: use a random sample, where everyone has an equal chance of being picked. Collect enough data; bigger samples even out chance differences. Decide how you will measure before you start, and measure every trial the same way. Keep all the data, including results that surprise you, and report outliers. Ask someone else to check your graph and reasoning. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 “Try it” (Take a sample, Reveal true value). Written Review short answer 9 asks students to design an unbiased survey.

LAB BENCH · STATION 1: PATTERN ANALYZER · SL2.S1

Worked example: the Jasper trail

Predict the air temperature at 1600 m using the elevation data from Learn 2. Made-up practice data.

STEP 1 · RELATIONSHIP

Negative

As elevation goes up, temperature goes down.

STEP 2 · PREDICT

Two points: (700, 18.0) and (2300, 7.6)

m = (7.6 − 18.0) ÷ (2300 − 700)
m = −10.4 ÷ 1600 = −0.0065 °C per m

y = 18.0 + (−0.0065) × (1600 − 700)
y = 18.0 − 5.85

≈ 12.2 °C

STEP 3 · TRUST IT?

Interpolation

1600 m is inside the measured range, 700 to 2300 m, so the prediction is fairly trustworthy.

Science 8 · SL2 · Practice

Station 1 asks students to analyze a real-world style data set in three steps: name the relationship, make a prediction from the trend line, then judge how trustworthy the prediction is. Station formulas: slope m = (y₂ − y₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁); prediction y = y₁ + m × (x − x₁). Note for the teacher: this worked example uses the Learn 2 Jasper trail data so everyone has the same numbers. The station itself picks two points on the trend line and generates a new data set each time. Using the first and last data points here gives a close estimate. It matches the measured values nearby (12.9 °C at 1500 m and 11.4 °C at 1700 m) and the real pattern in the Coverage tab note: air cools about 6.5 °C for every 1000 m of climb. Check for understanding: “What would change in Step 3 if we predicted at 3500 m?” (Extrapolation. The pattern may change out there, so treat the prediction with caution.) Then students run Station 1 on their own. Data sets include sunshine and solar power near Vegreville, home heating in Edmonton, canola growth, driving and fuel, practice and free throws, and two with no relationship (height and library books, phone number digit and test score). Three questions

1

Predicting a value far outside the range of your data is called…

A. interpolation
B. extrapolation
C. cherry-picking
D. random sampling

2

A survey on how students get to school is handed out only at the bike rack. The result will most likely…

A. be exactly right
B. underestimate how many bike
C. overestimate how many bike
D. show no pattern

3

A trend line passes through (2, 10) and (6, 22). What is its slope?

A. 2
B. 3
C. 4
D. 6

Science 8 · SL2 · Practice

Students answer on mini whiteboards or with fingers (A to D). Answer options are reordered from the lab quiz so the answer is not always first. Answers: 1. B, extrapolation. Extra means outside. Extrapolation goes beyond the measured data. (SL2.K1) 2. C, overestimate how many students bike. The sample is full of cyclists, so the estimate is pushed too high. (SL2.K3) 3. B, 3. m = (22 − 10) ÷ (6 − 2) = 12 ÷ 4 = 3. Follow-up from the quiz: using the same line, predict y when x = 4. (y = 10 + 3 × (4 − 2) = 16.)
