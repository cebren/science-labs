Science 8 · Sustainability Capstone · SL3

Sustainability

Understanding: Sustainability supports present needs while addressing long-term impacts.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Sustainable Futures Lab

Welcome students to the Grade 8 capstone. This deck follows the Sustainable Futures Lab in teaching order: Learn tab Lessons 1 to 10, then the Lab Bench investigations. Ask: what do you need today that you also want your grandchildren to have? Collect a few ideas (clean water, heat in winter, food, jobs, wild places). Tell students that today’s question is how Albertans meet present needs while thinking about long-term impacts. Lab link: Learn tab, header and understanding statement.

Learning goals

By the end of this capstone

  • I can relate environmental stewardship to sustainability.
  • I can explain actions that support sustainability.
  • I can investigate ways technologies can enhance sustainability.
  • I can explore ways energy resources can support sustainable energy in Alberta.
  • I can research possible impacts of sustainability efforts at a local or provincial level.

Science 8 · SL3 · Goals

These are the five skills statements (SL3.S1 to S5) written as “I can” goals. Have students rate themselves 1 to 3 on each now and again on the wrap-up slide. Point out that S4 and S5 ask them to explore and research, so the Lab Bench investigations matter as much as the lessons. Lab link: Coverage tab lists every statement and where it is taught.

Predict

On a January night at −30 °C, every Edmonton home needs heat. Can Alberta meet that need and still leave a healthy world for the people who come after us?

Write yes, no or it depends, and one reason. We will come back to your answer at the end.

Prediction prompt. Give 2 minutes of silent writing, then a quick show of hands for yes, no and it depends. Most strong answers will be “it depends”: on what energy sources we use, how we use resources, and what we plan for the future. Keep the answers for the wrap-up slide. Avoid letting the discussion become a debate about any one energy source yet; that comes on the debate slide.

Part 1

What sustainability means

Lasting systems, stewardship, and four actions that keep systems working into the future.

Section opener for Learn Lessons 1 and 2 (SL3.K1, K2, K3; S1, S2). Ask students to recall the Grade 7 stewardship capstone: protect, restore and use sustainably. Today we connect that to sustainability. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 and 2.

SL3.K2 · Lasting systems

Sustainability means a system keeps working

Definition

Sustainability is the ability of systems to function effectively into the future.

A system is a set of parts that work together: a lake and its fish, a farm, a city, an electricity grid.

The fishing lake model (made up for practice)

The lake starts with 100 thousand fish and makes new fish every year.

Catch 6 thousand a year: the fish settle at a steady level. The lake feeds people forever.

Catch 12 thousand a year: big catches at first, then the fish run out.

In the model, 7.5 thousand fish a year is the most that can be caught forever.

Science 8 · SL3 · What sustainability means

Teach SL3.K2. Open the fishing lake model live and move the slider from 0 to 15 while students predict what happens. Ask: which catch meets present needs best this year? Which one meets needs for 50 years? Emphasize that a catch of 0 is “sustainable” for the fish but meets nobody’s present needs, so sustainability is about balance, not stopping all use. The lake numbers are made up for practice. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

SL3.K1 · SL3.S1 · Stewardship and sustainability

Stewardship is what we do. Sustainability is the result.

Stewardship

Protect, restore and wisely use the environment

The shared job of individuals and groups, from your school to the world.

Present needs

Food, water, heat, homes and jobs today

Stewardship does not mean leaving nature untouched. People still need resources.

Future generations

Systems that still work for people not yet born

Meeting needs today in ways that also consider their needs.

Environmental stewardship supports sustainability by meeting present needs in ways that also consider the needs of future generations.

Science 8 · SL3 · What sustainability means

Teach SL3.K1 and skill S1 (relate stewardship to sustainability). Read the three cards left to right as a sentence: stewards care for the environment, so present needs are met, and future generations still have working systems. Discussion prompt: name one stewardship action you did in Grade 7 and explain how it helps a system keep working. Answer check: Written Review short answer 2. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 key box.

SL3.K3 · SL3.S2 · What we can do

Four actions that support sustainability

Use green technology

Solar farms in southern Alberta. Edmonton’s LRT and electric buses.

Manage resources, protect air, land and water

Reclaim land after a well closes. Water licences limit what is taken from rivers.

Plan expansion, preserve natural areas

Edmonton’s river valley parks. Wildlife crossings over highways in Banff National Park.

Set targets for pollution, waste and emissions

A target has a baseline, an amount and a date. Example: cut school garbage 30% by 2028.

Science 8 · SL3 · What sustainability means

Teach SL3.K3 and skill S2. Define greenhouse gas (a gas such as carbon dioxide or methane that traps energy and warms Earth) and baseline (the starting amount). Quick sort: call out examples and have students point to the right card (a new wetland park in a subdivision; a city goal for landfill waste; electric buses). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 action explorer, and Sort It, kind of action deck. Review flag SL3.K3/S2: the school garbage target is made up. Canada’s goals (40 to 45 percent below 2005 emissions by 2030, net zero by 2050) appear in the lab as context and need a currency check before you quote them.

Part 2

Technology for sustainability

Green technology, AI and drones, sustainable farms, and new ideas in farming and mining.

Section opener for Learn Lessons 3 to 6 (SL3.K4, K5, K6, K7; S3). Ask: name a technology that you think helps the environment. Then ask: does it have any downside? Every technology in this section is taught with a trade-off. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 3 to 6, and Lab Bench Investigation 3.

Science 8 · SL3 · Technology for sustainability

Teach SL3.K4. Define green technology: technology designed to reduce the impacts of human activity on the environment. Go row by row and ask students to add one more benefit or trade-off. Key idea: how much a technology helps often depends on where its energy comes from. Alberta example for carbon capture and storage: the Quest project near Fort Saskatchewan, operating since 2015. SL3.K5 · Smart monitoring

AI and drones: monitor, predict, improve

Monitor

Watch what is happening now

A drone counts bison in a park. Tower cameras with AI spot wildfire smoke. A drone with a gas sensor finds methane leaks.

Predict

Forecast what will happen

AI forecasts where a wildfire may spread tomorrow, how high a river may rise, or how much electricity a cold evening will need.

Improve resource use

Use only what is needed

A sprayer camera sprays only weeds. A smart system turns down heat in an empty school.

Trade-off: the computers that run AI use electricity, and many data centres use water for cooling.

Science 8 · SL3 · Technology for sustainability

Teach SL3.K5. Define AI (software that finds patterns in large amounts of data to make predictions or decisions) and drone (an aircraft with no pilot on board). Quick game: read an example and have students show 1, 2 or 3 fingers for monitor, predict or improve. Answers: satellite images of forest loss (monitor), AI flood forecast (predict), smart thermostat (improve). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4, and Sort It, monitor, predict or improve deck.

SL3.K6 · Sustainable farms

Sustainable agriculture has three goals

Goal 1

Protect biodiversity

Goal 2

Support soil and ecosystem health

Goal 3

Keep yields strong over the long term

Crop rotation

Canola, then wheat, then peas in the same field.

Zero tillage

Plant without ploughing so soil stays covered.

Shelterbelts and wetlands

Trees and sloughs block wind and house wildlife.

Rotational grazing

Move cattle so grass can regrow.

Science 8 · SL3 · Technology for sustainability

Teach SL3.K6. Define yield: the amount of crop harvested from an area of land. Ask students with farm connections to share which practices their families use. Cover crops are a fifth practice in the lab. Review flag SL3.K6: the statement says sustainable agriculture “maximizes long-term yield.” The lab teaches this as keeping harvests strong year after year for decades, not the biggest harvest in a single year. Make that distinction explicit. The farm plan scores in the lab are a simplified model. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 farm plan.

SL3.K7 · Innovation

Sustainability goals lead to new ideas

Farming

Precision agriculture

GPS, drones, sensors and AI treat each part of a field differently. Seed, fertilizer and sprays go only where they are needed.

Less waste, lower cost, less runoff into creeks.

Mining

Autonomous zero-emission equipment

Autonomous: runs itself without a driver. Zero emission: no exhaust, because it runs on batteries or hydrogen instead of diesel.

Driverless haul trucks already work in some Alberta oil sands mines. Most still burn diesel.

Science 8 · SL3 · Technology for sustainability

Teach SL3.K7. Define innovate: create new and better ways of doing things. Use the haul truck builder in the lab: switch driver and power to show each feature, then both together. Discuss jobs: driverless trucks reduce driving jobs but add jobs in remote operation and maintenance (a social impact, which links to K11). Review flag SL3.K7: autonomous haul trucks are taught as already in use in some oil sands mines, mostly diesel powered, with battery-electric and hydrogen versions being tested. Please check current status before sharing. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6.

SL3.S3 · Lab Bench · Investigation 3

Precision field: fly the drone

The investigation (practice data)

A canola field is split into 64 squares. Blanket spraying gives every square 2 kg of fertilizer. A drone scan with AI shows what each square really needs: 0, 1 or 2 kg.

Extra fertilizer can wash into creeks and wetlands.

Worked example

Blanket: 64 × 2 kg = 128 kg

Precision (from the scan): 52 kg

(128 − 52) ÷ 128 × 100 ≈ 59% saved

76 kg less fertilizer reaches the field.

Science 8 · SL3 · Technology for sustainability

Lab Bench Investigation 3 (SL3.S3, K5, K7). Run it live: press Fly drone scan, read the precision total, then calculate percent saved together before revealing the lab’s answer. Press New field to show that the savings change field by field. Then let students try the randomized practice problems. Formula: percent saved = (blanket − precision) ÷ blanket × 100. The 52 kg value is made up for practice; the lab’s fields are randomly generated. Connect: this is monitoring (drone) and improving resource use (precision spraying) from K5, and precision agriculture from K7. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 3.

Science 8 · SL3 · Energy for Alberta

Teach SL3.K8: cleaner energy resources (natural gas, hydrogen, nuclear, renewables) help balance energy demand with sustainability goals by reducing greenhouse gas emissions. Ask: which source is Alberta’s main power source today? (natural gas). How much lower than coal is it? (about 40 percent). Point out that natural gas is still a fossil fuel and methane leaks add to its impact, and that hydrogen is missing from the chart because it is an energy carrier: its emissions depend on how it is made (next slide). Review flag SL3.K8: natural gas taught precisely as cleaner than coal, not emission free. Values are rounded IPCC (2014) lifecycle medians and should be spot checked. The lab states that Alberta stopped making electricity from coal in 2024; this needs a currency check. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7 chart.

Science 8 · SL3 · Energy for Alberta

Teach SL3.K9. A fuel cell combines hydrogen with oxygen to make electricity, and its only exhaust is water. Hydrogen is an energy carrier: it must be made using another energy source, so how clean it is depends on how it is made (top row). The Edmonton region already produces a lot of hydrogen for industry, mostly from natural gas. Check: ask students to name the five uses from memory (gas utility systems, transportation, industrial processes, power generation, power storage). Review flag SL3.K9: grey, blue and green are common names, not official categories. Edmonton region production and home gas blending trials are described generally; verify any project names you add. SL3.K10 · Uranium and nuclear

Uranium: from rock to power

Alberta has uranium in its northeast, where the Athabasca Basin crosses from Saskatchewan. It is not mined today, and Alberta has no nuclear plant yet. Alberta is studying nuclear energy, including small modular reactors, as part of a clean energy strategy.

1

Find

Explore rock for uranium

2

Mine

Separate uranium from ore

3

Make fuel

Ceramic pellets in fuel rods

4

Split atoms

Fission heats water to steam that spins a turbine

5

Store

Used fuel kept sealed for a very long time

Benefit: very low emissions, day and night, in any weather.

Challenge: high cost, long build times, and long-term storage of used fuel.

Science 8 · SL3 · Energy for Alberta

Teach SL3.K10. Nuclear fission splits uranium atoms, releasing a large amount of energy that heats water into steam. This links to the Grade 8 atoms unit. The lab notes that a fingertip-sized fuel pellet can release about as much energy as a tonne of coal; treat that as a rounded comparison. Sensitive and debated topic: present benefits and challenges evenly and let students weigh them on the debate slide. Northeastern Alberta is within Treaty 8 territory; decisions about mining and energy projects involve consultation with First Nations and Métis communities. Review flag SL3.K10: uranium location, “not mined today,” “no nuclear plant yet” and Alberta’s study of small modular reactors all need a currency check; the Treaty 8 and consultation note is general, please review wording. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 9 steps.

SL3.S4 · Lab Bench · Investigation 2

Build an electricity mix for Alberta

mix emissions = (share₁ × emissions₁) + (share₂ × emissions₂) + …

Worked example: 60% natural gas, 40% wind

0.60 × 490 + 0.40 × 11

= 294 + 4.4 = 298.4 g per kWh

All natural gas is 490 g per kWh, so this mix is (490 − 298.4) ÷ 490 × 100 ≈ 39% lower.

Reliability check (model rule)

At least half the power should be on demand: natural gas, gas with carbon capture, hydro or nuclear.

Wind and solar change with the weather. A calm, dark January night is the hard test.

Science 8 · SL3 · Energy for Alberta

Main Lab Bench investigation (SL3.S4, with K8 and K10). Demonstrate the mix builder: start from the made-up starting mix, then challenge pairs to get below 150 g per kWh while passing the reliability check. Ask what they had to add, and what that would cost or require (for example, nuclear needs a new plant and years to build; carbon capture adds cost). The 60/40 example: does it pass the reliability check? (Yes, 60 percent is on demand.) The on-demand rule and the starting mix are simplified practice material; real grids also use batteries, imports and storage. Then assign the randomized practice problems. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 2.

Part 4

Weighing impacts

Every sustainability effort affects people, nature and money. Good decisions look through all three lenses.

Section opener for Learn Lesson 10 (SL3.K11) and Lab Bench Investigation 4 (SL3.S5). Ask: can a “green” project have a downside? Collect examples from earlier slides (bird strikes, mining for battery metals, building costs). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 10, and Lab Bench, Investigation 4.

SL3.K11 · SL3.S5 · Three kinds of impact

One wind farm, three lenses

Social

People and communities

+ Local jobs building and servicing turbines

− Changes the view for neighbours

Environmental

Air, land, water, life and climate

+ Very low emissions electricity

− Turbines can harm some birds and bats

Economic

Costs, savings, prices and income

+ Lease payments to farmers, taxes to counties

− Costly to build; backup power still needed

Impacts can be positive or negative, and different groups feel them differently. Good decisions weigh all three and listen to the people affected, including First Nations and Métis communities.

Science 8 · SL3 · Weighing impacts

Teach SL3.K11 and launch skill S5. Ask: who might feel each impact most? (a farmer leasing land, a neighbour, a county budget, migrating birds). Then open Lab Bench Investigation 4: students pick a local or provincial case file (rooftop solar on schools, southern Alberta wind farms, carbon capture near Fort Saskatchewan, hydrogen in home gas, a vertical farm in Edmonton, zero tillage on Prairie farms) and record positive and negative impacts through each lens. Review flag SL3.S5: case files are short summaries meant to start research, not replace it, and describe kinds of projects generally. The note about consulting First Nations and Métis communities is general; please review wording. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 10, and Lab Bench, Investigation 4.

Quick check

Three questions

1

A school cut garbage from 1000 kg to 800 kg a month. What percent reduction is that?

2

Why is hydrogen called an energy carrier, and why does grey or green matter?

3

Lower fuel costs for a city that switches to electric buses: social, environmental or economic impact?

Science 8 · SL3 · Weighing impacts

Answers. 1: (1000 − 800) ÷ 1000 × 100 = 20 percent (a target calculation, SL3.K3). 2: hydrogen must be made using another energy source, so it carries energy rather than being a source. Grey hydrogen from natural gas releases carbon dioxide; green hydrogen made with renewable electricity releases almost none (SL3.K9). 3: economic, because money is saved (SL3.K11); students may add that cleaner air is an environmental and social benefit too. Lab link: Quiz tab for a full ten-question round.

Debate

Should Alberta build nuclear power plants that use its own uranium?

Present evidence for both sides. Use social, environmental and economic impacts. Then state and justify your position.

Extended response prompt 3 from the Written Review. Structure: pairs list evidence for (very low emissions, steady power in any weather, Alberta has uranium, long-term skilled jobs) and against (very high cost and long build times, used fuel stored for a very long time, mining and plants need community support including First Nations and Métis consultation). Then a four-corners or line-up activity before writing. This is a debated public question: assess the quality of evidence and reasoning, not which side students choose. Model answer and key-idea checklist: Written Review tab, extended response 3.
