Part 1 · SP2.K1

Gravity and orbits

Why does the Moon go around Earth instead of flying away or crashing in?

Section opener. Ask the question and collect ideas without correcting yet. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 (Newton’s cannon).

SP2.K1 · Gravity

Gravity pulls, sideways motion keeps going

Gravity is a force of attraction between objects that have mass. An orbit is the curved path one body follows around another.

More mass

Stronger pull. The Sun’s huge mass holds all eight planets.

Farther apart

Weaker pull. Neptune feels far less of the Sun’s pull than Mercury.

Plus sideways speed

Gravity bends the path, so the body keeps falling around.

Science 7 · SP2 · Gravity and orbits

Key move: gravity alone would pull a planet straight into the Sun. An orbit needs gravity plus fast sideways motion. Accuracy flag (SP2.K1): the statement says gravity “can cause celestial bodies to orbit each other”; the lab teaches it precisely as gravity plus sideways motion. Gravity pulls both ways: Earth and the Moon both circle a shared balance point about 4700 km from Earth’s centre, inside Earth. Discussion prompt: why doesn’t a ball you throw go into orbit? (Far too slow.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

Part 2 · SP2.K2 · SP2.S2

Models of the solar system

The solar system is too big, too far and too slow to study directly. So we build models.

Section opener. Ask: what models of the solar system have you seen? (Posters, mobiles, apps, planetarium shows.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2.

SP2.K2 · Types of models

Every model shows some things and hides others

Scale model

Shows: real sizes or real distances, shrunk by the same amount. Hides: motion, and sizes plus distances together.

Orrery

Shows: movement patterns, such as inner planets moving faster. Hides: real distances and sizes.

Diagram or poster

Shows: the order of the planets and labels. Hides: motion and true scale. Planets never really line up.

Computer simulation

Shows: motion over thousands of years and predictions. Hides: only what its data and rules leave out.

Science 7 · SP2 · Models

A model is a simpler version of something real that helps us explain or predict. Models can represent movement patterns and distances (SP2.K2). Activity: in pairs, pick one model type and name one question it answers well and one it cannot. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2, tap each model type. Lesson 6 has a working orrery.

Part 3 · SP2.K3 · SP2.S1

Two models, one sky

Earth at the centre, or the Sun? And how did people decide?

Section opener. Return to the hook predictions. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 3 and 4.

SP2.K3 · Geocentric model

Geo means Earth: Earth sits still at the centre

The Sun, the Moon, the planets and the stars all move around Earth.

It made sense: we do not feel Earth move, and the sky seems to turn around us.

Ptolemy, around the year 150, put each planet on a small circle called an epicycle. The model was used for about 1400 years.

Science 7 · SP2 · Two models, one sky

In the diagram: blue Earth at the centre, the Sun on a circle around Earth, and red Mars riding on a grey epicycle whose centre travels on the big dashed circle. Stress that this was a smart, useful model: it predicted planet positions well enough for calendars and navigation. Accuracy flag (SP2.K3): the lab’s epicycle model is simplified (no equant) and drawn to match the heliocentric geometry. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3, Geocentric view.

SP2.S1 · Retrograde motion

Mars seems to go backward. Two ways to explain it.

Every so often Mars stops, moves backward against the stars for a few weeks, then goes forward again. This is called retrograde motion.

Analogy: when you pass a slower car on the highway, it seems to slide backward against the far hills, even though it is moving forward. Key point: both models predict the same loop in the sky, so watching Mars alone cannot decide between them. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3. SP2.K3

Both models predicted the same sky. Evidence had to decide.

Pause here. Ask: if two models make the same prediction, how can we choose? (Find an observation where they predict different things.) That sets up Galileo and Venus on the next slide.

SP2.K3 · SP2.S1 · Evidence

Galileo’s test: the phases of Venus

Heliocentric prediction: a full set of phases, from nearly full to a thin crescent

Ptolemy’s prediction: Venus always between Earth and the Sun, so crescents only

Around 1610 Galileo saw the top row. He also saw moons orbiting Jupiter, so not everything orbits Earth.

Science 7 · SP2 · Two models, one sky

This is the observation where the two models disagree. In the heliocentric model Venus can go behind the Sun and look small and nearly full; when it swings close to Earth it looks large and thin. Ptolemy’s model keeps Venus between Earth and the Sun, so we would see only crescents. Sizes in the drawing are not exact. Accuracy flag (SP2.K3): Venus’s phases ruled out Ptolemy’s model but not every Earth‑centred model; Tycho Brahe’s hybrid also fit, which is why Kepler’s accurate predictions and Newton’s gravity matter on the next slide. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4.

SP2.K3 · Timeline, part 1

Ideas from many places

~270 BCE

Aristarchus, Greece: Earth moves around the Sun. Few accepted it.

~150

Ptolemy, Alexandria, Egypt: geocentric model with epicycles.

499

Aryabhata, India: Earth spins on its axis.

1200s to 1300s

al‑Tusi (Persia) and Ibn al‑Shatir (Damascus) improve planet models with new mathematics.

1543

Copernicus, Poland: publishes a heliocentric model.

Science 7 · SP2 · Two models, one sky

The change took ideas from many people and places over hundreds of years. Copernicus later used mathematical methods very similar to those of al‑Tusi and Ibn al‑Shatir; historians still study how these ideas travelled. Copernicus still used perfect circles, so his predictions were not much better than Ptolemy’s at first. Accuracy flag (SP2.K3): worldwide contributions and dates are summarized briefly and approximately; please spot check the wording. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 timeline.

SP2.K3 · Timeline, part 2

Evidence builds over 300 years

1570s to 1601

Tycho Brahe, Denmark: the most precise planet measurements before telescopes.

1609 to 1610

Galileo, Italy: Jupiter’s moons and the full set of Venus’s phases.

1609, 1619

Kepler, Germany: orbits are ellipses. Predictions become far more accurate.

1687

Newton, England: gravity explains why planets orbit the Sun.

1838

Bessel, Germany: measures stellar parallax, direct proof that Earth moves.

Science 7 · SP2 · Two models, one sky

Stellar parallax is a tiny yearly shift in a nearby star’s position caused by Earth moving around the Sun. Return to the hook: what evidence did you suggest? Compare with Galileo, Kepler and Bessel. Discussion prompt: why did it take almost 300 years from Copernicus to Bessel? (Better tools, better data, and people needed strong evidence to change a model that worked.) Accuracy flag (SP2.K3): the statement says geocentric models were used “until evidence supported the heliocentric model”; the lab teaches the change as gradual. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4.

Measuring and moving

Section opener. Ask students to guess how many times farther Neptune is from the Sun than Earth. (About 30.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 5 and 6.

SP2.K4 · The astronomical unit

1 AU is the average distance from Earth to the Sun

≈ 150 000 000 km

8.3 minutes

Time for sunlight to travel 1 AU

Smaller numbers

Jupiter at 5.2 AU is easier to compare than 778 million km

Other stars

Planetary systems use AU too. Between stars: light‑years

Science 7 · SP2 · Measuring and moving

Formula shown symbolically, not for memorizing: distance in km = distance in AU × 150 000 000 km. Accuracy flag (SP2.K4): the AU is defined as exactly 149 597 870 700 m; we round to 150 million km for practice. The statement says “solar systems exist on large scales”; the lab teaches other stars’ systems as planetary systems, with ours being the solar system. The light‑year (about 63 000 AU) is added as context for distances between stars. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (AU ruler) and Lab Bench 2 (AU converter).

SP2.S3 · Movement patterns

Four patterns we can predict

Same direction

Seen from above Earth’s North Pole, every planet orbits counterclockwise.

Nearly flat

The orbits lie close to one flat plane, like grooves on a record.

Nearly round

Orbits are ellipses very close to circles.

Farther means slower

Mercury: 88 days at about 47 km/s. Neptune: about 165 years at about 5 km/s.

Science 7 · SP2 · Measuring and moving

Link back to gravity: the Sun’s pull is weaker far away, so outer planets move more slowly and also have longer paths, so their orbital period (their year) is much longer. Moons follow patterns too: the Moon orbits Earth about once a month while Earth carries it around the Sun. Because patterns repeat, astronomers predict planet positions years ahead. Alberta link: from the Beaver Hills Dark Sky Preserve east of Edmonton you can see Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn without a telescope. Accuracy flag (SP2.S3): periods and speeds are rounded reference values. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (orrery). Press Play and tap planets.

Quick check

Three questions

1

If the Sun’s gravity suddenly vanished, what would Earth do?

2

Galileo saw Venus look nearly full. Which model does that fit, and why?

3

Jupiter is about 5.2 AU from the Sun. About how many million km is that?

Science 7 · SP2 · Practice

Answers. 1: Earth would move off in a straight line, because no force would bend its path. 2: The heliocentric model. Venus can only look nearly full when it is on the far side of the Sun, which needs it to orbit the Sun; Ptolemy’s model predicts crescents only. 3: 5.2 × 150 million km ≈ 780 million km. Science 7 · SP2 · Practice

Run as a four corners debate or a written response. Model answer: the claim is not accurate. Copernicus published in 1543, but his model still used circles and did not predict much better than Ptolemy’s, so many people kept the geocentric model. It took Galileo’s observations around 1610, Kepler’s ellipses, Newton’s gravity in 1687 and finally stellar parallax in 1838 before the evidence was overwhelming. Ideas from Greece, India and the Islamic world also helped along the way. Lab link: Written Review tab, Extended response 3, with a key‑idea checklist.
