Title · Energy Flow and Cycles: The Architecture of Ecosystems. Title slide. Science 7, Life Science (LS2), master guide and explainer. A line drawing of an aspen tree on a blueprint grid.
Big idea · The Two Rules of Ecosystem Stability. Matter cycles: the same atoms of water and carbon are used again and again. Energy flows: it comes in as sunlight, passes from one living thing to the next, and leaves as heat that cannot be reused. An ecosystem maintains stability when matter keeps being recycled and new sunlight keeps arriving.
Big idea · Decoding the System: Matter vs. Energy. A table comparing matter and energy. Nature: matter is anything with mass that takes up space; energy is the ability to cause change. Unit: atoms for matter; joules (J) for energy. Movement: matter is recycled within the ecosystem; energy flows one way. Ultimate fate: matter is reused by producers; energy is lost to the environment as heat.
LS2.K1 · The Water Cycle Engine: How Water Moves and Supports Life. A labelled water cycle diagram with Rocky Mountains, a river and groundwater. Condensation makes clouds. Precipitation is the main way fresh water reaches land, such as Rocky Mountain snowpack. Transpiration keeps nutrients moving up through plants. Evaporation lifts water so it can fall far away. Runoff gives habitat for fish and beavers, and the North Saskatchewan River supplies Edmonton's drinking water. Infiltration stores groundwater that feeds springs and wetlands in dry months. Uptake: roots absorb water and animals drink it; every cell needs water.
LS2.S1 · Stress Testing the System: Extremes. Drought: rivers run low and warm, groundwater-fed wetlands shrink, plant growth stalls, and wildfire risk rises. Very wet year: wetlands expand, flood risk is high, submerged roots can drown, and excess runoff can wash topsoil away.
LS2.K2 · The Carbon Cycle Ledger: Balancing the Atmosphere. Photosynthesis removes CO2 from the air. Respiration, decomposition and emission (burning fossil fuels or forests) add CO2. Ocean exchange moves CO2 both ways; the ocean absorbs about 25 percent of human additions. Geological processes store carbon long term as coal, oil, gas and limestone, and volcanoes release it. Eating also moves carbon, as when a hare eats aspen bark.
LS2.K3 · Harnessing the Primary Source. Sunlight, carbon dioxide and water go into a leaf; glucose and oxygen come out. 6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy gives C6H12O6 + 6O2. Producers capture only about 1 percent of sunlight, yet this powers the whole food web. The exception is chemosynthesis at deep-sea vents, where bacteria make food from chemical energy.
LS2.K4 · The Trophic Hierarchy of Alberta: Who Eats Whom. Producer: grasses, willow, trembling aspen, lake algae. Primary consumer: grasshopper, snowshoe hare, moose, zooplankton. Secondary consumer: boreal chorus frog, lynx, yellow perch. Tertiary consumer: garter snake, northern pike. Apex predator: grey wolf, red-tailed hawk, great horned owl. Apex means top, not a level number; a grey wolf eating a moose is a secondary consumer and the apex predator.
LS2.K5 · S3 · The Energy Pyramid: The 10 Percent Rule. A lake energy pyramid: algae 50 000 kJ, zooplankton 5 000 kJ, yellow perch 500 kJ, northern pike 50 kJ. Most energy at each step becomes heat or leaves in waste; only about 10 percent is stored for the next level (real values are about 5 to 20 percent). An energy pyramid is always widest at the bottom.
LS2.S4 · The Math of Survival: Calculating Transfer and Loss. Willow 24 000 kJ to moose 2 160 kJ. Transfer efficiency = higher level divided by lower level times 100 = 9 percent. Energy lost = lower minus higher = 21 840 kJ (91 percent). Example for 100 kJ of aspen bark eaten by a hare: about 50 kJ becomes heat, 40 kJ leaves in droppings, and 10 kJ becomes new body tissue.
LS2.K6 · Synthesis: Where Matter and Energy Meet. Energy flows from an aspen to a hare, then to a lynx and an owl. All of them pass dead matter to decomposers in the soil, which release the last energy as heat and return carbon and nutrients to the soil for producers to reuse. Arrows in a food web point from the food to the eater.
LS2.S5 · Predictive Modeling: The Ripple Effect. If snowshoe hares disappear: lynx decline sharply, then owls and foxes hunt more grouse and mice; willow and aspen seedlings grow more. Steps: what did it eat (its food usually increases), what ate it (predators decrease), follow the arrows (predators switch prey), and look for competitors (they may increase).
Review · Defining Stability: The Ultimate Question. Why do ecosystems need a constant supply of sunlight but not a constant supply of new carbon? Carbon is matter and is recycled by decomposers and producers. Energy leaves as heat at every step and cannot be reused by plants, so ecosystems rely on the Sun to replace it.
