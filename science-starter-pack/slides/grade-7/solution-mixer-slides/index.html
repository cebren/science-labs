Solutions and solubility

Particles can interact to form solutions.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Solution Mixer Lab

Ask: where did you see something dissolve today? (Sugar in tea, drink powder, salt on icy steps.) Lab link: open the Solution Mixer Lab on the Learn tab before you start.

Read the three goals aloud. Point out that the three words in goal one (solubility, concentration, dissolving) sound alike but answer different questions. By the end students should be able to say which question each word answers. Return to these goals on the last slide. Lab link: Coverage tab lists every statement for teachers.

Make a prediction

Sugar is piling up on the bottom of your glass. Will stirring harder make it dissolve?

A. Yes, stirring always dissolves more.

B. No, but warming the water might.

C. No, nothing can make more dissolve.

Choose one and write a reason. We will come back to it.

Science 7 · M2 · Prediction

Give students one minute to choose and write a reason. Do not reveal the answer yet. The answer is B: once the water is saturated, stirring only changes how fast sugar dissolves, not how much can dissolve. Warming the water raises the solubility of sugar, so more can dissolve. Adding more water would also let more dissolve in total. Revisit this on the “Stirring changes how fast” slide. Lab link: Sort It tab, “Rate or amount?” deck checks this idea later.

Part 1 · M2.K1, M2.K2

Making a solution

What is a solution made of, and what happens to the particles?

Section opener for Learn Lessons 1 and 2. Remind students that in M1 they learned a solution is a homogeneous mixture. Now they will name its parts and watch it form. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 and 2.

M2.K1 · Three words

Solute + solvent = solution

Solute

The substance that dissolves.

Salt in salt water

Solvent

The substance that does the dissolving. Usually there is more of it.

Water in salt water

Solution

The homogeneous mixture they make together.

Salt water itself

A solution in water is called an aqueous solution.

Science 7 · M2 · Making a solution

Teach the three words with salt water. Ask students to name the solute and solvent in sweet tea (sugar, water) and in drink made from powder (powder, water). Note that the solvent is usually the part there is more of. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1, “Who is the solute?” model. Flashcards tab has all three terms.

M2.K2 · Careful with words

Dissolving is not melting, and nothing disappears

Not melting

Melting needs heat to turn a solid into a liquid. Sugar dissolves in cold water without melting.

Mass is kept

10 g + 100 g = 110 g

Sugar plus water makes sugar water with the same total mass.

Science 7 · M2 · Making a solution

Common misconception check. Many students say sugar “melts” in tea. Ask them to explain the difference. Mass conservation is brief context to show the particles are still there. Dissolved particles also pass through filter paper, which links back to M1. Accuracy note for Lance (M2.K2): the lab distinguishes dissolving from melting and mentions conservation of mass as context. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 note; Quiz tab has a mass question.

Part 2 · M2.K3, M2.K4

How fast and how strong

The rate of dissolving, and the concentration of a solution.

Section opener for Learn Lessons 3 and 4. Two new ideas: how fast a solute dissolves (rate), and how much is dissolved (concentration). Keep them separate. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 3 and 4.

M2.K3 · Rate of dissolving

Stirring and more surface area speed up dissolving

One 2 cm cube

24 cm²

Cut into 8 cubes

48 cm²

Cut into 64 cubes

96 cm²

Same sugar, same volume, more surface touching water. Stirring keeps bringing fresh water to that surface.

Science 7 · M2 · How fast and how strong

Only particles on the outside of a piece can be pulled away by water. Cutting the cube keeps the volume at 8 cm³ but doubles the surface area each time the edge is halved. Have students check: a 1 cm cube has 6 faces of 1 cm², so 8 cubes have 48 cm². The lab also mentions once that warm water usually speeds dissolving; the draft lists only stirring and surface area. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3, “Cut the cube, then race” (race times are made up for practice).

Back to the prediction

Stirring changes how fast, not how much.

Once no more sugar can dissolve, stirring harder will not help. Warming the water can.

Return to the opening prediction. The best answer was B. Stirring and crushing change the rate of dissolving. They do not change the solubility. Warming the water raises the solubility of sugar. Adding more water also lets more dissolve in total. Ask students who chose A to explain what they would now change. Lab link: Sort It tab, “Rate or amount?” deck.

M2.K4 · Concentration

Concentration is how much solute is in each amount of solution

c = m ÷ V

concentration = mass of solute ÷ volume of solution, in g per 100 mL or g/L

Dilute

Only a little solute dissolved. Weak juice.

Concentrated

A lot of solute dissolved. Strong juice.

Science 7 · M2 · How fast and how strong

Dilute and concentrated are comparison words with no fixed cut-off. Show the formula as a tool; students are not asked to memorize it. Quick example: 10 g of salt in 100 mL is 10 g per 100 mL. Adding water dilutes a solution: the same solute in more solvent. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4, salt water beaker (move both sliders).

M2.K4 · Saturated

Saturated means no more can dissolve

Word pairQuestion it answersExample
Dilute or concentratedHow much solute is dissolved?Weak juice or strong juice
Unsaturated or saturatedCan any more dissolve at this temperature?Salt water with crystals left on the bottom is saturated

At 20 °C, about 36 g of salt saturates 100 mL of water. One more gram stays on the bottom.

Science 7 · M2 · How fast and how strong

Accuracy note for Lance (M2.K4): the draft says “dilute or saturated” as if they were opposites. The lab teaches two separate scales: dilute and concentrated describe how much is dissolved, and saturated and unsaturated describe whether more can dissolve. A saturated solution of a barely soluble substance, like chalk, is still very dilute. Test for saturation: add a pinch of solute and stir. If it stays on the bottom, the solution was saturated. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4; Sort It tab, “Dilute, concentrated or saturated” deck.

Part 3 · M2.K5, M2.K6

Solubility

The most that can dissolve, and how temperature changes it.

Section opener for Learn Lessons 5, 6 and 7. Students now have the words to describe what they saw in the prediction: the solution was saturated. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 5 to 7.

M2.K5 · A physical property

Solubility names three things

The greatest amount of solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a specific temperature.

36 g of salt · in 100 mL of water · at 20 °C

Each substance has its own value, so solubility can help identify it.

In 100 mL of water at 20 °CSolubility
Sugarabout 204 g
Table saltabout 36 g
Potassium nitrateabout 31.6 g
Baking sodaabout 9.6 g
Chalk, sandinsoluble

Science 7 · M2 · Solubility

A solubility statement is only complete with all three parts. Twice the water holds twice the solute, and temperature changes the amount. Solubility also depends on the solvent: cooking oil does not dissolve in water. Alberta example: near Fort Saskatchewan, water pumped into deep salt layers dissolves salt and leaves caverns used to store fuels (described generally). Accuracy note for Lance (M2.K5): values are rounded reference values in g per 100 g of water, about 100 mL; please spot check against your data book. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5, solubility bars.

M2.K6 · Temperature

Most solids dissolve more when warm

0 to 100 °C across · grid lines every 50 g per 100 mL · dashed line at 60 °C

Potassium nitrate rises steeply: about 31.6 g at 20 °C, about 110 g at 60 °C.

Table salt stays almost flat: about 36 g at 20 °C, about 37 g at 60 °C.

Science 7 · M2 · Solubility

Read the graph with students: find 60 °C on the dashed line and read both curves. Ask: which would be easier to dissolve more of by heating? (Potassium nitrate.) Accuracy note for Lance (M2.K6): “can increase” is taught as true for most solids; table salt changes very little, and gases do the opposite (next slide). Values are rounded reference values. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6, solubility curves with the temperature slider.

M2.K6 · Two more patterns

Cooling makes crystals, and gases work the other way

Crystallization

A warm saturated solution cools. It cannot hold all its solute, so the extra comes out as crystals. Rock candy grows this way.

Gases

Oxygen: about 14.6 mg per litre of water at 0 °C, about 6.4 at 40 °C. Warm pop goes flat. Trout need cold water.

Science 7 · M2 · Solubility

Crystallization is brief context for M2.K6. Gases are included so students do not over-generalize “warmer always dissolves more”. Trout in Alberta’s mountain streams need cold water that holds more oxygen. Accuracy note for Lance (M2.K6): the draft says solubility “can increase” with temperature; the lab teaches this for most solids, notes that salt barely changes, and that gases such as oxygen and carbon dioxide become less soluble as water warms. Oxygen values are rounded reference values for water in contact with air. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6, oxygen graph.

M2.S1 · Putting it together

Three ideas, three questions

Dissolving

What is happening? Solute particles spread among solvent particles.

Concentration

How much is dissolved right now, for each amount of solution?

Solubility

What is the most that could dissolve at this temperature?

When the concentration reaches the solubility, the solution is saturated and dissolving seems to stop.

Science 7 · M2 · Solubility

This is the key slide for M2.S1. Run the potassium nitrate story in the lab live: add 10 g four times at 20 °C (the fourth scoop leaves about 8 g of solid), heat 10 °C (it all dissolves), then cool 20 °C (about 19 g crystallize). After each step ask: what is dissolving doing, what is the concentration, what is the solubility? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7, potassium nitrate story.

M2.S3 · Lab Bench, Investigation 1

Find the solubility at different temperatures

Change

Water temperature, for example 20, 40 and 60 °C.

Measure

Grams that dissolve before solid stays on the bottom.

Keep the same

100 mL of water, the same solute, the same stirring.

Add small scoops. Stir and wait after each one. If 30 g dissolved and 31 g left solid, the solubility is between 30 g and 31 g.

Science 7 · M2 · Lab Bench

Model the investigation before students try it in pairs. Turn stirring off once to show that it only changes the wait, not the result: stirring is a controlled variable. Students record each result and graph it, then switch on the reference curve to compare. The simulation values are practice data built from the reference curves. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 1, “Find the solubility”.

M2.S1, M2.S2 · Worked examples

Two calculations

Concentration

18 g of salt in 250 mL of solution.

c = 18 ÷ 250 × 100
= 7.2 g per 100 mL

Below 36 g per 100 mL, so unsaturated at 20 °C.

Crystals on cooling

Saturated potassium nitrate in 100 mL cools from 60 °C to 20 °C.

110 g − 31.6 g
= 78.4 g of crystals

Science 7 · M2 · Lab Bench

Work the first example together, then have students try the second with the data table. The formula is shown to use, not to memorize. Extension: if you add 250 mL of water to the salt solution, what is the new concentration? (18 ÷ 500 × 100 = 3.6 g per 100 mL; doubling the volume halves the concentration.) Quick check

Three questions

1. In soda pop, name one solute and the solvent.

2. Which is more concentrated: 20 g of sugar in 100 mL, or 30 g of sugar in 300 mL?

3. Salt crystals stay on the bottom after stirring. Is the solution saturated? How could you dissolve them?

Science 7 · M2 · Quick check

Answers. 1: Sugar or carbon dioxide gas is a solute; water is the solvent. 2: 20 g in 100 mL is 20 g per 100 mL; 30 g in 300 mL is 10 g per 100 mL, so the first is more concentrated. 3: Yes, it is saturated. Adding more water would dissolve them; warming helps only a little for salt, because its solubility barely changes with temperature. Lab link: Quiz tab for a full 10-question round.

Extended response

Explain the drink jug

You make a jug of drink from powder: first a little, then a lot, then too much.

Use the words dissolving, concentration, saturated and solubility to explain what happens at each stage. Then describe two ways to make the extra powder dissolve.

Science 7 · M2 · Extended response

Key ideas for a strong answer: the powder’s particles dissolve and spread evenly; with a little powder the drink is dilute; as more dissolves the concentration rises; stirring only helps it dissolve faster; at the solubility for that water and temperature the drink is saturated and extra powder stays on the bottom; adding water or warming it lets more dissolve. Have students write first, then compare with the model answer and tick key ideas. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 2.
