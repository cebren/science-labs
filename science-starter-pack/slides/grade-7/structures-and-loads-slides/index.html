Predict

An eggshell is less than half a millimetre thick. Why is it so hard to crush an egg by squeezing its ends?

Write your prediction. Then think of one human-made structure that might use the same idea.

Think, pair, share for two minutes. Collect ideas without correcting them. Part 1

Parts and kinds of structures

Beams, columns and arches. Mass, frame and shell. Ideas borrowed from nature.

Section opener for F1.K1, K2, K8 and K9. A structure is anything with a shape that holds up a load, natural or human-made. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 to 3.

F1.K1 · Components

Beams, columns and arches work together

Beam

Lies across a gap and carries a load. A bridge deck or a floor joist.

Column

Stands upright and carries weight from above down to the ground.

Arch

Carries a load around its curve. Abutments stop its ends from spreading.

Science 7 · F1 · Parts and kinds

Components are the parts of a structure. In an arch bridge, the deck beam rests on columns, and the columns pass the load to the arch. A loaded beam bends: compression on top, tension underneath (Lesson 5). A tall, thin column can buckle sideways. Trusses are beams joined in triangles. Ask: where do you see each part in our school? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1; press Find it for a quick self-check (F1.S2).

F1.K8 · Categories

Mass, frame and shell

Mass (solid)

Piled-up material. Its own weight holds it in place.

Natural: beaver dam, termite mound
Human-made: earth-fill dam, stone wall

Frame

A skeleton of joined parts carries the load.

Natural: skeleton, spider web
Human-made: house framing, bicycle, High Level Bridge

Shell

A thin curved layer spreads the load over its surface.

Natural: egg, turtle shell
Human-made: dome, bike helmet, grain bin

Science 7 · F1 · Parts and kinds

The draft says “mass or solid”: these are the same category. Strengths and weaknesses: mass structures are hard to knock over but use lots of material; frames are strong for their mass but joints can fail; shells use little material but a sharp point or dent can break them. Quick sort: hold up classroom objects and have students vote mass, frame or shell. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2; Sort It tab, “Mass, frame or shell?” deck.

Part 2

Loads and the forces inside

What pushes and pulls on a structure, and what happens inside its materials.

Section opener for F1.K3, K4 and K5. Quick prompt: what is pushing on our school building right now? Collect answers for the next slide. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4 to 6.

F1.K3 · Loads

A load is a force that acts on a structure

Permanent

Always there and always the same.

The weight of the structure itself: walls, roof, beams, foundation.

Engineers say: dead load

Non-permanent

Comes and goes, or changes size.

Wind, the weight of snow, the weight of people and furniture, ground shifting.

Engineers say: live and environmental loads

Science 7 · F1 · Loads and forces

Forces are measured in newtons (N). Ground shifting includes soil settling, frost heave in Alberta winters and earthquakes. Review flag (F1.K3): permanent loads are linked to the engineering term dead load and non-permanent loads to live loads (people, furniture) and environmental loads (wind, snow, ground shifting). Quick check: is the weight of a dome’s concrete permanent? (Yes.) Is ice on a power line? (No.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (turn loads on a house on and off); Sort It, “Permanent or non-permanent load?” (F1.S3).

F1.K3 · An Alberta load

How heavy is the snow on a roof?

m = density × area × depth
m = 300 kg/m³ × 60 m² × 0.40 m
m = 7200 kg

W = m × 9.8 N/kg
W ≈ 70 600 N

7200 kg

about the mass of five cars resting on the roof

Science 7 · F1 · Loads and forces · Example roof made up for practice

Worked example with a made-up 60 m² roof and 40 cm of settled snow. Snow densities are rounded typical values: fresh, fluffy snow about 100 kg/m³, settled about 300 kg/m³, wet spring snow about 450 kg/m³. The formulas are shown for context; students do not need to memorize them. The car comparison uses about 1500 kg per car. Ask: what happens to the load if this snow turns wet in March? (It rises by half, to about 10 800 kg.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4, Snow load estimator.

F1.K4 · Internal forces

Loads outside cause forces inside

External forces act on a structure from outside. Internal forces act inside its materials.

Compression

Squeezes, pushes together.

A column under a roof

Tension

Stretches, pulls apart.

A bridge cable

Shear

Pushes parts to slide past each other.

Scissors cutting paper

Torsion

Twists.

Wringing out a towel

Science 7 · F1 · Loads and forces

Review flag (F1.K4): the draft names internal and external forces without defining them. The lab defines external forces as loads acting from outside and internal forces as compression, tension, shear and torsion inside materials; bending is taught as compression and tension together (next slide). Quick demo: a foam pool noodle shows all four. Squeeze it, stretch it, slide your hands past each other, twist it. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5, Four internal forces; Sort It, “Which internal force?”

F1.K4 · Deformation

A bending beam is squeezed and stretched

Top: compression (arrows push in) · Bottom: tension (arrows pull out)

Small load: bends, then springs back.

Large load: stays bent. Its shape has changed.

Too much load: cracks underneath and loses its integrity.

Science 7 · F1 · Loads and forces

Deformation is a change in a structure’s shape. Elastic deformation springs back; permanent deformation stays; failure means the structure can no longer do its job. Integrity means its ability to hold together and do its job. Demo: bend a metre stick or a ruler gently over two textbooks and ask where it would crack first. (Underneath, where it is stretched most.) The lab’s beam model is qualitative. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5, Loaded beam slider.

F1.K5 · Fatigue

Small loads, repeated many times

Structural fatigue is progressive damage from repeated or cyclic loading. It can lead to failure over time.

Cycle 1

Load on, load off. A tiny crack starts.

Many cycles

The crack grows a little each time. It is hard to see.

Failure

The part snaps under a load it held many times before.

Science 7 · F1 · Loads and forces

Each load can be much smaller than the one that would break the part in one go. Examples: bridges with daily traffic, airplane wings that flex on every flight, playground swings. Engineers inspect for small cracks before they grow. Review flag (F1.K5): fatigue is taught as cracks that grow a little with each cycle; the lab’s paper clip cycle counts are made up and randomized. Activity: students bend a paper clip back and forth and count the cycles to failure, then compare small and large bends (eye protection; the snapped ends are sharp). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6.

Part 3

Failure and design

Why structures fail, what designers think about, and how to compare designs.

Section opener for F1.K6, K7 and the skills F1.S1, S5 and S6. Some real failures in this section hurt or killed people. Introduce them with care, and be aware that some students may have personal connections to accidents. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 7 and 8; Lab Bench.

F1.K6 · Causes of failure

Four causes to check

Flawed design

The plan was wrong: parts too weak or a load missed.

Poor construction

A good plan, built badly: missing bolts, weak joints.

Improper load

More load, or a different load, than it was designed for.

Structural fatigue

Repeated loading slowly grew cracks until a part failed.

Science 7 · F1 · Failure and design

A failure can have more than one cause; investigators look at where it broke, how it was built and what loads acted on it. Quick examples to sort aloud: a truck heavier than the posted limit crosses a small bridge (improper load); builders use half the bolts shown on the plans (poor construction); a sign post cracks after years of swaying in the wind (fatigue). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7; Sort It, “Why did it fail?”

F1.S5 · Case files

Two real failures engineers still learn from

Quebec Bridge · 1907

The bridge’s own weight was underestimated and not rechecked. Parts in compression bent, and the partly built span collapsed.

Main cause: flawed design. Prevention: recheck calculations and have other engineers review the design.

Tacoma Narrows Bridge · 1940

A very thin, flexible deck twisted back and forth in a moderate wind until it tore apart.

Main cause: flawed design. Prevention: test models in a wind tunnel and build a stiffer deck.

Science 7 · F1 · Failure and design

Sensitive topic: many workers were killed in the Quebec Bridge collapse. Introduce it with care. No people died at Tacoma Narrows. Review flag (F1.K6 and S5): please verify these summaries. Quebec Bridge: redesigned and finished in 1917; Canada’s Iron Ring tradition for new engineers, which began in the 1920s, is often linked to this disaster as a reminder of their duty to keep people safe (the myth that rings were made from the bridge’s steel is not repeated). Tacoma Narrows (Washington State, USA) opened in 1940 and failed about four months later by twisting (torsion); it was rebuilt with a deeper, stiffer deck. Ask: what evidence points to design rather than construction? Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 4 (two real and four made-up practice cases).

F1.S1 · F1.S6 · Lab Bench

Craft stick bridge test: strength for its mass

Same function: span a 40 cm gap. Change one thing at a time. Add load in the middle until the bridge fails.

efficiency = greatest load held (g) ÷ mass of bridge (g)

Worked example

A: 60 g, holds 4.2 kg
4200 g ÷ 60 g = 70
B: 90 g, holds 5.4 kg
5400 g ÷ 90 g = 60

B is stronger, but A is more efficient: it holds 70 times its own mass.

Science 7 · F1 · Failure and design · Example data made up for practice

Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 1 (craft stick bridge test) and Investigation 2 (efficiency practice). In the simulation, students choose flat beam, deep beam, truss or arch, set the number of sticks, then add load until it fails; each failure is recorded in a trials table. Turning off the arch abutments shows the ends spreading. The model’s strengths are made up with about 10 percent random variation, but the pattern matches real tests: triangles and arches usually beat flat beams. Remind students to change kilograms to grams first (1 kg = 1000 g). Extension: build real bridges with a stick limit and compare efficiencies as a class.

Quick check

Three questions

1

Is a grain bin a mass, frame or shell structure? Explain how it holds its load.

2

A plank bridge sags when you cross it. Which part of the plank is in tension?

3

A swing set’s top bar cracks after 15 years of safe daily use. What is the most likely cause?

Science 7 · F1 · Failure and design

Answers. 1: Shell. Its thin curved steel walls hold their shape and spread the load. 2: The bottom of the plank is in tension; the top is in compression. 3: Structural fatigue. Thousands of load cycles grew a crack, even though each load was safe. Use mini whiteboards or fingers (1, 2, 3, 4) for quick responses. Lab link: Quiz tab for a 10-question round from a bank of 25.

Extended response

Design an Edmonton bus shelter that can stand up to heavy snow, strong winds and freeze and thaw cycles.

Use the words permanent load, non-permanent load, internal forces and fatigue. Give specific design choices.

From the Written Review, extended response 3. Key ideas: permanent load is the weight of the frame, roof and glass; non-permanent loads are snow, wind, people leaning on it and frost heave. Snow puts the roof beams in bending (compression on top, tension underneath) and the columns in compression. Wind causes shear and twisting at joints. Repeated wind and freeze and thaw cycles can cause fatigue at joints. Design choices: strong welds or bolts, a sloped roof so snow slides off, triangle bracing, deep footings below the frost line, yearly inspection and a safety factor. Option: groups sketch and present, then the class judges which design best meets the four design factors. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended 3.
