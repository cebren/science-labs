Science 7 · Space · SP1

Tides, eclipses and moon phases

Interactions of celestial bodies are observed and experienced on Earth.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Moon and Tides Lab

Welcome. Today we start the Space unit. Ask: who has noticed the Moon looking different on different nights? Who has been to an ocean beach and seen the water move in and out? Tell students they will explain both, plus eclipses, using the positions of the Sun, Earth and the Moon. Lab link: open the Moon and Tides Lab, Learn tab, to show the six lessons ahead.

Make a prediction

Why does the Moon look different from night to night?

A

Earth’s shadow covers part of the Moon.

B

We see different amounts of the Moon’s sunlit half.

C

Clouds in space block part of the Moon.

Science 7 · SP1 · Opening

Have students vote with fingers (1, 2 or 3) and write one sentence explaining their choice. Do not reveal the answer yet. Answer for later: B. Many students and adults pick A; this is a common misconception that Lesson 2 addresses directly. Return to this vote on the half-lit slide.

Section 1 · Lesson 1

Sky events

Three bodies interact. We notice the results on Earth.

Section opener. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1. Ask students to name the three bodies that matter most in this unit before you move on.

SP1.K1

Three events we notice on Earth

Tides

2 high tides a day

Gravity from the Moon and Sun pulls the oceans. Most coasts: about every 24 h 50 min.

Lunar phases

29.5-day cycle

As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different amounts of its sunlit half.

Eclipses

Only when lined up

One body blocks sunlight from another. At least two solar eclipses happen somewhere each year.

A celestial body is a natural object in space. The Sun, Earth and the Moon interact through gravity and sunlight.

Science 7 · SP1 · Sky events

Define celestial body and orbit: an orbit is the curved path one body follows around another, held by gravity. Ask which of the three events students have experienced. In Alberta, tides are hard to experience because we have no ocean coast; ask who has visited a coast. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1. Students tap the Sun, Earth and Moon, then each event chip.

Section 2 · Lesson 2

Lunar phases

The Moon’s shape seems to change. The Moon itself does not.

Section opener. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (orbit and view model). Remind students of their prediction vote from the start of class.

SP1.K5

The Moon is always half lit

The Moon makes no light. The half facing the Sun is always lit.

As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different amounts of that lit half. These observed changes are lunar phases.

Phases are not caused by Earth’s shadow.

Science 7 · SP1 · Lunar phases

Return to the prediction vote: the answer is B. Point out that in the diagram every Moon is half lit on the Sun side, yet from Earth we see a different share of that lit half at each place. Ask: at full moon Earth is between the Sun and the Moon, so why is the Moon fully lit if Earth’s shadow causes phases? (Usually the Moon passes above or below Earth’s shadow.) Earth’s shadow reaches the Moon only during a lunar eclipse. Demo idea: a lamp, a ball on a stick and students’ heads. Accuracy note for teacher: the draft says phases result from “cyclical patterns in the Moon’s orbit”; the lab teaches this precisely as the changing angle between the Sun, Earth and the Moon. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2.

SP1.K5 · SP1.S3

The four main phases

New moon

Day 0

Lit side faces away. We cannot see it.

First quarter

About day 7

Right half lit, seen from Alberta.

Full moon

About day 15

We see the whole lit side.

Last quarter

About day 22

Left half lit, seen from Alberta.

The whole cycle repeats about every 29.5 days. Main phases are about 7.4 days apart.

Science 7 · SP1 · Lunar phases

These are the four phases the curriculum names. Note the word “quarter” refers to a quarter of the way through the cycle, even though we see half of the Moon’s face lit. Last quarter is also called third quarter. In the Southern Hemisphere the lit sides are flipped. Ask students to sketch these four in their notebook (SP1.S3 represent lunar phases). Predict a phase

Worked example

Tonight is a first quarter moon. What will it look like in about 15 days?

Last quarter

Science 7 · SP1 · Lunar phases

Model the counting out loud, then give students two more to try on whiteboards: (1) Tonight is a full moon. What about 7 days from now? Answer: last quarter. (2) It is day 4 after new moon. Which phase? Answer: waxing crescent. Students do not need to memorize 29.5 ÷ 4; it is shown so they can reason. Section 3 · Lesson 3

Tides

The Moon and the Sun pull on the oceans.

Section opener. Ask: has anyone seen a beach at high tide and low tide on the same day? What changed? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (tide bulges model).

SP1.K2 · SP1.S1

Gravity makes two bulges

1. The Moon pulls hardest on the near side and weakest on the far side.

2. The oceans stretch into two bulges: high tide (gold dots). Between them: low tide (grey dots).

3. Earth spins through both bulges: about 2 high tides every 24 h 50 min.

Science 7 · SP1 · Tides

Walk through the three steps. The far-side bulge surprises students: explain that the Moon pulls Earth’s centre more than the far ocean, so the far water is “left behind”. Ask why the day is 24 h 50 min instead of 24 h: the Moon moves along its orbit, so Earth needs about 50 more minutes to face it again. Accuracy flag (SP1.K2): the draft says tides result from gravitational forces of the Sun and Moon; the lab teaches more precisely that tides come from the difference in pull across Earth. That is why the Moon has the bigger tidal effect even though the Sun’s overall pull on Earth is stronger. Drawing is not to scale; real bulges are tiny compared with Earth. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3.

SP1.K2 · SP1.S1

The Sun helps or partly cancels

Spring tide

New and full moon. Sun and Moon line up, so effects add. Big tidal range.

Neap tide

Quarter moons. Sun and Moon at right angles, so effects partly cancel. Small range.

Science 7 · SP1 · Tides

The Sun’s effect on tides is about half the Moon’s. “Spring” has nothing to do with the season: spring tides happen twice every month, all year. Ask: if today is a full moon, what kind of tide will there be in about a week? (Neap, at last quarter.) The neap diagram is a simplified model: the Moon’s bulge is still the larger one, so the ocean stays a little stretched toward the Moon. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 slider, and Sort It tab deck “Spring or neap tide?”.

Bay of Fundy · New Brunswick and Nova Scotia

Up to about 16 m

Some of the highest tides in the world. The shape of the bay makes the water pile up.

Alberta has no ocean coast. Tides in our lakes are far too small to notice.

Science 7 · SP1 · Tides

Gravity sets up the tides, but coastline shape changes how big they are at each place. The Bay of Fundy is long and funnel shaped, so its tides are huge. Ask: why might a fisher or a boat captain need a tide table? Accuracy flag (SP1.K2): coastline effects and the Bay of Fundy figure (up to about 16 m) are context beyond the statement; please spot check the figure. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 note.

Section 4 · Lessons 4 to 6

Eclipses

When the Sun, Earth and the Moon line up, one body’s shadow falls on another.

Section opener. Ask students to share any eclipse they have seen or heard about, such as the April 8, 2024 total solar eclipse in eastern Canada. Note that some families and cultures hold teachings or customs about eclipses; welcome sharing without asking anyone to explain. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4 to 6.

SP1.K3 · SP1.S2

Solar eclipse: the Moon blocks the Sun

In the umbra

Dark centre of the shadow. Total eclipse along a narrow path.

In the penumbra

Lighter outer shadow. Partial eclipse over a wider area.

Eye safety

Never look at the Sun. Use certified eclipse glasses or a pinhole projector.

Science 7 · SP1 · Eclipses

Key ideas: it can only happen at new moon; the Moon’s shadow falls on a small part of Earth; everyone outside the shadow sees no eclipse. The path of totality is usually less than about 270 km wide and totality lasts only a few minutes in one place. Ask: why do so few people ever see a total solar eclipse from home? Accuracy flag (SP1.K3): the umbra and penumbra are named to explain “totally or partially”; annular eclipses are added later as context. The April 8, 2024 eclipse route through Ontario, Quebec and Atlantic Canada should be spot checked before teaching it. Stress eye safety; some students may feel anxious about eye damage, so focus on how to watch safely. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (tap A, B, C).

SP1.K4 · SP1.S2

Lunar eclipse: Earth’s shadow on the Moon

Earth is directly between the Sun and the Moon, at full moon.

Total: the whole Moon is in Earth’s umbra. Partial: only part of it is.

The Moon looks coppery red: Earth’s air bends reddish light into the shadow, like a sunset.

Seen from all of Earth’s night side. Safe to watch.

Science 7 · SP1 · Eclipses

In the diagram, the small grey Moon at the top right is outside the shadow (a normal full moon) and the red Moon is in the umbra (totality). Ask: why can a lunar eclipse only happen at full moon? Accuracy flag (SP1.K4): “directly between” is taught as a close line-up at full moon; a faint penumbral stage appears in the lab model only as “faint dimming”. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5: choose “Through the middle”, “Off centre” and “Misses the umbra” and drag the Moon across.

SP1.K3 · SP1.K4 · SP1.S2

Why not an eclipse every month?

The Moon’s orbit is tilted about 5°. Most months the new moon passes above or below the Sun, and the full moon misses Earth’s shadow.

Eclipses happen only when a new or full moon falls where the orbits cross: eclipse seasons, about every 6 months.

Science 7 · SP1 · Eclipses

In the diagram the dashed line is the plane of Earth’s orbit and the solid line is the Moon’s tilted orbit. The new moon (dark, upper left of Earth) passes above the line to the Sun, and the full moon (bright, lower right) passes below Earth’s shadow, so there is no eclipse this month. The tilt is exaggerated. This idea is added beyond the statements to answer the question students always ask. At least two solar eclipses happen somewhere on Earth each year. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6: switch between New moon and Full moon and move the slider to zero.

Lab Bench · SP1.S2

Total or annular?

apparent size ≈ diameter ÷ distance

Sun: 1 392 700 ÷ 149 600 000 ≈ 0.00931
Moon at 380 000 km: 3 474 ÷ 380 000 ≈ 0.00914

The Moon looks a bit smaller, so a ring of Sun shows: an annular eclipse.

Annular: ring of Sun

Science 7 · SP1 · Eclipses

Main Lab Bench investigation for eclipses. The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and about 400 times farther away, so they look almost the same size. The Moon’s orbit is an oval, so its distance changes from about 356 500 km to about 406 700 km. When it is closer than about 373 000 km at an exact line-up, it covers the Sun: total. Students do not need to memorize these numbers. Quick practice: Moon at 371 000 km? (3 474 ÷ 371 000 ≈ 0.00936, bigger than 0.00931: total.) Moon at 395 000 km? (≈ 0.00879: annular.) Accuracy note: annular eclipses are context beyond SP1.K3; values are rounded. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 4.

Quick check

Three questions

1

Tonight is a full moon. What phase will it be in about 7 days?

2

Why are tides bigger near new moon and full moon than near the quarter moons?

3

The Moon turns coppery red one night. Which body is in the middle of the line-up?

Science 7 · SP1 · Review

Answers: (1) Last quarter. (2) Near new and full moon, the Sun and Moon line up with Earth, so their tidal effects add, giving spring tides with a big range; at the quarter moons they pull at right angles and partly cancel (neap tides). (3) Earth: a total lunar eclipse at full moon, with Earth between the Sun and the Moon. Use mini whiteboards. Lab link: Quiz tab for a 10-question round drawn from 25 questions.

Extended response

“Eclipse this month!”

A headline promises an eclipse this month, and tonight is a first quarter moon. Predict when any eclipse could happen, what kind each could be, and what else must be true.

Lunar only at full moon

Solar only at new moon

Timing from first quarter

Tilted orbit, close line-up

Science 7 · SP1 · Review

Give students 10 minutes to write, then pair-share. A strong answer: a lunar eclipse could only happen at the next full moon, about 7 days after first quarter; a solar eclipse could only happen at the next new moon, about 22 days away; and the Moon must also be near the place where its tilted orbit crosses the plane of Earth’s orbit so the line-up is close. One or both might happen. The pills are the key-idea checklist. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 4, with the model answer and checklist.
