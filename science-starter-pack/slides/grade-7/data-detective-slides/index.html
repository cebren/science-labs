What we notice, what we conclude, and the two kinds of data.

SL1.K2 · Observation

Senses and tools gather information

Your senses

Sight, hearing, touch and smell. Your hand can tell you the water feels “cool”.

Tools

Thermometer, ruler, balance, hand lens. A thermometer gives an exact number in degrees Celsius.

A good observation describes only what is there. It does not explain why.

Science 7 · SL1 · Gathering evidence

Safety: in a lab we never taste samples, and we smell by gently wafting air toward the nose. The lab’s Lesson 1 model observes a jar of North Saskatchewan River water with each sense or tool. The lab also notes that careful, long-term observation is part of Indigenous knowledge systems, with knowledge keepers on Treaty 6 territory noting when lakes freeze, berries ripen and animals move. Share that respectfully and in general terms only; please review this wording and consider inviting a community knowledge keeper rather than speaking for them. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

SL1.K3 · Inference

Observation + prior knowledge = inference

“The sidewalk is wet” is an observation. “It rained last night” is an inference. New observations can change it.

Accent statement slide. An inference is an explanation or conclusion based on observations plus prior knowledge. Two people with the same observations can infer different things because they know different things. That is why scientists test inferences. Try the lab’s tracks in the snow model in Whitemud Ravine: each new clue and each piece of prior knowledge changes the best inference. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2; Sort It, Observation or inference deck.

SL1.K4 · Two kinds of data

Words or numbers

Qualitative

Describes qualities with words: colour, texture, smell, behaviour. Think quality.

“The chickadees were noisy at the feeder.”

Quantitative

Uses numbers from counting or measuring, usually with units. Think quantity.

“We counted 23 chickadee calls in 5 minutes.”

Both kinds can support a scientific explanation.

Science 7 · SL1 · Gathering evidence

Quantitative data is easier to compare and graph. Qualitative data can capture things numbers miss, like an animal’s behaviour or a strange smell. Have pairs turn three qualitative notes into quantitative ones. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (flip each note); Sort It, Qualitative or quantitative deck; Lab Bench, Station 2.

SL1.K5 · Class data set

How fast does a sugar cube dissolve?

Water (°C)102030405060
Time (s)927155413022

One sugar cube in 200 mL of water at each temperature. Only the temperature was changed.

Example data made up for practice.

Science 7 · SL1 · Thinking with data

Ask students what they notice before naming any skills. Expected: time drops every step as temperature rises. Ask them to predict 70 °C (the lab’s model predicts about 15 s, following the curve). Remind them that predictions inside the tested range are safer than ones far outside it. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4.

SL1.K5 · Critical thinking

Five skills, one data set

SkillApplied to the sugar cube data
Find patternsWarmer water always dissolved the cube faster.
PredictAt 70 °C it should take about 15 s.
Analyze a relationshipOnly temperature changed, so it is likely the cause.
Test a hypothesis“Warmer is faster” is supported. “No effect” is contradicted.
Justify a conclusionTime fell every step, from 92 s to 22 s. Repeat trials to be surer.

Science 7 · SL1 · Thinking with data

Key teaching point for analyzing a relationship: without a fair test, two things changing together does not prove cause and effect. Here the class kept water volume, cube size and stirring the same. Give each table group one skill and have them explain it back. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (choose a thinking skill); Written Review, extended response 3.

SL1.K1 · Scientific explanation

Claim, evidence, reasoning

Claim

Your answer to the question.

Evidence

Observations and data that support the claim.

Reasoning

The science idea that connects the evidence to the claim.

Explanations rest on observations and interpretations of data, not opinion. They can change when new evidence appears.

Science 7 · SL1 · Thinking with data

Model it with the lab’s Lesson 5 question: why did the puddles on the school field disappear faster on Tuesday than on Monday? Build the explanation one part at a time with the class. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5; Lab Bench, Station 3 step 3.

Section opener. Ask: have you ever seen an ad claim something is “proven”? Who paid for that test? Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 6 and 7.

SL1.K6 · Bias

Four ways bias sneaks in

BiasWhenWhat happens
SamplingCollectingThe sample does not represent the whole group.
MeasurementCollectingA tool or method is always off in the same direction.
ConfirmationInterpretingNoticing only the results you expected.
InterestInterpretingSomeone who gains from a result reads data in their favour.

Scientists reduce bias with large random samples, checked tools, repeated trials and review by others.

Science 7 · SL1 · Bias and careers

Bias is anything that pushes results in one direction so they no longer give a fair picture. Read a case for each row and have students name the bias (the lab’s Lesson 6 “Spot the bias” has ready cases). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6.

Walleye in Pigeon Lake: which sample is fair?

Catches all sizes.

Science 7 · SL1 · Bias and careers

The lab models a lake with 400 virtual fish; the numbers are a model, not real survey data. Have students predict which method gives a too-high or too-low average, then test each in the simulator and compare to the true lake average. Answer: random fine-mesh nets are fair; the large-mesh net and contest photos overestimate length; shore sampling underestimates. Discuss: how could a biased estimate lead to a wrong claim that the walleye population is healthy and full of large adults? Lab link: Lab Bench, Station 4.

Science 7 · Lab Bench

Station 3 steps: 1. Write a hypothesis (If the ice cube is covered with ..., then it will melt ..., because ...). 2. Run three trials of each and read the means. 3. Build a claim, evidence and reasoning explanation. Expected claim: rock salt melts ice fastest, because salt lowers the temperature at which water freezes. The worked mean uses the Station 2 bean seedling heights. Ask students how to make the test more reliable: same size cubes, same lamp distance, more trials. Lab link: Lab Bench, Stations 2 and 3.

SL1.S3 · Hypotheses and predictions

Explain, then expect, then test

Hypothesis

A testable explanation. Salt lowers the temperature at which water freezes.

Prediction

What you expect to see. Salted ice will melt fastest of all four.

Test

The data supports or contradicts the hypothesis.

Either result is useful. A contradicted hypothesis still teaches us something.

Science 7 · Lab Bench

Written Review short answer: a hypothesis is a testable explanation, often written as If… then… because. A prediction is what you expect to observe if the hypothesis is correct. Testing the prediction lets you support or contradict the hypothesis. Have students write their own Station 3 hypothesis before running trials. Lab link: Lab Bench, Station 3 step 1; Written Review, short answer.

Quick check

Three questions

1

“The bird has a red chest.” Observation or inference?

2

“We counted 23 chickadee calls in 5 minutes.” Qualitative or quantitative?

3

A survey measures only fish caught in a large-mesh net. Which bias is this?

Science 7 · SL1 · Practice

Answers: 1. Observation, because it describes what is seen and does not explain why. 2. Quantitative, because it is a number from counting. 3. Sampling bias, because small fish slip through, so the sample does not represent the whole lake. Use mini whiteboards. For a longer check, run a Quiz round in the lab. Lab link: Quiz tab; Sort It.

Debate · Written Review extended 4

Would you trust this claim?

A news story claims a new sports drink makes students run faster. The study was paid for by the company and tested 6 students on the school team.

Use what you know about bias and scientific explanations. How would you design a fair study?

Science 7 · SL1 · Practice

This is a made-up scenario for practice. Key ideas from the lab’s checklist: interest bias from the company paying for the study; a small, unrepresentative sample (6 students, all on the school team); was there a fair test with a control group? A fair design: many randomly chosen students, half given the drink and half a look-alike drink, with the runners and timers not knowing which is which. Students end with a justified judgment about the claim. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 4.
