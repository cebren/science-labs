Environmental stewardship

Environmental stewardship can address present needs that consider the environment and human well-being.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Stewardship Action Lab

Welcome students to the Science 7 stewardship capstone. Read the understanding statement aloud and ask: what does “present needs” mean for us here in Edmonton? (Water, food, homes, places to play.) Explain that stewardship is about meeting those needs while caring for land, water, living things and people. Lab link: open the Stewardship Action Lab to the Learn tab before the next slide. Who owns the North Saskatchewan River?

who is responsible for keeping it clean?

Part 1

What is stewardship?

A shared job: protect, restore and sustainably use the environment, at every level from your home to the whole world.

Section opener. Ask: have you heard the word “steward” before? A steward takes care of something for others. Connect to class jobs, a pet sitter or a team equipment manager. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

SL2.K1 · Definition

Three parts of stewardship

Environmental stewardship is the shared responsibility of individuals and groups to protect, restore and sustainably use the environment and its resources.

Protect

Keep healthy land, water and living things from being damaged.

Example: setting aside a wetland as a park.

Restore

Help damaged places and populations recover.

Example: planting native shrubs on an eroded creek bank.

Use sustainably

Use water, wood and soil in a way that leaves enough for the future.

Example: buying used clothes instead of new.

Science 7 · SL2 · What is stewardship?

Key message: stewardship does not mean leaving nature untouched. People still need water, food and homes. Stewardship meets those needs today while caring for the environment and for people’s well-being. Quick check: ask students to give one more example for each card. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1, and the Sort It deck “Protect, restore or use”.

SL2.K1 · Levels

A shared responsibility at four levels

Local

Home, school, city

A class runs a litter pickup in an Edmonton river valley ravine.

Provincial

Alberta

Alberta’s deposit on drink containers. Water licences limit how much water large users take.

National

Canada

National parks such as Elk Island. The Species at Risk Act protects species at risk.

International

Many countries

The Migratory Birds Convention (1916) between Canada and the United States.

Rivers, birds and air cross every border, so no one person or group can care for them alone.

Science 7 · SL2 · What is stewardship?

Use the nested rings model in the lab: each level sits inside a bigger one. Ask students to name one thing they personally can do at the local level. Other international examples in the lab: the Montreal Protocol (1987) on the ozone layer, and the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022), where countries including Canada set a goal to protect 30 percent of land and water by 2030. Part 2

What do stewards do?

Four kinds of action protect, restore and sustainably use the environment.

Section opener. Ask students to brainstorm stewardship actions they already know about in Edmonton or Alberta before revealing the four categories. Sort their ideas into the four categories on the next slide. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (action explorer).

SL2.K2 · Actions

Four kinds of stewardship action

Protected areas

Parks and reserves set aside to conserve biodiversity. They need ongoing care: monitoring wildlife and managing visitors. Example: Wood Buffalo National Park.

Waste reduction and circular economy

Make less waste, and keep materials in use longer. Example: food scrap composting and clothing resale.

Water management and conservation

Share water fairly, keep it clean and don’t waste it. Example: fixing leaks, water licences.

Reintroduction of native species

Bring a species back to a place where it used to live. Example: plains bison in Banff National Park.

Science 7 · SL2 · What do stewards do?

Define native species: a species that lived in a place naturally before people moved it there. Plains bison and swift foxes are native to Alberta’s grasslands. Point out that many actions do more than one job: a national park protects habitat and can also be where a native species is reintroduced. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (action explorer shows which actions protect, restore or use sustainably).

SL2.K2 · Protected areas and native species

Bringing species back in Alberta

Over a century

Elk Island National Park

A fenced park east of Edmonton that has helped conserve plains bison and wood bison. Its bison have started herds in other places.

From the 1980s

Swift fox

After disappearing from the Canadian prairies, foxes were released in southern Alberta and Saskatchewan. A wild population lives there again, still listed as threatened.

2017

Plains bison in Banff

A small herd moved from Elk Island into Banff National Park, where bison had been missing for more than a century.

Reintroduction is not finished on release day. Teams use collars and counts to see whether animals survive, have young and spread.

Science 7 · SL2 · What do stewards do?

Link back to LS3: these species were extirpated (gone from one area but alive elsewhere), which is why reintroduction was possible. Monitoring connects to “monitoring data” from LS3.K5. Bison are culturally significant to many First Nations and Métis people; describe this generally and avoid speaking for specific communities. Accuracy flag (SL2.K1/K2): check the Banff 2017 date, swift fox history and status, and Elk Island’s role on Parks Canada and Species at Risk Public Registry pages before sharing. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 2 (case files: Bison return to Banff, Swift fox comeback, Elk Island).

SL2.K2 · Water

Responsible water management and conservation

Share it fairly

People, farms, industry and nature all need water from the same rivers. In Alberta, large users need a licence that limits how much they take.

Keep it clean

Edmonton’s drinking water comes from the North Saskatchewan River. Keeping pollution out upstream protects people and fish.

Use less

Fix leaks, use low-flow taps and shower heads, and water gardens less.

water saved = (old flow − new flow) × minutes × number of times
(9 − 6) L/min × 5 min × 7 showers = 105 L saved each week

Science 7 · SL2 · What do stewards do?

Walk through the worked example: an old shower head uses 9 L per minute and a low-flow one uses 6 L per minute; these flows are made-up practice values. Students do not need to memorize the formula. Ask: what else could a household do? Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 4 (randomized practice includes water-saved problems).

Part 3

Closing the loop

A circular economy keeps materials in use instead of throwing them away.

Section opener. Quick poll: hands up if you have ever worn a hand-me-down, bought something used, or rented skates or a costume. Every hand up is a circular economy practice. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3.

SL2.K3 · Circular economy

A line or a loop?

Linear economy

Take → Make → Use → Throw away

Materials go one way and end in a landfill. New materials must be dug up, grown or pumped out to make the next product.

Circular economy

Design → Make → Use → Share → Repair → Resell → Recycle

Products are designed and used so materials are reused, repaired or recycled instead of thrown away. This reduces waste and protects the environment.

Example from the curriculum: clothing rental and resale programs. A hockey jacket rented for a season or sold at a swap is worn by many people instead of one.

Science 7 · SL2 · Closing the loop

Ask students to place three items they used today on the line or the loop. The circular economy starts at design: products built to last, to be fixed and to come apart easily are easier to keep in the loop. Accuracy flag (SL2.K3): the curriculum defines the circular economy as reuse, repair or recycle; the lab adds “design” as the first step (the statement says “a way of designing and using products”) and puts recycling last. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (toggle Linear and Circular on the loop diagram) and the Sort It deck “Linear or circular”.

SL2.K3 · Which loop first?

Inner loops first, recycle last

1

Keep using

Take care of it so it lasts.

2

Share or rent

Costumes, skates, tents.

3

Repair

A zipper, a bike tire.

4

Resell

Swaps, thrift stores, hand-me-downs.

5

Recycle

Only when it is truly worn out.

Keeping a product in use keeps all of its value. Recycling breaks it into materials, which takes energy, and the material is often lower quality. So reduce, reuse and repair come first.

Science 7 · SL2 · Closing the loop

Discussion prompt: why is recycling a can good, but refilling a water bottle better? (Refilling keeps the whole bottle in use with no energy to melt and remake it.) Accuracy flag (SL2.K3): the order of loops is added for precision; the curriculum lists reuse, repair and recycle without ranking them. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3; Written Review short answer 5.

SL2.S4 · Lab Bench investigation

The jacket loop

Each student wears a jacket about 100 times a winter, then outgrows it. A jacket lasts about 300 wears, or 450 with repairs. Numbers are made up for practice.

Linear

90

9000 wears ÷ 100 wears each = 90 jackets. Each is thrown out after one winter.

Pass jackets on

30

9000 ÷ 300 = 30 jackets. That is about 67% fewer: (90 − 30) ÷ 90 × 100.

Pass on and repair

20

9000 ÷ 450 = 20 jackets. Repair only helps when jackets are passed on.

Recycling worn-out jackets keeps material out of the landfill, but new jackets still have to be made.

Science 7 · SL2 · Closing the loop

Model the calculation: total wears = 30 students × 3 winters × 100 wears = 9000 wears. Then jackets needed = total wears ÷ wears each jacket gives. Ask: why does repair do nothing in the linear case? (Each jacket is outgrown and thrown away long before it wears out.) Students do not need to memorize the formulas. Accuracy flag (SL2.K3): all model numbers are made up and labelled as practice data. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 3 (jacket loop sliders, worked example and randomized practice).

Big idea

Keep it in use before you recycle it.

The most circular product is the one that is still being worn, shared or fixed.

Pause here. Ask students to name one thing they own that could be shared, repaired or resold instead of thrown away. Collect a few ideas for the stewardship plan later in the capstone. Lab link: Sort It, “Linear or circular” deck as a quick warm-up.

Part 4

Who benefits, and why act?

Stewardship helps ecosystems and people. People act for different reasons, and caring for something is not the same as owning it.

Section opener. Ask: when you do something good for the environment, why do you do it? Collect answers on the board and keep them for the motivations slide. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4 to 6.

SL2.K4 · Benefits

Protecting a wetland helps twice

Enhanced ecosystem stability

Habitat for ducks, frogs, insects and plants keeps biodiversity high.

The wetland filters water and traps sediment.

It stores water in floods and droughts, so the ecosystem recovers faster.

Improved human well-being

Cleaner water downstream for drinking.

Less flooding of homes and roads.

Places for birdwatching, learning and quiet time.

Well-being means being healthy, safe and able to live a good life. A stable ecosystem keeps giving people what they need.

Science 7 · SL2 · Who benefits, and why act?

Recall from LS1: ecosystem stability is the capacity of an ecosystem to keep its structure, biodiversity and processes over time. Ask students to pick another action (bison, clothing resale, saving water, native plants) and name one benefit for the ecosystem and one for people. Accuracy note (SL2.K4): benefits are described generally, with no health statistics. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (benefit web with five actions).

SL2.K5 · SL2.S1 · Motivations

Why do people act?

Future generations

Caring for the environment so children and grandchildren have clean water and wild places.

Planting trees that grow for decades.

Monetary incentives

Saving money, earning money, or getting a refund or rebate.

Alberta’s drink container deposit.

Government regulations

Rules and laws, often with fines for breaking them.

City rules on sorting waste.

Most actions have more than one motivation. Fixing a leaky tap saves money on the water bill and saves water for the future.

Science 7 · SL2 · Who benefits, and why act?

Return to the reasons students gave on the section opener and sort them into the three motivations. Mixer practice: “A family installs a low-flow shower head to cut their water bill and save water for the future” (money and future generations). “An oil sands company reclaims a mine site because Alberta law requires it” (regulation). Accuracy note (SL2.K5): regulation examples are described generally. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 1 (motivation mixer, eight scenarios) and the Sort It deck “Motivation”.

SL2.S5 · Caring and owning

Caring for something versus owning it

Owning only

Rights

The law says it is yours. You can sell it and keep others from using it.

Both

Decisions

You make choices that affect its future. You can feel responsible for it.

Caring only

Responsibility

You look after something that is not yours: a river, a city park, a library book.

Owning does not guarantee caring, and caring does not need owning. Rivers, air and wildlife belong to no one person, so stewardship asks everyone to act as a caretaker.

Science 7 · SL2 · Who benefits, and why act?

Return to the hook: who owns the North Saskatchewan River? Use it to land the idea that shared resources need shared care. Treaty 6 note: many First Nations and Métis people describe their relationship with the land as one of responsibility and respect rather than ownership. Keep this general, invite a local Knowledge Keeper or Elder to share perspectives, and review the wording before teaching. Accuracy flag (SL2.S5): the lab says most of Alberta’s land is public (Crown) land; check this figure before sharing. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (Venn diagram) and the Sort It deck “Owning or caring”.

Part 5

Your turn to act

Research a real stewardship action, then plan one of your own for home or school.

Section opener for the project work. Decide whether students work alone or in pairs, and whether plans focus on home or school. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigations 2, 4 and 5.

SL2.S2 · SL2.S3 · Research and plan

From research to a plan

Research an action

Pick a case: bison in Banff, the swift fox, Elk Island, Alberta’s container deposit, Edmonton’s waste sorting or the Migratory Birds Convention.

Find who is involved and at what level, the kind of action, the motivation, the benefits, and the monitoring evidence. Use two reliable sources.

Plan your own

Choose home or school and a focus: waste, water or habitat.

Pick up to three actions, a way to measure success, who will help, and when you will check results.

diversion rate = mass kept out of landfill ÷ total mass × 100
2600 g composted or recycled ÷ 4000 g total × 100 = 65%

Science 7 · SL2 · Your turn to act

Model a measurement for the plan with the waste audit: a bin holds 4000 g and 2600 g is composted or recycled, so the diversion rate is 65%. Students record a number before they start and again after a few weeks. Accuracy flag (SL2.S2/S3): case files are short starting points, not full research; the audit data are made up, and sorting rules vary by city, so check your local guide. Recycle, because it breaks the product into materials, which takes energy and often lowers quality, while reuse and repair keep the whole product in use. 3. Extended response

If no one owns it, whose job is it?

Most of the environment, such as rivers, air and wildlife, is not owned by any one person. Explain why stewardship is still everyone’s responsibility. Use the ideas of caring and owning, and the four levels of stewardship.

Caring vs owning

Owning gives rights. Caring is about responsibility.

Levels

Give examples from at least three of the four levels.

Future

Explain what happens to shared resources if no one cares for them.

Science 7 · SL2 · Review

This is Written Review extended response 4. Option: run it as a four corners debate first (strongly agree to strongly disagree with “If you don’t own it, it’s not your job”). Key ideas for the model answer: you do not have to own something to care for it; no one owns the North Saskatchewan River or migrating birds, so everyone who uses or affects them shares responsibility; each level has a role (individuals and schools reduce waste and water use, Alberta manages water licences, Canada protects national parks and species at risk, countries make agreements about birds and the ozone layer); if everyone acts only as a user, shared resources can be damaged, and if everyone acts as a steward, they last for future generations. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 4.
