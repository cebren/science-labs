Think, pair, share

A wildfire burns a forest north of Edmonton. What will that land look like in 1 year? In 50 years?

Sketch or list your prediction. Keep it. We will check it against what scientists know.

Hook, about 5 minutes. Students predict silently for 1 minute, then share with a partner. Collect a few ideas: many students expect a burned forest to stay bare for a long time. Do not correct yet. Revisit this prediction on slide 10, when secondary succession after fire is taught. Sensitivity note: some students or families may have been affected by wildfire evacuations (for example Fort McMurray 2016 or recent seasons). Frame the discussion around the forest, and give students room to opt out of sharing personal stories.

LS3.K2 · Disturbance

What is a disturbance?

An event that suddenly changes an ecosystem. It kills or removes living things, or changes their habitat.

Geological processes

Landslides, rockfalls, earthquakes, volcanic eruptions

Extreme weather

Floods, droughts, tornadoes, lightning wildfires

Human activity

Clearing land, draining wetlands, mining, pollution, moving species

LS3.K1 · Ecological succession

The pattern of change in the species and habitat of an area over time.

Each wave of living things changes the place, which makes room for the next wave.

Section opener. Key vocabulary: ecological succession. The curriculum calls it a predictable pattern. Teach it as predictable in its general shape (small fast-growing plants first, then shrubs, then trees), while the exact species and timing vary from place to place. Another disturbance can restart the clock. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2.

LS3.K1 · Two kinds of succession

Is there soil yet?

Primary succession

Starts with no soil: bare rock from a melting glacier, a lava flow, a fresh landslide.

First arrivals: pioneer species such as lichens and mosses, which slowly build soil.

Speed: hundreds of years.

Secondary succession

Starts with soil left behind: a burned forest, a flooded field, an unused farm field.

First arrivals: plants regrow from surviving roots and seeds in the soil.

Speed: years to decades.

Science 7 · LS3 · Succession

The deciding question is whether soil remains. The draft curriculum describes secondary succession as following when "existing areas undergo change"; this deck teaches the more precise version: a disturbance removes most living things but leaves soil, roots and seeds. Lab link: Sort It, "Primary or secondary" deck (8 cards), good as a 3-minute check right after this slide.

LS3.K1 · LS3.K6 · Built for fire

Fire is part of the boreal forest

Cones

Sealed until it burns

Lodgepole and jack pine cones are sealed with resin. Heat opens them and seeds fall on fresh ash.

Roots

Alive underground

Trembling aspen roots survive the fire and send up thousands of new shoots.

Light

Room to grow

Burns open the canopy, so sun-loving plants like fireweed can thrive.

Science 7 · LS3 · Succession

Connects succession to adaptation (from LS1) and sets up forest management (slide 18). Key point: in the boreal forest, fire is a natural disturbance that the ecosystem recovers from; problems come when fires are too frequent, too severe, or reach communities. Optional discussion: why might putting out every fire make later fires worse? (Fuel builds up.) This question returns as the debate on slide 21.

Check both figures against the latest reports before sharing (the Living Planet Index is updated every two years). The 73 percent is an average change in population size across monitored populations of vertebrates, not "73 percent of animals died" and not 73 percent of species lost; this is a common misconception worth naming. LS3.K3 · LS3.S2 · Four causes

What drives the decline

Habitat destruction

Much of Alberta’s native prairie has been plowed or built on.

Pollution

Fertilizer runoff feeds algae blooms that use up a lake’s oxygen.

Invasive species

Prussian carp spread through Alberta rivers and crowd out native fish.

Climate change

Warmer winters helped mountain pine beetle spread into Alberta.

Science 7 · LS3 · Declining biodiversity

These are the four causes the curriculum names. Scientists (IPBES) also name overexploitation, such as overfishing and overhunting, as a major cause; mention it briefly. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (turn on pressures and watch a wetland lose species; stress that pressures add up) and Sort It, "Cause of decline" deck. Alberta also inspects boats to keep zebra and quagga mussels out of its lakes, a good prevention example for slide 17.

LS3.K4 · Losing species

Endangered, extirpated, extinct

Endangered

Likely to disappear soon if nothing changes.

Whooping crane

Extirpated

Gone from the wild in one area, but alive somewhere else.

Greater prairie-chicken in Canada

Extinct

Gone from Earth forever.

Passenger pigeon (last one died in 1914)

Canada’s scientists (COSEWIC) also use threatened (likely to become endangered) and special concern (sensitive, worth watching).

Science 7 · LS3 · Declining biodiversity

Key contrast: extirpation can sometimes be undone by bringing a species back from where it still lives; extinction cannot. Even before a species disappears, a shrinking population loses genetic diversity, which links back to LS1. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (status ladder) and Sort It, "Species status" deck. Statuses change; check the Species at Risk Public Registry before sharing.

Science 7 · LS3 · Declining biodiversity · Statuses as known in 2026; check the Species at Risk Public Registry

Statuses are federal (COSEWIC or SARA) as understood in 2026 and should be verified on the Species at Risk Public Registry before sharing. The westslope cutthroat trout status refers to the Alberta population. Swift foxes disappeared from the Canadian prairies by the late 1930s, were listed as extirpated in Canada in 1978, and reintroductions began in 1983. Extension: students pick one species and research what is being done to help it.

LS3.K5 · LS3.S4 · Interventions

Three ways to help

Species relocation

Move animals to restore a population.

2017: Parks Canada moved 16 plains bison from Elk Island to Banff. Swift fox releases on the prairie.

Habitat conservation

Protect land from being cleared.

Elk Island National Park and the Beaver Hills Biosphere; ranchers’ agreements to keep prairie unplowed.

Protecting water

Keep rivers, lakes and wetlands clean and full.

Fencing cattle back from streams, planting willows, inspecting boats for invasive mussels.

Science 7 · LS3 · Caring for ecosystems

Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6, and Lab Bench, Investigation 5 (Stewardship planner: students pick three actions for a ranch creek, a lake with boaters, or a southeast grassland). A strong plan combines an intervention, monitoring and collaboration. Many First Nations have long worked to bring bison back to the land; check wording before adding specifics. Activity: groups choose one place near the school and propose one action of each kind.

Check for understanding

Quick check: show fingers 1, 2 or 3

A. A melting glacier leaves bare gravel. What comes first? 1 spruce trees · 2 lichens and mosses · 3 fireweed

B. A species is gone from Canada but lives in Montana. It is: 1 extinct · 2 endangered · 3 extirpated

C. Moving bison from Elk Island to Banff is: 1 habitat conservation · 2 species relocation · 3 controlled burning

Science 7 · LS3 · Review

Answers: A = 2 (lichens and mosses are pioneer species on bare rock; fireweed needs soil and comes after fire in secondary succession). B = 3 (extirpated). C = 2 (species relocation). Extended response · Take a position

“Every wildfire should be put out as fast as possible.” Do you agree?

Use evidence about disturbance, succession and forest management. Think about both people and the forest.

This is extended response 4 in the lab's Written Review tab, which has a model answer and a key-idea checklist. A strong answer takes a clear position, explains fire as a natural disturbance in the boreal forest, uses evidence such as sealed pine cones and aspen suckers, mentions fuel build-up when every fire is put out, and refers to controlled burning or FireSmart. Fires near communities must be fought; the debate is about remote fires and long-term planning. Use a four-corners or line-up activity before writing. Be sensitive to students who have experienced wildfire evacuations.

Wrap-up

What to remember

Disturbances come from geological processes, extreme weather and human activity.

Succession rebuilds ecosystems: primary with no soil, secondary with soil left.

Biodiversity is declining from habitat destruction, pollution, invasive species and climate change.

Caring is shared: collaboration, monitoring data, relocation, conservation, clean water and smart forestry.
