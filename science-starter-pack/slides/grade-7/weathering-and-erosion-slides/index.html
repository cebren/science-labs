Science 7 · Earth Science · ES1

Weathering, erosion and mass wasting

Earth’s surface is constantly changing due to the dynamic interactions of various geological processes.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Weathering and Erosion Lab

Welcome students to the Earth Science unit. Read the understanding aloud and ask: what does “dynamic” mean? (Always changing.) Tell students that this week they will break rock with frost and roots, follow a grain of sand from a mountain to a delta and study real landslides in Alberta. Lab link: open the Weathering and Erosion Lab on the Learn tab. Students can save their progress in their own browser; nothing is collected. Sensitive topic heads-up for later: the Frank Slide and the 2013 floods involved deaths and lost homes. Some families may have lived through Alberta floods or slides.

Predict

Every spring, new potholes appear on Edmonton roads. Why?

A

Heavy trucks wear the road down all winter.

B

Water gets into cracks, freezes, expands and breaks the road apart.

C

Salt and sand on the road slowly dissolve the pavement.

Choose one and write a sentence explaining your choice. We will come back to it.

Science 7 · ES1 · Hook

Give students one minute to choose and write a reason. Take a quick vote. Answer: B is the main cause. Freeze-thaw (frost wedging) is physical weathering. Traffic (A) helps by breaking the weakened surface, so A is partly right. Road salt can speed some damage, but dissolving pavement (C) is not the main cause. Bridge: rock in nature breaks the same way. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (frost wedging model), and Lab Bench, Investigation 4 (the pothole field note).

Part 1 · ES1.K1 · ES1.K4

A changing planet

What shapes Earth’s surface, and what is the crust made of?

Section opener. Ask: name a place in Alberta that looks different than it did when your grandparents were young. (Riverbanks, glaciers in the Rockies, new gullies, road cuts.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 and 2.

ES1.K1 · A dynamic planet

Building up and wearing down

Internal processes

Happen inside Earth. Powered by Earth’s inner heat. Mostly build up land.

Volcanoes · earthquakes · mountain building, like the Rocky Mountains

External processes

Happen at the surface. Powered by the Sun’s energy (moving water, wind and ice) and by gravity. Mostly wear down land.

Weathering · erosion · glaciers · rivers · landslides

Earth is dynamic: always changing. Most change is too slow to see in one lifetime. Some happens in seconds.

Science 7 · ES1 · A changing planet

Define geological process: a natural process that forms or changes Earth’s rock and land. Discussion: why do the Rockies still stand if external processes wear land down? (Internal processes pushed them up; erosion is slowly wearing them down.) Teacher note (accuracy flag ES1.K1): the lab teaches internal processes as powered by Earth’s inner heat and external processes as powered by the Sun’s energy and gravity. Plate tectonics is not named here (Science 9). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1. Students tap the seven numbered spots and decide internal or external before reading. Sort It deck 3 practises the same split.

ES1.K4 · The crust

Earth’s crust is made of rocks and minerals

Mineral

A natural, non‑living solid with its own chemical makeup and a regular crystal pattern.

Quartz · feldspar · mica · calcite

Rock

A natural solid made of one or more minerals, or other natural materials.

Granite (quartz, feldspar, mica) · limestone (mostly calcite) · sandstone (quartz grains)

Hard minerals like quartz resist weathering. Calcite dissolves in weak acid, so limestone wears away faster.

Science 7 · ES1 · A changing planet

If you have rock samples, pass around granite and limestone with hand lenses. Ask students to count how many kinds of grains they see in granite. Alberta links: granite boulders around Edmonton were carried by glaciers from the Canadian Shield. Much of the Rockies, including Turtle Mountain at the Frank Slide, is limestone that formed in ancient seas. Coal in the Edmonton area formed from ancient plant remains, which is why the definition says “or other natural materials.” Teacher note (accuracy flag ES1.K4): rock types and mineral identification are left to ES2 (rock cycle). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (hand lens model).

Breaking and moving rock

How does solid rock become sand on a beach hundreds of kilometres away?

Section opener. Have students write a first guess to the question, then revisit it after the process chain slide. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 3 to 7, then Lab Bench, Investigation 1.

ES1.K5 · Weathering

Three ways rock breaks down into sediment

Physical

Smaller pieces, same material.

Frost wedging: water freezes in a crack and expands by about 9%. Abrasion: rock grinds on rock.

Chemical

Minerals change into new substances.

Weak acid in rain dissolves limestone. Iron minerals rust. Feldspar turns to clay.

Biological

Caused by living things.

Roots pry cracks open. Lichens release acids. Burrowing animals expose fresh rock.

Weathering happens in place. The pieces, called sediments, have not moved yet.

Science 7 · ES1 · Breaking and moving rock

Return to the pothole hook: the answer was physical weathering by frost wedging. Edmonton has many fall and spring days when the temperature crosses 0 °C. Quick sort aloud: tree roots lifting a sidewalk (biological), orange rust streaks on brick (chemical), sand blasting a rock (physical). Teacher note (accuracy flag ES1.K5): water expands by about 9% when it freezes. Dissolving limestone is chemical weathering; carrying the dissolved minerals away is erosion. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (run freeze-thaw cycles, then tap the nine examples). Sort It deck 1 practises the three kinds.

ES1.K3 · Four linked processes

The journey of one grain of sand

1

Weathering

Frost cracks granite on a Rocky Mountain peak. A quartz grain comes loose.

2

Erosion

Snowmelt runoff picks the grain up and removes it from the slope.

3

Transport

A river carries it hundreds of kilometres, rounding it smooth.

4

Deposition

The river slows at a lake and drops the grain. Layers build a delta.

Each process depends on the one before it. Over millions of years they wear mountains down and fill low places up.

Science 7 · ES1 · Breaking and moving rock

Act it out: four students stand in a line as the four processes and pass a small pebble along, each explaining their job. Discussion: what would happen if there were no weathering? (Little loose sediment to erode.) No deposition? (No deltas or beaches would form.) Teacher note (accuracy flag ES1.K3/K6): many geologists include transport inside erosion. The lab keeps them as separate steps to match the draft, defining erosion as removal and transport as carrying. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (step the grain from peak to delta). Sort It deck 2 practises the processes.

ES1.K2 · Hydrological processes

Water at work, in all three states

Wave action

Waves throw sand and pebbles at the shore and cut a notch until the cliff above falls.

Shorelines of Lesser Slave Lake and Sylvan Lake

Glaciers

Moving ice plucks and scrapes rock, carves U‑shaped valleys and carries boulders far away.

Athabasca Glacier · Big Rock near Okotoks

Groundwater

Slightly acidic water underground dissolves limestone, forming caves and sinkholes.

Rat’s Nest Cave near Canmore

Running water in rivers is a fourth hydrological process. It moves more sediment than anything else on land.

Science 7 · ES1 · Breaking and moving rock

Define hydrological processes: the ways water moves and changes on and under Earth’s surface. Ask: which of these could happen in Edmonton today? (Running water in the North Saskatchewan River, groundwater; the glaciers left more than 10 000 years ago, but their sediment is still under our feet.) Teacher note (accuracy flag ES1.K2): wave action, glacial processes and groundwater flow are taught as named in the draft; running water is added briefly. Big Rock was carried from the Jasper area; the lab mentions once that the name Okotoks comes from the Blackfoot word for rock. Please review that wording. Rat’s Nest Cave is a protected cave entered only with a licensed guide. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (four water processes, tap the labels for Alberta examples).

ES1.K6 · Landforms of erosion

Hoodoos above ground, caves below

Hoodoo

A hard cap rock protects softer sandstone and mudstone below. Rain washes the soft rock around it away.

Drumheller badlands

Cave

Slightly acidic groundwater seeps through cracks in limestone, dissolves it and carries it away.

Rat’s Nest Cave, Grotto Mountain

Erosion removes and carries away weathered sediment. Hard rock resists while soft rock wears away.

Science 7 · ES1 · Breaking and moving rock

Ask: what happens to a hoodoo when its cap falls off? (The soft column wears down quickly.) This is why visitors must never climb on or touch hoodoo caps. Teacher note (accuracy flag ES1.K6): Drumheller hoodoos are described as harder sandstone caps on softer sandstone and mudstone; Rat’s Nest Cave near Canmore is named as a limestone solution cave. Please verify both descriptions. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6. Students grow a hoodoo, remove its cap, then switch to the cave and move the time slider.

Ask students to find one local example for each row. For example, a worn shortcut across the school field (human activity) or a steep riverbank with bare soil (topography and vegetation). Why plants help: leaves soften raindrops before they hit the soil, and roots hold soil together. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7 (hillside erosion model). Change one factor at a time. ES1.K8 · Mass wasting

Rock and soil moving downhill because of gravity

Very fast

Rockfall

Blocks break off a cliff and fall or bounce.

Fast

Landslide

A mass of rock or soil slides along a weak surface.

Slow to fast

Slump

A block slips and rotates on a curved surface.

Fast

Debris flow

Soaked soil, rock and logs flow like wet concrete.

Very slow

Creep

Soil inches down, tilting posts and bending trees.

Mass wasting is a type of erosion. No river or glacier has to carry the material: gravity pulls it down the slope.

Science 7 · ES1 · Gravity takes over

Alberta examples from the lab: rockfall along Highway 93 and Highway 1 in the Rockies (crews scale loose rock and install fences and nets); the Frank Slide (a huge rock avalanche); slumps on the North Saskatchewan River valley banks in Edmonton; the 2013 Cougar Creek debris flood in Canmore; creep on grassy valley slopes. Teacher note (accuracy flag ES1.K8): some textbooks treat mass wasting as separate from erosion, since no carrying agent is needed. The lab follows the draft and calls it a type of erosion driven by gravity. Slump, debris flow and creep are added to the draft’s landslides and rockfalls. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 8 (five kinds of downhill movement).

ES1.K9 · Triggers

What makes a slope fail?

A slope holds while friction and the strength of rock and soil are bigger than gravity’s pull down the slope. A trigger tips the balance.

Water

Heavy rain, snowmelt or rising groundwater adds weight and lowers friction.

Earthquakes

Shaking breaks rock and shakes loose material free.

Human activity

Cutting into the base of a slope, mining, or adding buildings at the top.

Science 7 · ES1 · Gravity takes over

Demo idea: build a sand slope in a tray. Add water a little at a time until it slumps, then repeat with a cut at the base. Ask: which trigger is most common in Edmonton? (Water, together with the river cutting into the base of the valley walls.) Alberta has few strong earthquakes, but they matter in other mountain regions. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 8 for the triggers; Lab Bench, Investigation 2 lets students add triggers to a model slope. Sort It deck 4 sorts triggers and solutions.

Lab Bench · Investigation 2 · ES1.S5

Will this slope hold?

Factor of safety

FS = resisting ÷ driving

Worked example: resisting force 900 kN, driving force 600 kN.

FS = 900 ÷ 600 = 1.50

Above 1, so it holds. It also meets the 1.3 safety target.

Solutions that raise FS

Drainage pipes remove water

Retaining walls support the base

Rock bolts and mesh hold loose rock

Deep‑rooted plants bind soil

Land‑use rules keep homes back from edges

Monitoring gives early warning

Science 7 · ES1 · Gravity takes over

Students do not memorize the formula. Ask: a slope has a resisting force of 800 kN and a driving force of 1000 kN. What happens? (FS = 0.80, below 1, so it fails.) Discussion: which solution would you choose for a house at the top of an Edmonton river valley slope, and why? Match each solution to the trigger it reduces (drainage to water, retaining wall to a cut base, land-use rules to added weight). The tester in the lab uses a simplified, made-up model; engineers usually want FS of at least 1.3 because nobody knows every detail inside a hill. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 2 (add triggers, then solutions, then randomized FS practice). Written Review extended 3 asks for three solutions.

Demo: shake sand, gravel and soil in a clear jar of water and let it settle. The gravel lands first and the water stays cloudy with clay for a long time. Quick check

Three questions

1

Tree roots crack a boulder. Which kind of weathering is this, and why?

2

Put in order: deposition, weathering, transport, erosion.

3

Name two triggers of mass wasting and one solution that reduces the risk.

Science 7 · ES1 · Review

Answers: 1. Biological weathering, because a living thing (the roots) caused the rock to break. (Physical breaking is also acceptable if the student names the living cause.) 2. Weathering, erosion, transport, deposition. 3. Triggers: water (heavy rain, snowmelt, groundwater), earthquakes, human activity (cutting the base, mining, building at the top). Solutions: drainage, retaining walls, rock bolts and mesh, deep-rooted plants, land-use rules, monitoring. Extended response · ES1.S5

Build safely on the edge?

A town wants to build homes at the top of a river valley slope with clay soil. Suggest at least three solutions to reduce the risk of mass wasting and explain how each one works.

A strong answer

· Gives at least three solutions
· Links each one to the trigger it reduces (water, weight or lost support)
· Mentions monitoring or land‑use planning

Science 7 · ES1 · Review

Use as a written task or a debate: half the class argues for building with safeguards, half for keeping the land as a park. Model answer (from the lab): First, keep homes back from the edge, so less weight is added near the slope and people are out of harm’s way. Second, install drainage so rain and snowmelt cannot soak the clay, which keeps friction high. Third, plant deep-rooted vegetation to bind the soil and take up water. A retaining wall at the base and monitoring for slow movement would also help. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 3 (students write, then open the model answer and tick key ideas). Other extended prompts: the grain of sand journey, the Frank Slide impact discussion and the fair test design.
