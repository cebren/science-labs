Ecosystems and their parts

Ecosystems change over time, and they are built in levels.

Section opener for Learn sections 1 and 2. Lab link: Learn tab, sections 1 and 2.

LS1.K1 · Ecosystems are dynamic

A beaver pond over 40 years

Year 0

A small creek runs through an aspen grove.

Year 3

Beavers build a dam. A pond forms and ducks, muskrats and frogs arrive.

Year 10

The pond is full of life. Most aspen near the water is cut.

Year 25

Beavers move away. The dam breaks and the pond drains.

Year 40

A beaver meadow of sedges and willow. Aspen return.

Still an ecosystem the whole time, but its species and characteristics change.

Science 7 · LS1 · Ecosystems and their parts

Ecosystems are dynamic: some changes repeat every year, like Edmonton’s river valley going from green summer forest to snowy winter woods. Others take years, like this beaver pond. Use the lab slider to step through the years. The lab also notes that many First Nations and Métis families have watched the same lands for generations and noticed changes in berry crops, water levels and animal numbers. Review note for Lance: that sentence is general; consider a local community partner before adding specific examples. Lab link: Learn tab, section 1, beaver pond model.

LS1.K2 · Levels of organization

Four levels at Elk Island

LevelMeaningElk Island example
SpeciesOne type of organism whose members can have young that also reproduce.Wood bison
PopulationAll the members of one species living and interacting in one area.All the wood bison in the park’s south area
CommunityAll the interacting populations of different species in that area.Bison, elk, beavers, aspen, sedges, songbirds
EcosystemThe community interacting with abiotic factors.The community plus lake water, soil, sunlight and snow

Science 7 · LS1 · Ecosystems and their parts

Each level is built from the one before it. Living parts are biotic factors; non-living parts are abiotic factors. Elk Island keeps plains bison north of Highway 16 and wood bison south of it, so the two stay apart. Accuracy note for Lance (LS1.K2): the draft defines a species as “the same type of organism”; the lab teaches the fuller definition that members can mate and produce offspring that can also reproduce. Part 2 · LS1.K3, LS1.K4

Biodiversity and stability

Three ways to measure the variety of life, and what it means for an ecosystem to be stable.

Section opener for Learn sections 3 and 4. Lab link: Learn tab, sections 3 and 4.

LS1.K3 · Biodiversity

Three kinds of biodiversity

Genetic

Variation within one species.

Not every snowshoe hare or aspen tree has the same genes.

Species

The number of species (richness) and how evenly individuals are spread (evenness).

Ecosystem

The number of habitat types.

Aspen groves, sloughs, grassland and spruce bog in one landscape.

Science 7 · LS1 · Biodiversity and stability

Biodiversity is the variety of life. Accuracy note for Lance (LS1.K3): the draft places ecosystem diversity “in an ecosystem” (number of habitat types). Many sources use the term for the variety of whole ecosystems across a region. The lab teaches the curriculum wording and notes the wider use with Alberta’s six natural regions: Grassland, Parkland, Foothills, Rocky Mountain, Boreal Forest and Canadian Shield. Lab link: Learn tab, section 3; Sort It tab, Kinds of biodiversity deck.

These are the lab’s made-up model meadows, not field data. Ask: which meadow is most diverse? (C: most species and even.) Which is least? (B: the invasive grass dominates.) LS1.K4 · Stability

A stable ecosystem keeps three things over time

Structure

Its layers and parts, and who eats whom.

Biodiversity

The variety of species it holds.

Processes

Jobs like photosynthesis, decomposition and filtering water.

A stable ecosystem still bends during a drought or hard winter, then returns to about the same condition, like a ball rolling back in a deep bowl.

Science 7 · LS1 · Biodiversity and stability

Ecosystem stability is the capacity of an ecosystem to maintain its structure, biodiversity and processes over time. Use the ball in a bowl model: a deep bowl lets the ball roll back after a push; a shallow bowl lets it roll out. Try small, medium and large pushes. Lab link: Learn tab, section 4, “Give the ecosystem a push”.

Niches and adaptations

How many species share one place without running out of food.

Section opener for Learn sections 5 and 6. Lab link: Learn tab, sections 5 and 6.

LS1.K5, LS1.K8 · Niches

Five warblers share one spruce tree

WarblerMain feeding zone
Cape MayVery top and outer tips
BlackburnianOuter branches, upper tree
Black-throated greenOuter branches, middle of the tree
Bay-breastedMiddle of the tree, near the trunk (blue band)
Yellow-rumpedLow branches, the ground and the air

Science 7 · LS1 · Niches and adaptations

A habitat is where a species lives. An ecological niche is its role in that habitat: what it eats, where and when it feeds, where it nests and what conditions it needs. Ecologist Robert MacArthur studied warblers that all eat insects in spruce trees; each fed mostly in a different part of the tree. All five also nest in Alberta’s boreal forest. Different niches mean less overlap, so competition is reduced (LS1.K8). In the lab, tick “Make every warbler feed in the same zone” and watch competition jump. Accuracy note for Lance (LS1.K5): the lab keeps the draft’s “role in their habitat” and adds what the role includes, and separates niche from habitat. Lab link: Learn tab, section 5.

LS1.K7 · Adaptations

Features that fit a species to its environment

Structural

Canada lynx

Wide, furry paws for chasing hares through deep snow.

Behavioural

Ground squirrel

Hibernates for more than half the year.

Physiological

Wood frog

Survives freezing with sugar that acts like antifreeze.

Science 7 · LS1 · Niches and adaptations

An adaptation is a feature that makes a species well suited to a specific environment. Structural is a body part or shape; behavioural is something the organism does; physiological is how the body works inside. Adaptations also shape a niche: the lynx’s paws let it hunt hares in deep snow where other predators struggle. More examples in the lab: snowshoe hare, lodgepole pine (resin-sealed cones opened by fire; Alberta’s provincial tree), moose. The ground squirrel is Richardson’s ground squirrel. Lab link: Learn tab, section 6 (Alberta species field guide); Lab Bench tab, Bench 3 (adaptation match).

LS1.K8 · The key idea

Different niches mean less competition.

That is how five ducks can share one slough, and five warblers can share one tree.

Pause here and have students explain the idea in their own words to a partner. Then ask: what would happen if every warbler fed in the same zone? (Competition would be high; some birds would go hungry and one species might be pushed out.) Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Bench 4, find the duck pair that overlaps most and least.

Part 4 · LS1.K9, LS1.K10

Living together

Predators, partners and parasites, then the Lab Bench.

Section opener for Learn sections 7 and 8 and the Lab Bench investigations. Lab link: Learn tab, sections 7 and 8.

LS1.K9 · Predation

Predators help keep the balance

With lynx

Hares rise, then lynx rise and catch more hares. Willow shrubs stay healthy. Numbers swing, but the system holds together.

Without lynx

Hares climb high, eat the willow down, then starve and crash. Bigger swings, and the plants take a heavy hit.

Hudson’s Bay Company fur records show hares and lynx rising and falling in a cycle of about 10 years.

Science 7 · LS1 · Living together

Predation is when a predator hunts and eats its prey. Predators keep prey populations from growing so large that they eat up their food supply. Both food supply and predators drive the hare and lynx cycle. Run the lab model with lynx, then without, and compare the willow line. The model is a simplified teaching model; its numbers are model settings, not field data. Lab link: Learn tab, section 7 (willow, hares and lynx).

LS1.K10 · Symbiosis

Three kinds of symbiosis

KindSignsMeaningAlberta example
Mutualism+ / +Both species benefit.Bumblebees and saskatoon flowers
Commensalism+ / 0One benefits; the other is not affected.Cowbirds catch insects stirred up by bison
Parasitism+ / −One benefits; the host is harmed.Winter ticks drinking moose blood

Symbiosis is a close, long-term relationship between two different species.

Science 7 · LS1 · Living together

Plus means the species benefits, zero means it is not affected and minus means it is harmed. A parasite usually harms its host without killing it quickly, because it needs the host alive. Accuracy note for Lance (LS1.K10): the lab notes that predation is a separate interaction, not usually counted as symbiosis, because predator and prey do not live together for a long time. More examples in the lab: lichen (fungus and alga), great horned owls in old magpie nests, dwarf mistletoe on pines. Lab link: Learn tab, section 8; Sort It tab, Species interactions deck.

LS1.S2 · Lab Bench 2

Quadrat survey: measure biodiversity

Method

Lay square frames in random spots and count what is inside. Record richness and Simpson’s index.

D = 1 − Σ (n ÷ N)²

Worked example

Slough edge: 6 cattails, 3 sedges, 1 bulrush. N = 10.

0.36 + 0.09 + 0.01 = 0.46
D = 1 − 0.46 = 0.54

D runs from 0 to 1. Closer to 1 means more diverse.

Science 7 · LS1 · Lab Bench

Ecologists cannot count every plant, so they sample with quadrats placed at random to avoid bias. n is the count of one species; N is the total of all individuals; Σ means add up for every species. Show the formula as a tool; students are not asked to memorize it. Accuracy note for Lance: Simpson’s index is brief context to support LS1.S2. Work the example: (6 ÷ 10)² = 0.36, (3 ÷ 10)² = 0.09, (1 ÷ 10)² = 0.01. Then students survey four Alberta ecosystems in the lab and compare their results table. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Bench 2.

LS1.S5 · Lab Bench 5

Drought test: does biodiversity help?

Change

Number of plant species in each plot.

Event

A drought hits in years 4 and 5. Each species copes differently.

Compare

Plant cover lost, and how fast 20 plots recover.

Write a claim, your evidence (numbers from the test) and your reasoning.

Science 7 · LS1 · Lab Bench

The model is based on the pattern from David Tilman’s long experiment in Minnesota grasslands: plots with more plant species lost less plant cover in a drought and recovered faster. Run 20 plots with 1 species, then with 8 or 12, and compare. The model is simplified; its numbers are model settings, not field data. Return to the opening prediction: the native fescue grassland is the better bet. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Bench 5, then the claim, evidence and reasoning boxes.

Quick check

Three questions

1. Is “all the wood bison in Elk Island’s south area” a population or a community? Why?

2. Cowbirds catch insects stirred up by grazing bison. Which kind of symbiosis is this?

3. Two meadows each have 4 species. Why can one still be less diverse?

Science 7 · LS1 · Quick check

Answers. 1: A population, because it is all the members of one species in one area. A community would include the other species too. 2: Commensalism: the cowbirds benefit and the bison are not affected. 3: Evenness. If one species dominates, like invasive smooth brome in Meadow B, the meadow is less diverse even with the same richness. Lab link: Quiz tab for a full 10-question round.

Extended response

What if the lynx disappeared?

All lynx vanish from a boreal forest.

Predict how snowshoe hares, willow shrubs and other species would change over several years. Explain your reasoning using the idea of ecosystem stability.

Science 7 · LS1 · Extended response

Key ideas for a strong answer: fewer hares are eaten, so the hare population grows quickly; extra hares eat more willow and shrubs in winter; when food runs out, many hares starve and the population crashes; animals that use willow, such as moose and songbirds, lose food and shelter; other predators like foxes or owls may eat more hares, but swings are likely bigger. This shows predation helps maintain stability. Have students write first, then compare with the model answer. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 2.
