Science 7 · Matter: Pure Substances and Mixtures · M1

Classifying and separating matter

Matter can be classified based on physical properties and separated according to its particles.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Matter Sorter Lab

Welcome students to the matter unit. Read the understanding statement aloud and ask: what do you think “pure” means in everyday life? Collect two or three ideas and tell students that scientists use the word in a much stricter way. Lab link: open the Matter Sorter Lab on the Learn tab before Lesson 1 so students can follow along on their own devices.

Learning goals

Science 7 · M1 · Predict

Give students one minute to predict alone, then share with a partner. Do not give answers yet; come back to this slide after Lesson 1. Answers: tap water is a mixture (dissolved minerals and chlorine); a 24 karat gold ring is close to pure gold (an element); air is a mixture of gases; table salt is a pure substance (the compound sodium chloride). Many students will say tap water is pure because it looks clear. That is the misconception to address: pure means one type of particle, not clean or clear. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

Part 1 · Lessons 1 and 2

Sorting matter

Every sample of matter is either a pure substance or a mixture. The particles decide which.

Section opener. Remind students that matter is anything that has mass and takes up space, and that all matter is made of particles far too small to see. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 and 2.

M1.K1 · M1.K2 · M1.K5

Two big groups of matter

Pure substance

Only one type of particle. Physical techniques cannot separate it.

Examples: copper wire, distilled water, oxygen gas, table salt

Mixture

Two or more types of particles. Physical techniques can separate them.

Examples: salt water, air, tap water, trail mix

Physical techniques, like filtering or boiling, do not change what the particles are.

Science 7 · M1 · Sorting matter

Key idea: one type of particle means pure substance; two or more types means mixture. Return to the prediction slide and sort the four samples together. Stress that “pure” in science is about particles, not about being clean or natural. Discussion prompt: is bottled spring water pure? (No, it has dissolved minerals.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 (zoom in on the particles). Have students tap each sample and read the badge.

M1.K2 · M1.S2

Drawing matter as particles

Copper: an element

One kind of atom, packed in rows

Water: a compound

Every particle is the same joined group of atoms

Salt water: a mixture

Two types of particle, evenly mixed

A compound breaks into elements only by a chemical change, never by a physical technique.

Science 7 · M1 · Sorting matter

Model these drawings on the board: circles stand for particles, joined circles for particles made of joined atoms. Ask students to draw oxygen gas (pairs of joined atoms, far apart) and air (several kinds, far apart). Accuracy flag M1.K2: the draft says a pure substance has “only one type of particle”. The lab teaches this precisely: an element has one kind of atom, and a compound has one kind of particle made of two or more kinds of joined atoms. It also adds that compounds can be broken down by chemical change (for example passing electricity through water), so students do not conclude that pure substances can never be broken down. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (try to separate it) and Lab Bench, Investigation 2 (particle picture match).

Part 2 · Lessons 3 and 4

Identifying pure substances

Each pure substance has its own set of physical properties, like a fingerprint.

Section opener. Ask: if you found an unlabelled shiny grey metal, how could you find out what it is without changing it? Collect ideas (colour, shine, weigh it, magnet, melt it). Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 3 and 4.

Define each property in student language: appearance (colour and lustre, which means shine), density, melting point, freezing point and boiling point. M1.K3

Melting point equals freezing point

0 °C

Pure water melts at 0 °C and freezes at 0 °C. It is the same change, run in opposite directions.

Sharp

A pure substance melts and boils at one sharp temperature. Mixtures usually change over a range.

A sharp melting point is one clue that a sample is pure.

Science 7 · M1 · Identifying pure substances

Accuracy flag M1.K3: the draft lists freezing point and melting point as separate properties. The lab teaches that for a pure substance they are the same temperature, and that mixtures usually melt or boil over a range. Demonstration idea: slide the lab’s temperature slider to exactly 0 °C for water to show “solid and liquid together”. Discussion prompt: why does salt on an icy sidewalk help? (Salt water freezes below 0 °C, because it is a mixture.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3.

M1.K4 · M1.S3

Density is mass per unit volume

d = m ÷ V

mass in grams (g), volume in cm³ or mL. 1 mL = 1 cm³.

Worked example

An aluminum block has a mass of 54 g and a volume of 20 cm³.

d = 54 g ÷ 20 cm³
d = 2.7 g/cm³

You do not need to memorize the formula. You need to know what each letter means.

Science 7 · M1 · Identifying pure substances

Walk through the worked example with the units written each time. Ask students to check the answer against the table: 2.7 g/cm³ matches aluminum. Try a second one together: a copper fitting with mass 89.6 g and volume 10 cm³ (8.96 g/cm³). Note that density changes a little with temperature, which is why ice floats on water. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4, then Lab Bench, Investigation 3 for randomized practice.

The big idea about density

An iron paper clip and an iron beam have the same density.

Bigger samples have more mass and more volume, so mass ÷ volume stays the same. That is why density can identify a substance.

Pause here for think, pair, share: why is density more useful than mass for identifying a substance? Expected answer: mass depends on how much you have; density does not. Both samples are about 7.9 g/cm³. Lab link: Written Review, extended response 3.

Part 3 · Lessons 5, 6 and 7

Mixtures and separating them

Each separation technique uses a difference between the parts: size, density, magnetism or boiling point.

Section opener. Quick poll: name a mixture you separated at home this week (draining pasta, a coffee filter, picking out raisins). Each one used a physical property. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 5 to 7.

M1.K5 · M1.K6 · M1.S4

Two kinds of mixture

Heterogeneous

Not the same all the way through. A mechanical mixture has parts you can see.

Trail mix, gravel, granite, oil and vinegar dressing, muddy water

Homogeneous

Looks the same all the way through. A solution has particles spread evenly.

Salt water, vinegar, clear apple juice, air, brass

Quick test: could one scoop hold different stuff than another scoop? Then it is heterogeneous.

Science 7 · M1 · Mixtures and separation

Use the “scoop test”. Surprise examples: air and brass are solutions too, one a gas and one a solid. Accuracy flag M1.K6: the lab teaches “mechanical mixture” as a heterogeneous mixture with visible parts, and includes suspensions such as muddy water as heterogeneous, since the mud settles out. Discussion prompt: is milk heterogeneous or homogeneous? Accept reasoned answers; the lab’s Sort It deck treats milk only as a mixture. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (mixture magnifier) and Sort It, heterogeneous or homogeneous deck.

Magnetic separation works only on magnetic materials such as iron, nickel and steel. Ask: would a magnet pull aluminum cans out of recycling? (No. Aluminum is not magnetic.) The Alberta examples are described in general terms only, as in the lab; check local details before adding specifics, and consider inviting a guest from a local recycling or water treatment facility. M1.K8

Distillation separates solutions

1

Heat

The part with the lowest boiling point boils and becomes a gas.

2

Condense

The gas cools in the condenser and turns back into a liquid.

3

Collect

The distillate drips into a clean container.

Salt water: pure water (boils at 100 °C) is collected. Salt (boils at 1465 °C) stays in the flask. Filtering cannot do this, because dissolved salt passes through filter paper.

Science 7 · M1 · Mixtures and separation

Run the lab’s distillation model live: set the burner below 100 °C and heat (nothing collects), then at 100 °C (pure water collects). Then switch to water and ethanol at about 80 °C. Accuracy flag M1.K8: the lab teaches distillation for a solid dissolved in a liquid and for two liquids. One distillation of water and ethanol gives a liquid richer in ethanol, not pure ethanol, because their boiling points are close. Evaporation is not taught as a separate technique. Alberta link: refineries near Edmonton use fractional distillation to separate crude oil. Safety: never boil a flask dry. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7.

Lab Bench · Investigation 1 · M1.S1 · M1.S3

Mystery substance

Measurements (practice data)

Appearance: grey, shiny metal
Mass on the balance: 47.2 g
Water level: 50.0 mL, then 56.0 mL
Not melted at 1200 °C

Worked answer

V = 56.0 − 50.0 = 6.0 cm³
d = m ÷ V
d = 47.2 ÷ 6.0 = 7.87 g/cm³

Iron. Density, appearance and a melting point above 1200 °C all match.

Science 7 · M1 · Lab Bench

The measurements are made up for practice, like the lab’s samples. Model the water displacement method: the rise in water level is the volume of the sample, and 1 mL equals 1 cm³. Ask: why check more than one property? (Two substances could have similar densities; several matching properties is stronger evidence.) Students then do five samples on their own. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 1 (mystery substance). Students record each measurement in the notes panel, calculate density and choose an identity.

Lab Bench · Investigation 4 · M1.S5

Separation challenge

One tub holds gravel, iron filings, sand, table salt and water. The salt has dissolved. Get all five back.

STEP 1 · SIZE

Sieve

Gravel stays on the mesh.

STEP 2 · MAGNETISM

Magnet

Iron filings stick to it.

STEP 3 · SIZE

Filter

Sand stays on the paper; salt water passes.

STEP 4 · BOILING POINT

Distill

Water is collected; salt stays in the flask.

Order matters: gravel would tear the filter, and iron would stay mixed with the sand.

Science 7 · M1 · Lab Bench

Have students attempt the challenge in the lab before showing this slide, or cover the cards and reveal one at a time. The lab gives feedback on wrong orders, for example trying to filter while gravel is still in the tub, or settling and decanting, which leaves wet sand mixed with water. Discussion prompt: could the magnet go first? (Yes, steps 1 and 2 can swap.) Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 4 (separation challenge).

Quick check

Three questions

1

Is brass a pure substance, a heterogeneous mixture or a homogeneous mixture? Explain using particles.

2

A liquid has a mass of 39.5 g and a volume of 50 mL. What is its density, and which substance could it be?

3

You filter salt water. What passes through the filter paper, and why?

Science 7 · M1 · Review

Answers. 1: Brass is a homogeneous mixture, a solid solution. It has two types of particle (copper and zinc atoms) spread evenly, so every piece is the same. 2: d = m ÷ V = 39.5 ÷ 50 = 0.79 g/mL, which matches ethanol. 3: Both the water and the dissolved salt pass through, because dissolved salt particles are small enough to fit through the paper. Filtration cannot separate a solution; distillation can. Use mini whiteboards or exit slips. Lab link: Quiz tab for a full ten-question round.

Extended response

Two shiny grey cubes

You find two shiny grey cubes. Design an investigation to decide if they are the same pure substance.

Say what you would measure, how you would calculate density, and how you would use the results to reach a conclusion.

Science 7 · M1 · Review

From the lab’s Written Review, extended response 2. Key ideas for a strong answer: measure mass on a balance and volume by water displacement or by measuring the sides; calculate density with d = m ÷ V; compare with reference values (2.70 g/cm³ is aluminum, 7.87 g/cm³ is iron); check at least one more property, such as whether a magnet attracts it or its melting point if equipment allows; explain that different densities mean different substances even if they look alike. Extension debate: “Two samples with the same density must be the same substance.” Agree or disagree? (Disagree: several properties together are stronger evidence.)
