Part 1 of 4

Force and work

What a force is, and when a force does work.

This part covers F2.K1 and F2.K2. Students need these two ideas before machines make sense, because a machine is judged by the force it needs and the work it does. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 and 2.

F2.K1 · Force

A force has a size and a direction

A force is a push or a pull, measured in newtons (N).

“200 N” is not enough. Say “200 N to the right”. That is why we draw forces as arrows.

Weight on Earth

mass × 9.8 N/kg

20 kg crate: 20 × 9.8 = 196 N, down

Orange arrow: a push of 200 N to the right

Blue arrow: the crate’s weight, 196 N down

Science 7 · F2 · Force and work

Key idea: a force needs a size in newtons and a direction. A small apple weighs about 1 N. Ask: what does a longer arrow mean? (A bigger force.) What would an arrow pointing left mean? (A push or pull to the left.) The weight formula is context so students can turn kilograms into newtons; they do not need to memorize it. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1. Let students drag the push slider and change the direction chips.

F2.K2 · Work

Work means a force moves something

W

work, in joules (J)

F

force, in newtons (N)

d

distance moved in the direction of the force, in metres (m)

W = F × d   ·   1 J = 1 N moving 1 m

Work needs two things: a force, and movement in the same direction as that force.

Science 7 · F2 · Force and work

Introduce W = F × d symbolically; students use it, they do not memorize it. Stress the phrase “in the direction of the force”. Worked example to do aloud: a farmer lifts a 250 N hay bale 1.2 m. W = 250 N × 1.2 m = 300 J. Ask: what happens to the work if the force doubles? (It doubles.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2, where work is shown as the area of a rectangle with force as height and distance as width.

F2.K2 · F2.S2 · Work or no work?

No movement, no work

Work is done

Lift a 50 N backpack 1 m onto a shelf
50 N × 1 m = 50 J

Push a cart with 80 N for 6 m
80 N × 6 m = 480 J

No work is done

Push hard on a wall that does not move
distance 0 m, so 0 J

Carry a box at the same height down a hall
holding force is up, motion is sideways: 0 J

Science 7 · F2 · Force and work

Students often say pushing a wall is hard work. Explain that your muscles use energy, but no work is done on the wall because it does not move. The carrying example is the trickiest: the upward holding force does no work because the box does not move up or down. Precise wording used in the lab (F2.K2): work counts only the distance moved in the direction of the force. Lab link: Sort It tab, “Is work done?” deck, then Lab Bench, Investigation 2 for calculations.

Part 2 of 4

The six simple machines

Six machines with no motor, and what each one changes about your force.

This part covers F2.K3. Return to the list of helpful tools students made on the cover slide and sort them into the six machines as you go. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (machine gallery).

F2.K3 · Simple machines

Six machines, one job: change your force

Inclined plane

A sloped surface. School entrance ramp, moving truck ramp.

Lever

A bar that turns on a fulcrum. Crowbar, seesaw, pump jack beam.

Wheel and axle

A big wheel turns a thin axle. Doorknob, steering wheel.

Screw

An inclined plane wrapped around a post. Jar lid, ice fishing auger.

Wedge

A moving inclined plane. Axe for firewood, knife, doorstop.

Pulley

A grooved wheel with a rope. School flagpole, window blinds.

You push or pull with an input force. The machine acts on the load with an output force.

Science 7 · F2 · Simple machines

Point out that three of the six are really the same idea: a screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a post, and a wedge is an inclined plane that moves. Define input force (the effort) and output force (the force on the load) here, because every later slide uses them. Discussion: which of these have you used this week? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3. Students can tap each machine in the gallery for Alberta examples and its ideal MA formula (extension only). Sort It tab, “Which simple machine?” deck for practice.

Part 3 of 4

Trading force for distance

Work in, work out, and how to measure the help a machine gives.

This part covers F2.K4 and F2.K5, and skills S2 and S4. Remind students of their prediction from the hook slide: we are about to check it. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4, 5 and 6.

F2.K4 · F2.S4 · Work in, work out

Less force, same work

Lift straight up

600 N × 1 m

= 600 J

Push up a 4 m ramp

150 N × 4 m

= 600 J

Input work = input force × input distance. Output work = output force × output distance. In an ideal machine, with no friction, they are equal.

Science 7 · F2 · Trading force for distance

Reveal the hook answer. The ramp cuts the force from 600 N to 150 N, but you push 4 times as far, so the work is the same 600 J. Accuracy flag F2.K4: the draft says input work equals output work. The lab teaches this as true for an ideal, frictionless machine only. Real machines always put out a little less work than goes in, which the next slide covers. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4. Have students change the ramp length and watch the input force drop while input work stays at 600 J.

F2.K4 · Big idea

A machine changes the force and the distance. It never makes extra work.

Real machines have friction, which turns some input work into heat. So output work is always a little less than input work.

efficiency = output work ÷ input work × 100%

This is the slide to anchor F2.S4: explain why the amount of work does not change when a simple machine is used. Precise wording: a machine never increases the work. In an ideal machine input work equals output work; in a real machine you put in slightly more work than you get out. Example from the lab: a real 3 m ramp needs about 255 N, so input work is about 765 J for 600 J of output work, an efficiency near 78% (simulated practice numbers). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4, “Real ramp (friction)” button.

F2.K5 · Mechanical advantage

How many times a machine multiplies your force

MA = output force ÷ input force

ideal MA = input distance ÷ output distance

Less input force means your force moves over a longer distance.

Crowbar: 50 N in, 400 N out. MA = 400 ÷ 50 = 8.

Rock 400 N · load arm 0.5 m · effort arm 1.5 m

Push: about 133 N. MA = 1.5 ÷ 0.5 = 3. Your hand moves 30 cm to lift the rock 10 cm.

Science 7 · F2 · Trading force for distance

Define the lever parts: fulcrum, effort arm, load arm. Accuracy flag F2.K5: the draft describes mechanical advantage only as reducing the input force. The lab defines MA as output force ÷ input force, notes that a fixed pulley has MA 1 (direction only), and that some tools such as a canoe paddle or snow shovel have MA below 1: they need more force but move the load farther and faster. Check: work in = 133 N × 0.3 m ≈ 40 J; work out = 400 N × 0.1 m = 40 J. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5. Slide the fulcrum toward the hand end to show MA dropping below 1.

The fixed pulley row shows a machine that changes only direction (MA 1). Ask: why would a farmer choose the 4 segment setup even though they must pull 4 m of rope? Part 4 of 4

Complex machines

Simple machines working together, and how their help multiplies.

This part covers F2.K6 and F2.K7, then moves into the Lab Bench investigations. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7.

F2.K6 · Complex machines

Two or more simple machines together

Wheelbarrow

Lever: handles and tray, with the axle as the fulcrum. Lifts a heavy load with less force.

Wheel and axle: lets it roll, which cuts friction.

Hand can opener

Levers: the handles squeeze the blade onto the rim.

Wheel and axle: the turning key.

Wedge: the sharp cutting wheel.

Science 7 · F2 · Complex machines

F2.K6 names the wheelbarrow and can opener, so both are here. If you have a can opener, pass it around and have students find each simple machine. Discussion: scissors, a bicycle and a hand crank pencil sharpener are also complex machines; what simple machines do they combine? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7 (tap the wheelbarrow parts) and Sort It tab, “Simple or complex?” deck.

F2.K7 · MA of a complex machine

In a chain, multiply the MAs

Your input

50 N

Lever, MA 3

150 N

Pulleys, MA 2

300 N

MA = MA₁ × MA₂ = 3 × 2 = 6

The output force of the first machine becomes the input force of the second, so each one multiplies the force again.

Science 7 · F2 · Complex machines

Accuracy flag F2.K7: the product rule works when the machines are linked in a chain, so the output of one drives the next. In a wheelbarrow, the wheel mainly reduces rolling friction rather than multiplying force, so the wheelbarrow’s MA comes mainly from its lever. MA values here are made up for practice. Check: what input force lifts a 1200 N load with this machine? (1200 ÷ 6 = 200 N.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 7 chain builder, and Lab Bench, Investigation 3 chain practice.

Lab Bench · Investigation 1 · F2.S2 · F2.S4

Does a longer ramp save work?

Pull a 600 N crate up to a 1 m platform with a spring scale. Output work is always 600 N × 1 m = 600 J. Example data, made up for practice:

Ramp lengthForceInput workOutput workEfficiency
2 m350 N700 J600 J86%
3 m255 N765 J600 J78%
4 m210 N840 J600 J71%
6 m160 N960 J600 J63%

Worked: 255 N × 3 m = 765 J · 600 ÷ 765 × 100% ≈ 78%

Science 7 · F2 · Lab Bench

Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 1 (Ramp trials). Students choose a length, press Pull the crate and build their own table; the simulation adds friction and small random variation, so their numbers will differ a little from these made-up values. Discussion: as the ramp gets longer, the force goes down. Input work never drops below 600 J; it is always a little more because of friction. On this simulated ramp the friction work grows with length, so efficiency falls for long ramps. Ask: so why build long ramps? (A smaller force is easier and safer, for example for a wheelchair.) This slide supports F2.S4 and the F2.K4 accuracy flag.

Lab Bench · Investigation 4 · F2.S1 · F2.S3

Work at the school entrance ramp

A person in a wheelchair (900 N together) goes up a 6 m ramp to a door 0.5 m above the sidewalk. Made-up numbers, close to real sizes.

Step 1 · Output work

900 N × 0.5 m = 450 J

Step 2 · Ideal push

450 J ÷ 6 m = 75 N

Step 3 · Compare

75 N, not 900 N

The ramp (ideal MA 900 ÷ 75 = 12) makes the force far smaller. The work is still 450 J, and a little more with friction.

Quick check

Three questions

1

A student pushes a toboggan with 60 N for 25 m along a flat trail. How much work is done?

2

A pulley system has 4 rope segments holding a 400 N load. What is the ideal input force, and how much rope do you pull to lift it 1 m?

3

A lever with an MA of 3 drives a pulley system with an MA of 4. What is the MA of the complex machine?

Science 7 · F2 · Quick check

Answers. 1: W = F × d = 60 N × 25 m = 1500 J. 2: ideal input force = 400 N ÷ 4 = 100 N; you pull 4 m of rope; work in = 100 N × 4 m = 400 J = work out. 3: MA = 3 × 4 = 12. Use mini whiteboards or fingers for question 3. Lab link: Quiz tab for a 10 question round drawn from a bank of 24.

Debate · Written Review, extended response 2

“Machines save work.”

Do you agree? Use a ramp, lever or pulley example with numbers to explain.

Give students two minutes to pick a side and one minute to find a numerical example in the lab. Model answer: disagree. Machines save force, not work. A pulley system with 3 supporting segments lifts a 240 N hay bale with 80 N, but you pull 3 m of rope to lift it 1 m: 80 N × 3 m = 240 J in, 240 N × 1 m = 240 J out. With friction, you actually do a little more work than without the machine. Machines help by lowering the force or changing its direction. Key ideas checklist: clear position; machines change force, not work; a numerical example; friction makes real input work slightly larger; how machines still help.
