Science 7 · Energy: Heat and Temperature · EN1

Energy transfer in systems

Transfer of energy can be used to explain change in objects and systems.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Energy Accounts Lab

Welcome students. Today’s big idea: scientists keep track of energy the way a careful bookkeeper tracks money. Count what comes in, count what goes out, and the difference tells you how the system changed. Have the Energy Accounts Lab open on the projector: Learn tab, Lesson 1. This unit opens Energy: Heat and Temperature; EN2 (temperature and heating) follows.

Learning goals

By the end of this lesson

EN1.S1

I can illustrate the transfer of energy into, within and out of a system.

EN1.S2

I can analyze changes in energy when work is done.

Science 7 · EN1 · Learning goals

Read both goals aloud. Ask students to rate themselves 1 to 4 on each goal now; they will rate again on the wrap-up slide. Point out that “system” and “work” have special science meanings that we will build today. Lab link: Coverage tab lists both skills and where the lab teaches them.

Predict

You pull a toboggan across a flat field, then let go. It slides a little and stops. Where did the energy go?

A. It was used up and no longer exists.

B. It is still inside the toboggan.

C. It moved somewhere else.

Science 7 · EN1 · Hook

Think, pair, share. Have students vote with fingers (1, 2 or 3). Do not give the answer yet. The answer is C: friction transferred the energy to the snow and air, warming them a tiny bit, and some became sound. We come back to this on the toaster and bench slides. Common idea to listen for: energy is “used up”. Keep a note of who says this and revisit it on the debate slide.

Part 1 · EN1.K1

What energy is

A number we keep track of, measured in joules.

Section opener. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 (the joule ladder).

Lesson 1 · EN1.K1

Energy is a quantity, not a substance

Not a stuff

You cannot pour, hold or weigh energy. It is not matter.

A number

Measured in joules (J). It tells how much work could be done.

Like money

A bank balance goes up when money comes in and down when it goes out.

1 kJ = 1000 J  ·  1 MJ = 1 000 000 J

Science 7 · EN1 · What energy is

Key move: separate everyday talk (“I’m full of energy”, “the battery is out of juice”) from science talk. Ask: can you put energy in a jar by itself? No. It is a number we calculate. Accuracy flag (EN1.K1): the draft says energy “indicates the amount of work that can be done”. Use this as a helpful description, but strictly energy is the capacity to do work or to heat, and not all of a system’s energy can be turned into work. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

Part 2 · EN1.K2

Systems and surroundings

Before you track energy, choose what you are studying.

Section opener. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 (move the boundary).

Lesson 2 · EN1.K2

Four words for any system

System

The part of the world you choose to study.

Components

Its parts, like the water and element in a kettle.

Interactions

How the parts act on each other: pushing, rubbing, warming.

Surroundings

Everything outside the system boundary.

Science 7 · EN1 · Systems and surroundings

The system boundary is an imaginary line you draw. Ask: in a hockey game, what could be the system? (The puck, the puck and ice, a player.) Have students name components and one interaction for a bike: rider’s legs push pedals, chain turns wheel. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2.

Lesson 2 · EN1.K2

Move the boundary, change the story

SystemEnergy inEnergy out
Kettle and waterElectrical energy from the wall wiresHeating of the kitchen air, a little sound
Just the waterHeating from the hot elementHeating of the kettle body and air, escaping steam

The element heating the water is within the first system but into the second.

Science 7 · EN1 · Systems and surroundings

Demonstrate with the lab model: Learn tab, Lesson 2, Electric kettle, switch between “Kettle and water” and “Just the water”. Then try the River valley cyclist: with rider and bike, the leg push is within; with just the bike, the push comes into the system. Takeaway: always state your system first.

Part 3 · EN1.K3

Transfers cause change

When energy moves, something speeds up, slows down, rises or warms.

Section opener. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (pull the toboggan).

Lesson 3 · EN1.K3

Work transfers energy

W = F × d

work (J) = force (N) × distance moved in the direction of the force (m)

Worked example

You pull a toboggan with 60 N for 10 m.

W = 60 N × 10 m
W = 600 J

600 J moved into the toboggan and the snow.

Science 7 · EN1 · Transfers cause change

Students do not need to memorize the formula; W = F × d comes from the Forces unit (F2.K2) and is used here as context. Stress “in the direction of the force”. The 600 J sped the toboggan up and warmed the snow under it a little through friction. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3, move the sliders; then Lab Bench, Investigation 1 for randomized practice.

Lesson 3 · EN1.K3

No movement, no work

WORK IS DONE

Lifting a backpack onto a shelf

A 40 N pack lifted 1.5 m: 40 N × 1.5 m = 60 J.

NO WORK IS DONE

Pushing a wall that does not move

200 N × 0 m = 0 J. You get tired, but the wall gains no energy.

Science 7 · EN1 · Transfers cause change

Quick vote on more examples: holding a heavy box still (no work on the box), kicking a ball (work), a crane lifting a beam (work). Students often feel that effort means work. Explain that the muscles are using energy inside the body, but no work is done on the wall. Lab link: Sort It tab, “Is work done?” deck.

Lesson 3 · EN1.K3

A change means energy moved.

It can move by work, a force moving something, or by heating, from hotter to colder.

Accuracy flag (EN1.K3): the draft says changes “indicate that work has been done”. The lab teaches that a change shows energy was transferred, by work or by heating. Example: a snowball melting in your glove changed because of heating, not a push. Heating is named briefly so EN2 does not contradict this lesson. Lab link: Sort It tab, “Work or heating?” deck.

Part 4 · EN1.K4 · EN1.S1

Keep the energy account

Count what comes in. Count what goes out.

Section opener. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4 and 5.

Lesson 4 · EN1.K4

Change = energy in − energy out

ΔE = E in − E out

Gains

Kettle water: 900 J in, 100 J out. ΔE = +800 J.

Loses

Cocoa outside: 0 J in, 400 J out. ΔE = −400 J.

Stays the same

600 J in, 600 J out. ΔE = 0, but energy still flows.

Science 7 · EN1 · Energy accounts

Δ (delta) means “change in”. Energy is never created or destroyed, only moved, which is why the account always balances. Example numbers are made up for practice. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4 (the energy account), try each preset and then drag the sliders. Practice: Lab Bench, Investigation 2.

Lesson 4 · EN1.K4

A house in an Edmonton January

IN EACH MINUTE

40 kJ

from the furnace

OUT EACH MINUTE

40 kJ

through walls and windows

CHANGE

0 kJ

temperature stays steady

What happens if the furnace shuts off? What if a window is left open?

Science 7 · EN1 · Energy accounts

Numbers are made up for practice. Answers: furnace off means 0 in and energy still out, so ΔE is negative and the house cools. Window open means more out than in, so it cools unless the furnace runs more. This is the steady state idea: equal in and out. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4, “House in January” preset.

Lesson 5 · EN1.S1

Into, within, out of: a toaster

INTO

Electrical energy from the outlet

1000 J

WITHIN: TOASTER AND BREAD

Current heats the wires. Glowing wires heat the bread.

Change: +350 J

OUT OF

Heating the kitchen air: 600 J

OUT OF

Light from the wires: 50 J

ΔE = 1000 J − 650 J = +350 J

Science 7 · EN1 · Energy accounts

Numbers are made up for practice and rounded to show the pattern. Ask: which transfers cross the boundary? Which stay inside? Why is the change positive? (The toaster and bread are hotter than before.) Point out that the energy that left is not lost from the universe; it spread into the kitchen where it is hard to use again. Return to the hook: the toboggan’s energy also spread into the snow and air. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (follow the joules), try the Block heater and Cyclist braking diagrams too.

Lab Bench · Investigation 3 · EN1.S2

Where does a bouncing ball’s energy go?

Tennis ballTop heightEnergy
Drop2.00 m1.20 J
Bounce 11.10 m0.66 J
Bounce 20.61 m0.36 J

Simulated data, made up for practice. Weight rounded to 0.6 N.

Worked example

Lift: W = 0.6 N × 2.0 m = 1.20 J
Bounce 1: 0.6 N × 1.1 m = 0.66 J
Out: 1.20 J − 0.66 J = 0.54 J

The 0.54 J warmed the ball and floor and made the bounce sound.

Science 7 · EN1 · Lab Bench

Lifting the ball is work: the force equals the ball’s weight and the distance is the height, so energy given = weight × height. Ask: which bounce loses the most joules? (The first, because the ball has the most energy then.) Have students run the investigation with a basketball and a rubber bouncy ball and compare. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Investigation 3. For the “into, within, out of” skill, follow with Investigation 4 (block heater).

Quick check

Three questions

1. You push on a wall with 200 N. It does not move. How much work do you do on the wall?

2. A pot of soup gains 50 kJ from the stove and loses 20 kJ to the air. What is its change in energy?

3. The system is just a bike. The rider pushes the pedals. Is this transfer into, within or out of the system?

Science 7 · EN1 · Check understanding

Answers. 1: 0 J, because the distance is 0 m (200 N × 0 m = 0 J). 2: +30 kJ (50 kJ − 20 kJ); the soup warms up. 3: Into the system, because the rider is in the surroundings when only the bike is the system. Use mini whiteboards. Lab link: Quiz tab for a 10 question round.

Extended response

A puck slides across an outdoor rink and stops. Your friend says, “Its energy is gone.” Agree or disagree?

Name your system and its boundary.

Track energy in and out.

Use conservation of energy.

Science 7 · EN1 · Check understanding

Model answer from the lab: take the puck as the system. The stick did work on it, transferring energy in. As it slides, friction with the ice and pushes from the air transfer energy out. That energy warms the ice and air a tiny bit, and the bang off the boards makes sound. The puck’s energy decreases until it stops, but the energy is not destroyed. It spread into the surroundings. Run it as a four corners debate (strongly agree to strongly disagree), then have students write. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 2.
