Temperature and heating

Objects or systems experience temperature changes because of energy transfer.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Temperature and Heating Lab

Welcome students and name the big idea: when something warms up or cools down, energy has moved into it or out of it. This deck follows the Temperature and Heating Lab in the same order as its six Learn lessons. Lab link: open the lab on the Learn tab so students can try each model as you go. Ask: what is one thing in this room that is warming up or cooling down right now?

Predict

Two benches, one cold morning

A metal bench and a wooden bench sit outside all night at −20 °C. In the morning you touch both with bare hands.

Metal bench

Which one feels colder?

Wooden bench

Are they really different temperatures?

Science 7 · EN2 · Predict

Give students one minute to predict and write a reason. Most will say the metal bench is colder. Reveal later (after the heating lessons): both benches are at the same temperature, −20 °C, after a whole night outside. The metal feels colder because it is a good conductor, so it pulls energy out of your warm hand much faster. Cold does not flow into your hand. Return to this question after the conduction slide. Part 1 · EN2.K1 and K2

What temperature measures

Particles that never stop moving.

Part 1 covers what temperature actually measures. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1 (the particle box). Have students move the slider from −40 °C to 100 °C and describe what changes.

EN2.K1

Temperature is an average

Temperature is a measure of the average energy of motion of the particles in an object or system. It describes how hot or cold something is.

Solid

Particles jiggle in place.

Liquid

Particles slide past each other.

Gas

Particles fly around and bump.

Some particles move fast and some move slowly. Temperature tells us the average.

Science 7 · EN2 · What temperature measures

Key move: separate the idea of particles always moving from the idea of temperature. In every state of matter the particles move; temperature is the average energy of that motion. In Canada we measure it in degrees Celsius. Discussion: why does the curriculum say average and not total? (Answer comes two slides later with the bathtub.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1, particle box.

Walk down the table. Stress that particles at −25 °C still move; cold means slower, not stopped. EN2.K1 · Average, not total

Bathtub or cocoa?

Warm bathtub

Science 7 · EN2 · What temperature measures

Ask first: which has more energy? Many students will say the cocoa. Then reveal both answers. Temperature is an average, so the cocoa is hotter. The bathtub holds more energy in total because it has so many more particles. This is a precision the lab adds to EN2.K1 (noted in the Coverage tab): temperature is not the same as the total energy of an object. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1, the note under the text.

Part 2 · EN2.K3 and K4

Heating: energy on the move

Hotter to colder, in three ways.

Part 2 covers the direction of heating and the three processes. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 2 and 3. Lab Bench, Investigation 1 (mixing water) and Investigation 4 (explain the heating) give practice.

EN2.K3

Heating goes from hotter to colder

80 °C

Hot mug cools down

energy

5 °C

Cold hands warm up

Heating is the transfer of energy from a higher‑temperature object to a lower‑temperature one. It continues until both reach the same temperature: thermal equilibrium.

Cold is not a thing that flows.

Science 7 · EN2 · Heating

Fast particles in the mug bump slower particles in the hands and speed them up. The mug cools and the hands warm. Return to the bench hook: the metal bench feels colder because energy leaves your hand faster into metal, not because cold flows in. Accuracy note (EN2.K3, flagged for review): the lab teaches that heating happens on its own from hotter to colder; fridges and heat pumps can move energy the other way only because they use electricity to do work. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2, put them in contact.

EN2.K3 · Worked example

Where do the temperatures meet?

The problem

200 g of water at 80 °C is mixed with 100 g of water at 20 °C. No energy escapes.

T final = (m₁ × T₁ + m₂ × T₂) ÷ (m₁ + m₂)

The answer

(200 × 80 + 100 × 20) ÷ 300
= 18 000 ÷ 300

= 60 °C

Closer to 80 °C, because there is more hot water.

Science 7 · EN2 · Heating

Students do not memorize this formula; it shows that energy moves from the hot water to the cold water until they share one temperature. Check with equal masses: 80 °C and 20 °C would meet halfway at 50 °C. Accuracy note: the lab adds this weighted-average calculation as a worked example of EN2.K3 and assumes no energy escapes to the surroundings; real mixes end a little cooler. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 1, mixing water, then try three randomized practice problems.

EN2.K4 · EN2.S1

Three ways heating happens

Conduction

Objects touch. Fast particles bump their neighbours and pass energy along. Best in solids, especially metals.

A spoon handle gets hot in soup.

Convection

A liquid or gas flows. Warm fluid becomes less dense and rises. Cooler fluid sinks. The loop is a convection current.

Warm air from a furnace vent fills a room.

Radiation

Energy travels as waves, such as light and infrared. No particles needed, so it crosses empty space.

A campfire warms your face from across the circle.

Conduction needs touching. Convection needs a flowing fluid. Radiation needs nothing in between.

Science 7 · EN2 · Heating

Act it out: conduction as students passing a ball down a line without moving their feet; convection as students walking a loop around the room carrying the ball; radiation as throwing the ball across a gap. Ask: why does the metal bench from the hook feel colder than the wood? (Metal conducts energy out of your hand faster.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3, watch each process. Sort It tab: conduction, convection or radiation deck.

EN2.K4

Only radiation can cross empty space.

That is how the Sun’s energy reaches Earth.

Pause on this statement. Space between the Sun and Earth is almost empty, with very few particles. Conduction and convection both need particles, so radiation is the only way across. This sets up Part 4 on Earth’s temperature. Discussion prompt: name another place where radiation does the heating without touching (campfire, a radiant heater at an outdoor rink shelter, a heat lamp).

Science 7 · EN2 · Using and slowing heating

The first three rows are the curriculum’s examples; the furnace and block heater are Alberta extras from the lab. Accuracy notes (EN2.K5, flagged for review): an electric coil also glows and radiates, but contact conduction is the main transfer to the pot. The hot-air balloon is taught as heated air becoming less dense and rising, the same idea that drives convection currents. Microwaves are absorbed mainly by water in food, and conduction then spreads energy inward, which is why food should stand after microwaving. Ask: which process does a toaster use? (Mostly radiation from glowing wires.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4, technology cards; Lab Bench, Investigation 4.

EN2.K6

Insulation slows heat transfer

In nature

Whale blubber

A thick fat layer conducts slowly and keeps the whale warm in icy water. Bison coats and fluffed feathers trap still air.

In technology

Cooler

Foam full of trapped air slows energy moving in. Vacuum bottles, attic insulation and parkas work the same way.

Insulation slows the rate of transfer. It never stops heating, and it never adds cold. Most insulators work by trapping still air.

Science 7 · EN2 · Using and slowing heating

Common misconception: a cooler or a parka “keeps the cold out” or “makes heat.” Insulation only slows transfer in whichever direction it is going. Still air conducts poorly and cannot form convection currents when it is trapped; shiny surfaces such as in a vacuum bottle also reflect radiation. Ask: why does a parka keep you warm but also keep a bag of ice frozen longer? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5, insulation cards; Sort It tab, conductor or insulator deck.

Part 4 · EN2.K7, K8 and K9

Earth’s energy budget

How the Sun’s radiation sets Earth’s temperature.

Part 4 follows sunlight to Earth. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (follow the sunlight diagram) and Lab Bench, Investigation 3 (Earth’s thermostat). Sensitive topic note: climate change can worry some students. Keep the focus on the science of absorption and reflection, and point to actions people take rather than doom.

EN2.K7

Where 100 units of sunlight go

≈30

≈20

≈50

Science 7 · EN2 · Earth’s energy budget

Numbers are rounded global averages out of 100 units (flagged for review under EN2.K7 to K9): about 30 reflected, about 20 absorbed by the atmosphere and about 50 by the surface. Fresh prairie snow reflects most of the sunlight that hits it, while open ocean and dark forest absorb most. Ask: why might a snowy Edmonton field stay cold on a bright sunny day in February? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6, tap each yellow arrow; Sort It tab, reflected or absorbed deck.

EN2.K8

The surface gives energy back

1

The warm surface gives off infrared radiation.

2

Greenhouse gases absorb some of it.

3

They send part back down. The rest escapes to space.

Most energy the surface absorbs leaves it again, mostly as infrared. Some also leaves by evaporating water and by rising warm air.

Greenhouse gases include water vapour and carbon dioxide.

Science 7 · EN2 · Earth’s energy budget

Accuracy note (EN2.K8, flagged for review): the draft says most absorbed energy “radiates back up.” The lab teaches that the surface gives energy back mostly as infrared radiation, and also names evaporation and rising warm air (convection). Infrared is invisible, but students feel it as warmth from a warm sidewalk after sunset. Ask: why do clear winter nights in Alberta get colder than cloudy ones? (Clouds absorb and send back more infrared.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6, tap the red arrows.

Both temperatures come from the lab’s simple one-layer energy balance model (flagged for review under EN2.S4): about −18 °C with no greenhouse effect and about 15 °C with today’s values, close to Earth’s real average. Science 7 · EN2 · Investigate

The data come from the lab’s simple cooling model and are labelled as made up for practice. Ask students to spot the flaw: the mug had no lid, but the foam cup and vacuum bottle had lids, so container and lid both changed. A fair test changes one variable at a time. Have students plan a fair version (same lid setting, same air temperature, same starting temperature). The vacuum bottle drops least because the vacuum stops conduction and convection and the shiny walls reflect radiation. Quick check

Three questions

1

You grab a metal railing at −25 °C and your hand feels cold. Which way is energy moving?

2

Which process lets the Sun’s energy reach Earth, and why?

3

Fresh snow covers the prairie. Does that area absorb more or less sunlight?

Science 7 · EN2 · Check

Answers. 1: Energy moves from your warm hand into the colder railing by conduction. Cold does not flow in. 2: Radiation, because space is nearly empty and conduction and convection need particles. 3: Less, because fresh snow reflects most sunlight; less absorption means the area stays cooler. Use mini whiteboards or fingers for quick responses. Lab link: Quiz tab for ten more questions drawn from a bank of 24.

Extended response

Lower the winter heating bill

A family in Edmonton wants to lower their winter heating bill. Explain how energy leaves their house by conduction, convection and radiation. Suggest one way to slow each.

Conduction

Through walls, windows, roof

Convection

Warm air leaking out of gaps

Radiation

Infrared from warm walls and windows

Science 7 · EN2 · Extended response

From the Written Review, extended response 2. Model answer: conduction through walls, windows and the roof is slowed by thicker attic insulation or triple-pane windows. Convection through gaps around doors and windows is slowed by weatherstripping and caulking. Radiation from warm walls and windows is slowed by heavy curtains at night and windows with reflective coatings. Slowing transfer means the furnace runs less. Key ideas checklist: a path and a fix for each process, insulation slows rather than stops transfer, and the link to the furnace running less. Run it as a think, pair, share before students write.
