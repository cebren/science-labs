The rock cycle

Transformation of rock through the rock cycle results from geological processes.

Created by Dr. Lance Pedersen · Use with the Rock Cycle Lab

Welcome students and name today’s big idea: rocks are not permanent. They form, break down and form again. This deck follows the Rock Cycle Lab in teaching order. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1. Have a few hand samples ready if you can: granite, sandstone, limestone or marble, and a piece of mica.

Predict

Big Rock near Okotoks is a huge block of rock. Will it last forever?

A

Yes. Rock is too hard to change.

B

No. It will slowly break into smaller pieces.

C

No. Its pieces could even become a new rock.

Science 7 · ES2 · Hook

Give students one minute to choose and justify. Do not reveal the answer yet. The best answer is C: weathering will break Big Rock into sediment, and that sediment could one day be compacted and cemented into new sedimentary rock. Big Rock is quartzite, a metamorphic rock that a glacier carried from the mountains. Return to this question after the sedimentary rock slide.

Part 1 · ES2.K1

The rock cycle

Rock is formed, broken down and formed again, on and below Earth’s surface.

Section opener. Ask: where have you seen rock changing? Expect answers like cracked sidewalks, river valleys and the Drumheller hoodoos. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

Walk the main loop from magma: cooling and crystallization, weathering and erosion, deposition with compaction and cementation, heat and pressure, then melting. Ask students to trace it with a finger and name each process aloud. Stress that the cycle has no real start or end. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1. ES2.K1 · ES2.S1

Not a one-way loop

The cycle has shortcuts. The path a rock takes depends on which processes act on it.

Igneous to metamorphic

Granite becomes gneiss

Heat and pressure change it directly. It never becomes sediment.

Sedimentary to sediment

Hoodoos wear away

The Drumheller hoodoos are sandstone being weathered and eroded today.

Any rock to magma

Deep enough, hot enough

Any rock buried deep enough and heated enough can melt into magma.

Science 7 · ES2 · The rock cycle

Accuracy note for ES2.K1: the lab teaches the rock cycle as many possible paths, not a fixed order. Ask: can a metamorphic rock become a sedimentary rock? Yes: uplift brings it to the surface, it is weathered into sediment, and the sediment is compacted and cemented. Lab link: Lab Bench, Station 1 (Rock cycle journey). Students reach a target stage in as few steps as they can.

Part 2 · ES2.K6 · ES2.K7

Minerals

Rocks are made of minerals. Geologists identify minerals by testing their properties.

Section opener. Pass around a granite sample and a hand lens if you have them. Ask: how many different materials can you see in this rock? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2.

ES2.K6

Four common minerals

Quartz

Glassy and very hard (7). Scratches glass. Most sand is mostly quartz.

In granite: yes

Feldspar

Often pink or white. Hard (6). The most common mineral group in the crust.

In granite: yes

Mica

Black or silvery. Soft. Peels into thin, flexible sheets.

In granite: yes

Calcite

White or clear. Soft (3). Fizzes in dilute acid. Main mineral in limestone and marble.

In granite: no

A mineral is a natural, non-living solid with a regular crystal pattern. A rock is made of one or more minerals.

Science 7 · ES2 · Minerals

Accuracy note for ES2.K6: mica and feldspar are mineral groups, not single minerals. The lab names biotite (black) and muscovite (silvery) as two kinds of mica. Calcite is taught as the main mineral in limestone and marble, not in granite. Ask: which mineral would you expect in a shell? (Calcite.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2, granite under a magnifier.

ES2.K7

Six properties to test

Colour

Easy to see, but the least reliable. Impurities change it.

Lustre

How the surface reflects light: metallic, glassy, pearly or dull.

Streak

Colour of the powder on a rough white tile. More reliable than colour.

Transparency

How much light passes through: transparent, translucent or opaque.

Hardness

How well it resists scratching, on the Mohs scale from 1 to 10.

Magnetism

Does a magnet stick? Only a few minerals, like magnetite, are strongly magnetic.

One property is rarely enough. Use several together.

Science 7 · ES2 · Minerals

Demonstrate streak with a porcelain tile if you have one. Hematite is a good example: it can look silver-grey but always leaves a red-brown streak. Ask: why is colour the least reliable property? Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3, property explorer.

Part 3 · ES2.K2 to ES2.K5

Three ways to make rock

Rocks are classified by how they formed. Their texture, layering and minerals are the clues.

Section opener. Ask: if you cannot watch a rock form, how could you tell how it formed? Steer students toward clues they can see. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4.

ES2.K2 · ES2.S3

Reading a rock

ClueIgneousSedimentaryMetamorphic
TextureInterlocking crystals in random directions, or glassRounded grains or pieces glued togetherCrystals that regrew, often flattened
LayeringUsually noneFlat layers called beds, sometimes fossilsBands or sheets called foliation
Mineral compositionQuartz, feldspar, mica in graniteQuartz in sandstone, calcite in limestoneCalcite in marble, quartz in quartzite

Look at all three clues together. One clue alone can mislead you.

Science 7 · ES2 · Three rock types

Point out that layering means two different things: beds in sedimentary rock and foliation in metamorphic rock. Some fine-grained rocks need more tests to classify. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4, the three texture swatches and the yes or no rock key. Students can run the key with a classroom sample.

ES2.K3 · Sedimentary rock

Layers that turn to stone

1

Weathering

Rock breaks into sediment.

2

Erosion

Water, wind or ice carry it away.

3

Deposition

It settles in flat layers.

4

Compaction

Weight of new layers squeezes out water.

5

Cementation

Minerals like calcite or quartz glue grains.

Where

On or just below Earth’s surface

Examples

Sandstone, shale, conglomerate, limestone, coal. Drumheller badlands and the Edmonton river valley show sandstone, mudstone and coal.

Science 7 · ES2 · Three rock types

The draft names compaction and cementation. Steps 1 to 3 connect back to ES1 (weathering, erosion, deposition). Accuracy note for ES2.K3: the lab defines sediment to include shells and plant remains so that limestone and coal fit. Rocks that form when minerals crystallize out of water, like rock salt, are not taught. Local examples are general descriptions to verify. Return to the hook: Big Rock’s sand could become new sandstone. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5, step through the five stages.

ES2.K4 · Igneous rock

Rock from melted rock

Magma is melted rock below the surface. Lava is melted rock on the surface. As it cools, particles lock into crystals: crystallization.

Below the surface · intrusive

Slow cooling

Large crystals you can see. Example: granite.

On the surface · extrusive

Fast cooling

Tiny crystals, or glass with none. Examples: basalt, obsidian.

Science 7 · ES2 · Three rock types

Accuracy note for ES2.K4: intrusive and extrusive are added as terms. Obsidian is glass with no crystals because lava cooled in seconds, so crystallization happens only when there is time for it. Most igneous rock at Alberta’s surface is in glacier-carried erratics or the Canadian Shield in the far northeast. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6, cooling speed slider.

Big idea · ES2.K4

Slow cooling grows big crystals. Fast cooling keeps them tiny.

Crystal size is a clock that tells you where a rock cooled.

Pause here. Ask students to explain the statement to a partner using granite and basalt. Then ask where obsidian fits. This is the idea they will test in the Lab Bench investigation.

ES2.K5 · Metamorphic rock

Changed without melting

Below the surface, great heat and pressure change an existing parent rock while it stays solid. Pressure can line minerals up in bands: foliation.

Parent rockMetamorphic rockFoliated?
ShaleSlate, then schist, then gneissYes
GraniteGneissYes
LimestoneMarbleNo
SandstoneQuartzite (Big Rock)No

Science 7 · ES2 · Three rock types

Accuracy note for ES2.K5: “extreme heat and pressure” is taught as heat and pressure high enough to change minerals while the rock stays solid. If the rock melts, it becomes magma, not metamorphic rock. Contact metamorphism next to magma is not named. Ask: what happens if we keep adding heat? Worked example

Manipulated: cooling time. Responding: crystal size.

Pattern: as cooling time goes up, crystal size goes up.

Conclusion: longer cooling gives particles more time to join crystals. Granite cooled slowly underground. Basalt and obsidian cooled fast at the surface.

Science 7 · ES2 · Investigate

These numbers are the base values of the lab’s made-up model; each lab trial adds a little random variation, like a real test. Have students run at least three cooling times twice each, then unlock the conclusion question. A hands-on version: cool a saturated solution quickly in a fridge and slowly at room temperature, then compare crystals with a hand lens. Lab link: Lab Bench, Station 3. Test 3

Reasoning

Hardness is between about 2.5 and 5.5. Quartz (7) is too hard and gypsum (2) is too soft. Only one common white mineral fizzes in acid.

It is calcite (hardness 3).

This is the worked example from the lab. Ask students which single test was most useful and why. Teaching move: challenge them to identify a mineral in the lab using as few tests as possible. Accuracy note for ES2.K7: the dilute acid test is context beyond the draft list; demonstrate it yourself with suitable safety equipment. Tool hardness values are approximate. Quick check

Three questions

1

A rock has large interlocking crystals of quartz, feldspar and mica, with no layers. Which rock type is it, and where did it cool?

2

Put these in order: cementation, deposition, weathering, compaction.

3

Why is streak more useful than colour for identifying a mineral?

Science 7 · ES2 · Review

Answers. 1: Igneous (granite). It cooled slowly below the surface, which is why the crystals are large. 2: Weathering, deposition, compaction, cementation. 3: Colour can change because of small impurities, but the colour of the powder stays the same; hematite can look silver but always leaves a red-brown streak. Lab link: Quiz tab for a 10-question round from a bank of 24.

Debate · Written Review

Agree or disagree?

“The rock cycle always goes igneous, then sedimentary, then metamorphic.”

Take a side. Use at least two examples of rocks and the processes that change them.

Science 7 · ES2 · Review

Model answer: disagree. The rock cycle has many paths. Granite (igneous) can become gneiss (metamorphic) through heat and pressure without becoming sediment. Sandstone can be weathered back into sand and form new sandstone. Any rock can melt into magma. Key ideas: a clear position, an example of a shortcut path, a second example, and the idea that processes decide the path. Run as a four-corners or line-up debate, then have students write.
