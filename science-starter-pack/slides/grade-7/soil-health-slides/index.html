Part 1

What is soil?

How soil forms, the main soil types, and the factors that make every soil different.

Section opener. Ask: where have you seen soil today? Is the soil in a garden the same as the soil on a riverbank or in a forest? Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 1 to 3.

ES3.K1 · How soil forms

Soil is minerals plus organic matter

1

Weathering

Breaks rock into smaller pieces. These become the minerals in soil.

2

Erosion

Moves the pieces by water, wind, ice or gravity and drops them somewhere new.

3

Decomposition

Bacteria, fungi and small animals break down dead things into dark humus.

Organic matter is material from living things: dead leaves, roots and animal waste.

Science 7 · ES3 · What is soil?

Define soil: a thin layer of minerals and organic matter, formed over time through weathering, erosion and decomposition. Connect to ES1 (weathering and erosion) and ES2 (the rock cycle): soil minerals are weathered rock. Ask students to sort what they see in a handful of garden soil into “mineral” and “organic.” Teacher note (accuracy flag ES3.K1): erosion is taught as moving and dropping material, which is how glacial and river deposits become the parent material that soil forms on. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1. Move the slider through the five stages.

Emphasize why soil loss matters: topsoil lost in one storm can take lifetimes to replace. Much of Alberta’s soil formed on material left by glaciers at the end of the last ice age, roughly 10 000 to 12 000 years ago. The layer diagram (soil profile) is context, not a curriculum requirement. ES3.K2 · Soil types

Three particle sizes, many soils

ParticleSizeFeelPores and drainage
Sand0.05 to 2 mmGrittyLarge pores, very fast
Silt0.002 to 0.05 mmSmooth, flouryMedium pores, medium
ClayUnder 0.002 mmSticky when wetTiny pores, very slow

Loam

A balanced mix of sand, silt and clay, usually with plenty of organic matter. Most garden plants grow best in loam.

Science 7 · ES3 · What is soil?

A sandy soil is mostly sand, a clay soil is mostly clay, and loam is a balanced mix. Quick demo: rub a pinch of sand, then of moist clay, between fingers. Clay rolls into a ribbon. Teacher note (accuracy flag ES3.K2): the draft names sand, clay and loam. Silt is added so loam can be defined. Size limits follow the Canadian and USDA systems. Clay has the smallest pores but often more total pore space than sand; avoid saying clay has “less space.” Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2. Compare the four soils under the magnifier.

ES3.K2 · Physical properties

Four ways to describe a soil

Composition

What it is made of: how much sand, silt, clay and organic matter.

Colour

Dark soil usually has more organic matter. Light soil usually has less.

Odour

Sweet and earthy means living microbes. Sour or rotten often means waterlogged soil with little oxygen.

Pore size

The size of the spaces between particles. Pores hold air and water.

Science 7 · ES3 · What is soil?

Have three labelled samples (sand, clay, garden loam) for students to look at, smell and feel. Ask them to use all four properties to describe each sample. Discussion prompt: which property would help most to tell sand from clay? (Feel and pore size, shown by drainage.) Teacher note (accuracy flag ES3.K2): colour is linked to organic matter only; odour is linked to living microbes and to waterlogging. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2 property table, then Lab Bench Station 3 (soil detective).

ES3.K3 · ES3.S2 · What shapes a soil

Three factors decide what a soil is like

Parent material

The rock or deposit soil forms from. It sets the minerals and texture. Sandy deposits make sandy soil.

Climate

Temperature and precipitation. Rain and freeze-thaw speed weathering. Dry places build soil slowly.

Biotic components

Living things. Grass roots add organic matter deep down. Earthworms and fungi mix and decompose it.

Alberta example: centuries of grass roots made the thick black soils around Edmonton. Drier southern grassland has browner, thinner topsoil.

Science 7 · ES3 · What is soil?

Use the recipe mixer in the lab live: change one factor at a time and ask students to predict the result first. Soil scientists also count time and the shape of the land; soil on a steep slope stays thin. Discussion prompt: why might the soil under a boreal forest in northern Alberta look different from soil in a wheat field near Edmonton? Teacher note (accuracy flag ES3.K3): time and landscape are added as extra factors. Alberta soil zone examples (black parkland soils, brown dry grassland soils, grey boreal forest soils) are general and should be checked against Alberta soil zone references. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (soil recipe mixer).

Part 2

Healthy soil

What is inside healthy soil, and why it matters for food, climate, water and life.

Section opener. Ask: is soil alive? Take a quick vote, then tell students that a handful of healthy soil can hold more tiny living things than there are people on Earth. Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 4 and 5.

ES3.K4 · Inside healthy soil

Healthy soil is a living mixture

45%

Minerals

5%

Organic matter

25%

Water

25%

Air: oxygen and carbon dioxide

Typical volume of a good loam. Real soils vary.

Nutrients

Nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, from minerals and decomposed organic matter.

Biodiverse organisms

Bacteria, fungi, earthworms, insects and mites. They decompose, mix and build pores.

Science 7 · ES3 · Healthy soil

Walk through the seven components named in the curriculum: organic matter, oxygen, carbon dioxide, minerals, water, nutrients and biodiverse organisms. About half of a good soil is solid and half is pore space shared by water and air. Ask: why does soil air hold more carbon dioxide than the air we breathe? (Roots and organisms use oxygen and give off carbon dioxide as they respire.) Ask what happens to oxygen when the soil is waterlogged. Teacher note (accuracy flag ES3.K4): 45/5/25/25 is a common textbook value for an ideal loam, not a fixed rule. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4. Move the moisture slider from dry to waterlogged.

ES3.K5 · Why it matters

Healthy soil improves six big things

Food security

People can reliably get enough healthy food.

Climate resilience

Land copes better with drought, floods and heat.

Nutrient cycling

Nutrients move from dead things back into living things.

Air and water quality

Soil filters water and stores carbon.

Biodiversity

Soil is home to a huge variety of life.

Sustainable agriculture

Farms keep producing food for many generations.

Science 7 · ES3 · Healthy soil

Introduce the six improvements from the curriculum. Ask pairs to pick one benefit and name a person in Alberta who depends on it (a farmer, a family buying groceries, a town drawing water from the North Saskatchewan River). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5 (benefits web). Tap each component to light up its benefits.

ES3.S3 · Explaining a benefit

Follow the chain: component, job, improvement

Component

Organic matter

What it does

Soaks up and holds water like a sponge

Improvement

Climate resilience

Crops last through dry spells. The middle link is the one people often skip.

Your turn: Organisms → ? → Nutrient cycling

Science 7 · ES3 · Healthy soil

Model the three-link chain aloud. Then give students the “Your turn” chain. Answer: organisms (bacteria, fungi, earthworms) decompose dead plants and animals and release nutrients back into the soil, where roots take them up again. More chains to try: oxygen → roots use it to stay alive and take up water → food security; water → nutrients dissolve in it so roots can take them up → nutrient cycling; carbon dioxide → dissolves in soil water to make a weak acid that slowly weathers minerals and frees nutrients → nutrient cycling. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Station 4 (chain builder).

Part 3

Investigating soil

Design a fair test of soil and plant growth, and measure what a soil is made of.

Section opener. Remind students of their prediction from the hook slide. Ask: how could we test it so the result is fair? Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Stations 1 and 2.

ES3.S1 · Controlled experiment

Change one thing, keep the rest the same

VariableIn our bean test
Manipulated (you change)Type of soil: sand, clay, loam
Responding (you measure)Plant height after a set number of days
Controlled (keep the same)Seed type, water, light, pot size, temperature, planting depth

Repeated trials

Use at least three pots of each soil and average the heights. One odd plant then cannot change the result much.

Science 7 · ES3 · Investigating soil

Have students build their own variable table before showing this one. Common error: listing “soil type” as a controlled variable, or giving each soil a different seed. If you run the real experiment in class, fast-growing beans or radishes show differences in about two weeks. Safety: wash hands after handling soil. Teacher note (accuracy flag ES3.S1): the lab’s plant heights come from a made-up model with random variation; leaving a controlled variable unticked adds extra variation to show why fair tests matter. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Station 1 (plant growth trial).

ES3.S1 · ES3.K2 · Worked examples

Two calculations from the bench

Average height

average = sum ÷ number of pots

Loam pots: 11.2, 12.6 and 10.9 cm

(11.2 + 12.6 + 10.9) ÷ 3
= 34.7 ÷ 3 ≈ 11.6 cm

Jar test

% = layer ÷ total × 100

Sand 24 mm, silt 16 mm, clay 10 mm. Total 50 mm.

sand 48% · silt 32% · clay 20%
A balanced mix: loam

Example measurements are made up for practice.

Science 7 · ES3 · Investigating soil

Work both examples on the board. Students do not need to memorize the formulas. For the jar test: shake soil with water, let it settle for a day; sand settles first at the bottom, then silt, then clay on top; organic bits float. Check: 24 ÷ 50 × 100 = 48; 16 ÷ 50 × 100 = 32; 10 ÷ 50 × 100 = 20; the three add to 100. Teacher note (accuracy flag ES3.K2): the lab uses a simplified soil type rule (clay 40% or more is clay; sand 70% or more with little clay is sandy; silt 80% or more is silty; otherwise loam), not the full texture triangle. Lab link: Lab Bench tab, Station 1 worked example and Station 2 (jar test, New jar for randomized practice).

Part 4

Caring for soil

How human activity changes soil, and six practices that protect it.

Section opener. Ask: what human activities near your school could change the soil? (Farming, building new neighbourhoods, gardens, pipelines and well sites, parks.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6, and Lab Bench tab, Station 5.

ES3.S4 · The Dirty Thirties

Bare soil erodes.

In the 1930s, a long drought dried prairie soil. Heavy plowing had left fields bare, so wind blew topsoil off many farms. Erosion is a natural process, but human choices made it much worse.

Science 7 · ES3 · Caring for soil

Use this as a story slide. Point out both kinds of cause: a geological process (wind erosion) and climate (drought), plus human activity (plowing that left soil bare). Ask: what could farmers do differently so the wind cannot reach the soil? Collect ideas before the next slide. Sensitive topic: the 1930s were a time of hardship for many prairie families; some students may have family stories to share. Teacher note (accuracy flag ES3.K6/S4): the Dirty Thirties summary is general and should be checked against a local history source. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6.

ES3.K6 · Protecting soil

Six ways people protect soil

Cover crops

Plants that cover the soil between main crops. Roots hold it.

Mulching

Straw, wood chips or crop leftovers shield soil and hold moisture.

Conservation tillage

Plow less or not at all. Leave crop stubble in place.

Buffer strips

Grass, shrubs or trees by streams trap soil and slow runoff.

Controlled burning

Planned fire on grassland clears old growth and returns some nutrients.

Rotational grazing

Move animals between pastures so plants and roots recover.

Science 7 · ES3 · Caring for soil

For each practice, ask students what it does to soil cover, roots and organic matter. Most work by keeping soil covered and held by roots. Controlled burning needs careful framing: it is a planned, managed fire by trained crews on grassland or pasture. Burnt ground can erode until plants regrow, and burning crop stubble is not good practice. First Nations peoples have used fire to care for prairie and parkland for a very long time; the lab describes this only in general terms. Teacher note (accuracy flag ES3.K6): please review the wording on Indigenous fire stewardship and consider inviting a community partner rather than adding detail. Sensitive topic: some students may have experienced wildfire evacuations. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6 (field protector). Turn practices on one at a time; numbers in the model are made up.

Quick check

Three questions

1

A soil is sticky when wet, cracks when dry and drains very slowly. What type is it, and why?

2

Name the three factors that affect the nature of soil.

3

In a test of soil type and plant growth, which variable must stay the same: soil type, water or plant height?

Science 7 · ES3 · Review

Answers. 1: Clay soil. Tiny clay particles pack tightly with tiny pores, so water moves slowly; clay is sticky when wet and cracks as it dries. 2: Parent material, climate and biotic components (living things). 3: Water. Soil type is the manipulated variable and plant height is the responding variable; water, light, seed type and pot size are controlled. Use mini whiteboards or a quick hand vote. Lab link: Quiz tab for ten more questions, and Sort It for soil types and soil lists.

Extended response · ES3.K4 · ES3.K5 · ES3.S3

“Soil is just dirt. It does not matter what is in it.”

Do you agree? Use the components and benefits of healthy soil to explain your answer.

A strong answer includes

A clear position · several components named · at least two component to benefit chains · what is lost when soil is damaged

Science 7 · ES3 · Review

Run as a four-corners debate (strongly agree to strongly disagree), then have students write their answer. Model answer: I disagree. Healthy soil is a living mixture of minerals, organic matter, water, air, nutrients and many organisms. Organic matter holds water, which builds climate resilience. Organisms decompose dead things and recycle nutrients, which supports food security. Roots and fungi hold soil in place and the soil filters water, which keeps rivers clean. Damaged soil cannot do these jobs. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 3. Students can check the key ideas they included.
