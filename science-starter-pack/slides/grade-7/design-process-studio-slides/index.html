Predict

Every winter recess, the boot room jams. Sixty students, one small room, nine minutes to get outside.

What would you change first? Who might your idea leave out?

Science 7 · CS3 · Opening · The boot room scenario is made up for practice

Hook. Give 60 seconds for students to write one idea on a sticky note. Collect and sort them quickly on the board. Most students will jump to a product (more hooks, a bigger room). Point that out without judging: today we learn why designers start with the people first. Ask the second question: who might your idea leave out? Prompt with a kindergarten buddy, a student who uses a wheelchair, the custodian. The nine-minute figure and the boot room are a made-up practice scenario used throughout the lab. Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 1 shows the same scenario.

Part 1 · CS3.K1

Design thinking

Start with the people, not the gadget.

Section opener for design thinking (CS3.K1). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

CS3.K1 · Design thinking

Design thinking starts with people

Design thinking is a way of solving problems that starts with the people who have the problem. It often leads to new ideas, which is why it is called innovative.

1

Watch

What do people do? Where do they get stuck?

2

Listen

What do they say they need? This builds empathy.

3

Ask

Turn it into a question: “How might we…?”

Science 7 · CS3 · Design thinking

Define empathy: understanding how someone else experiences a problem. Teaching move: have students open the lab and try the observation board. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1. Each user (Grade 7 student, kindergarten buddy, student who uses a wheelchair, custodian) has different observations. The observations are made up for practice; a real design team would watch and interview real users. Accuracy note for the teacher (CS3.K1): the draft calls design thinking “innovative”. The lab explains this as leading to new ideas and does not claim it is the only innovative method.

CS3.K1 · Design thinking

Two ways to start

Solution first

“We need more hooks.”

Jumps to one answer. If hooks are not the real problem, it fails.

People first

“How might we help 60 students get their winter gear on in five minutes?”

Leaves room for hooks, cubbies, a new schedule or buddy helpers.

Science 7 · CS3 · Design thinking

Ask: which start gives the team more possible solutions? Why? The “How might we” form names the users and the goal but not the answer. Practice: pair students and have them rewrite one of the hook ideas from the opening sticky notes as a “How might we” question. In the lab, the problem statement builder writes one automatically from a chosen user and need. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 1.

Part 2 · CS3.K2 and K3

The design loop

Five steps, repeated until the solution works for the people who use it.

Section opener for the design process (CS3.K2) and kinds of solutions (CS3.K3). Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 2 and 3.

CS3.K2 · Design processes

Five steps in a design process

1

Identify the problem

Who has it? What do they need?

2

Research

Ask users, measure, see what others tried.

3

Brainstorm solutions

Many ideas first. Judge them later.

4

Plan a prototype

A quick, early model: sketch, cardboard or practice run.

5

Test and refine

Try it with real users. Then improve it.

Iterative: go around more than once. Collaborative: teams and users share ideas and test together.

Science 7 · CS3 · Design loop

Walk through the five steps the curriculum names. Define prototype: a quick, early model of a solution used for testing. Stress the two describing words in CS3.K2: iterative (repeat the loop) and collaborative (work with a team and with users). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2; students can select each step on the loop diagram to see what the boot room team did. Accuracy note for the teacher (CS3.K2): these five steps are one common version of a design process. Many versions exist; one well-known design thinking model uses empathize, define, ideate, prototype and test. The lab teaches iteration as looping back to any earlier step, not only to “refine”.

CS3.K2 · Design processes

Tests show where to loop back

Little kids still cannot reach the hooksBack to brainstorm: low hooks or floor cubbies

Science 7 · CS3 · Design loop · Test results are made up for practice

Ask before revealing the right column: for each result, which step should the team go back to? The second row is the important one: sometimes testing shows you solved the wrong problem, so the loop goes all the way back to the start. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 2. The test result buttons under the loop diagram draw the loop-back arrow. These results are a made-up practice scenario.

CS3.K3 · Kinds of solutions

A solution is not always an object

Responds to a…Product (a thing)Process (a way of doing)Service (help people give)
NeedBoot trays for a dry floorFire drill routeBuddy helpers for zippers
WantHeated boot dryerMusic during gear-upLost and found delivery

A need is something people must have to be safe, healthy or able to take part. Some solutions fit more than one box, and that is fine.

Science 7 · CS3 · Kinds of solutions

CS3.K3: solutions can address needs, wants or problems and can be products, processes or services. Teaching move: cover one cell at a time and have students guess what kind of solution it is. Then ask students to add one new idea of their own to each column for the boot room. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 3 (solution grid); Sort It tab, “Product, process or service?” deck. Accuracy note for the teacher (CS3.K3): the categories are taught as helpful labels that can overlap. A school app is a product that may also change a process.

Part 3 · CS3.K4

Computational thinking

Four thinking tools from computer science. No computer needed.

Section opener for computational thinking (CS3.K4). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4.

CS3.K4 · Computational thinking

Four thinking tools

Decomposition

Break a big problem into smaller parts you can solve one at a time.

Pattern recognition

Notice what repeats or what is similar, so you can reuse a solution.

Abstraction

Keep the important details. Leave out the rest.

Algorithms

Write clear, ordered steps that solve the problem every time.

Science 7 · CS3 · Computational thinking

Define each tool in plain words. Abstraction is usually the hardest. Use the transit map example: an LRT map shows the order of the stops and where lines meet, and leaves out the real curves of the track because riders do not need them. Ask students for one everyday example of each tool (a recipe is an algorithm; a school bell schedule shows a pattern). Accuracy note for the teacher (CS3.K4): no programming is required here; coding is handled in CS1 and CS2. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4; Sort It tab, “Which thinking tool?” deck.

CS3.K4 · Computational thinking

The tools on the boot room problem

ToolWhat the team did
DecompositionSplit the jam into five parts: storing gear, moving people, wet floors, lost items, helping young students.
Pattern recognitionTiming data showed both short recesses were slow, but lunch was faster because classes leave at different times.
AbstractionDrew a floor plan with only walls, doors, hooks and walkway space. Wall colour left out.
AlgorithmPosted steps on the door: Class A enters, two minutes later Class B, boots on at the bench, then out.

Science 7 · CS3 · Computational thinking · Timing data in the lab are made up

Connect the four tools to one problem. Ask: which tool led to the idea of staggering recess? (Pattern recognition.) Then have students try the algorithm investigation: they get a random task with mixed-up steps and must put them in order. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 4, and Lab Bench, Investigation 2. Worked example from the lab: making a phone call on speaker; tapping the speaker button must come after tapping call, because the button only appears during a call. The timing table in the lab is made-up practice data.

Part 4 · CS3.K5 and K6

Designing for every user

User interaction, user interfaces and universal design.

Section opener for user interaction (CS3.K5) and universal design (CS3.K6). Lab link: Learn tab, Lessons 5 and 6. Sensitive topic note: this section discusses disability. Use respectful, general language, avoid asking students to share their own or classmates’ disabilities, and focus on design features rather than on individuals.

CS3.K5 · User interaction

Design for how people will use it

User interaction is everything that happens when a person uses a solution. A user interface is the part they use to control it: buttons, a screen, a door handle, an elevator panel.

Clear

Users can see it, reach it and understand it.

Feedback

A sound, light or message says it worked.

Fixable

A back or undo button lets users recover from mistakes.

Science 7 · CS3 · User interaction

Key line: a solution can work perfectly inside and still fail if people cannot figure out how to use it. Demo: open the lab’s library self-checkout screen builder. Start with every feature off: all six made-up users are blocked, and the screen says “Initiate circulation transaction”. Turn on plain language and ask what changed. Then turn on features one at a time and notice that most features help more than one user. Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 5. Accuracy note for the teacher (CS3.K5): user interaction is taught as the whole experience of using a solution; feedback and undo are added as features of good interfaces.

CS3.K6 · Universal design

Universal design plans from the start for people with many different needs and abilities.

The curb cut effect

Curb cuts were made for wheelchair users. They also help people with strollers, wagons, rolling suitcases and bikes. A feature made for one group often helps almost everyone.

Science 7 · CS3 · Universal design

Define universal design and accessibility: how easily all people, including people with disabilities, can reach, use and understand something. Universal design supports the design process: include users with different abilities when you research and when you test. Discussion prompt: where have you seen the curb cut effect at school or in Edmonton? (Automatic doors, captions, step-free entrances, picture signs.) Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6, “who does it help?” feature finder. Accuracy note for the teacher (CS3.K6): the lab credits architect Ronald Mace with making the term popular in the 1980s, and the seven principles to his team at the Center for Universal Design (North Carolina State University, 1997). Please verify these attributions. Universal Design for Learning (UDL) is a related idea for teaching and is not taught here. Sensitive topic: describe disability in general, respectful terms.

CS3.K6 · Universal design

Seven principles as a checklist

PrincipleAsk
1. Equitable useCan people with different abilities use it the same way?
2. Flexibility in useDoes it fit different choices, hands and paces?
3. Simple and intuitive useIs it easy to understand in any language?
4. Perceptible informationDoes information come by sight, sound or touch?
5. Tolerance for errorAre mistakes hard to make and easy to fix?
6. Low physical effortCan it be used without much strength?
7. Size and spaceIs there room to reach it sitting or standing?

Science 7 · CS3 · Universal design · Students do not need to memorize these

The seven principles are a tool for discussing and evaluating designs (CS3.S2 and S3). Students do not need to memorize them. Principle 7’s full name is “size and space for approach and use”. Quick practice: take a lever door handle and ask which principles it uses (flexibility in use, low physical effort). Lab link: Learn tab, Lesson 6, where the principles light up for each feature, and Lab Bench, Investigation 4 (universal design audit). Accuracy note for the teacher (CS3.K6): the principles are credited to the Center for Universal Design at North Carolina State University (1997); please verify.

CS3.S1 · Lab Bench investigation

Boot room prototype tester

Goal: everyone ready in 5 minutes or less, and every user able to use the room. Worked example:

RoundWhat changedTimeWhat the test showed
1Hooks at 150 cm, three classes at once9 minYoung and seated users cannot reach
2Hooks lowered to 120 cm, bench added8 minReach fixed, still too slow
3Two classes at a time (a process)5 minTime goal met

Each round tested one or two changes, so the team could tell what helped.

Science 7 · CS3 · Lab Bench · Simulator and times are a made-up model

Lab link: Lab Bench, Investigation 1. Students set hook height, how many classes enter at once, and extra features (benches, low cubbies, picture and name labels, boot trays, a clear 150 cm walkway, buddy helpers). Each test reports a time and which of five made-up users can use the room, and adds a line to the iteration log. Challenge: reach 5 minutes or less with all five users served, in as few rounds as possible. Point out that round 3 was a process change, not a product: CS3.K3 in action. Ask: why test only one or two changes per round? (So you know which change helped.) Note: the simulator is a simple made-up model; real testing would time real students.

Quick check

Three questions

1

A test shows the team solved the wrong problem. Which step do they go back to?

2

A transit map shows stops in order but not the real shape of the track. Which thinking tool is this?

3

Why is a step-free main entrance better universal design than a ramp at a back door?

Science 7 · CS3 · Review

Answers. 1: Back to identifying the problem; the loop can return to any earlier step. 2: Abstraction: keeping the important details and leaving out the rest. 3: With a step-free main entrance, everyone uses the same door in the same way; a back ramp works but sends some people to a separate entrance. Follow up with the lab’s Quiz tab (10 questions drawn from a bank of 22) and Sort It decks.

Debate · CS3.S2

“Universal design only helps people with disabilities, so it is not worth the cost.”

Do you agree? Take a side and support it with at least two examples.

Science 7 · CS3 · Universal design · From the Written Review, extended response 3

Run as a four corners or a quick pair debate, then have students write the full answer in the lab. Lab link: Written Review tab, extended response 3, with a model answer and key-idea checklist. Model answer points: universal design helps people with disabilities and almost everyone else (curb cuts help strollers and bikes; captions help English learners and people in noisy places; automatic doors help anyone carrying things). Designing it in from the start usually costs less than fixing it later. Cost is a real constraint, and a strong answer admits that. Students can use the decision matrix (Lab Bench, Investigation 3) to compare two options. Sensitive topic: keep the discussion on designs, not on individual classmates.
