Science 7 · Forces: Structures and Simple Machines · F2

Machines and Work Lab

Understanding: Machines can change the force required to complete a task. A ramp, a pulley and a crowbar all let you move heavy things with a smaller push or pull. This lab shows what force and work are, how the six simple machines trade force for distance, and how to measure the help a machine gives.

Learn

Seven short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with force and work, meet the six simple machines, then see how machines trade force for distance and how to measure the help they give.

Lesson 1 · Force
F2.K1

A force has a size and a direction

A force is a push or a pull. Forces are measured in newtons (N). A small apple weighs about 1 N.

Every force has a magnitude, which is its size, and a direction, which is the way it points. “200 N” is not enough. You need “200 N to the right” or “200 N up”.

That is why scientists draw a force as an arrow. The length shows the magnitude. The arrowhead shows the direction.

Weight is the force of gravity pulling an object down. On Earth, each kilogram of mass weighs about 9.8 N.

weight = mass × 9.8 N/kg
20 kg crate: 20 × 9.8 = 196 N, pointing down
Remember: to describe a force, give its size in newtons and its direction.

Model: forces on a crate

Change the push or pull and its direction. Change the mass to see the weight arrow grow.

Lesson 2 · Work
F2.K2F2.S2F2.S3

Work means a force moves something

In science, work is done when a force moves an object a distance in the direction of the force. Work is measured in joules (J).

W = F × d
work (J) = force (N) × distance moved in the direction of the force (m)

One joule is the work done when a 1 N force moves something 1 m.

Two things must happen for work to be done. There must be a force, and the object must move in the direction of that force. Pushing hard on a wall that does not move does no work on the wall. Holding a backpack still does no work on it, even though your arms get tired.

A bigger force or a longer distance means more work. Double either one and the work doubles.

Remember: no movement in the direction of the force means no work.

Model: work as a rectangle

Work is force times distance, so it is the area of this rectangle. Try a scenario, or set your own force and distance.

Lesson 3 · The six simple machines
F2.K3

Six machines with no motor

A simple machine is a device with few or no moving parts that helps you do a task. You push or pull on it with an input force, also called the effort. The machine pushes or pulls on the load with an output force.

A simple machine helps by changing the input force. It can change its magnitude (you push with less force) or its direction (you pull down to lift up), or both.

There are six simple machines: the inclined plane, the lever, the wheel and axle, the screw, the wedge and the pulley. A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a post. A wedge is an inclined plane that moves.

Remember: a machine changes the size or the direction of the force you need.

Model: machine gallery

Tap or tab to a machine to see how it changes the force, with examples from around Alberta.

Lesson 4 · Work in, work out
F2.K4F2.S4

A machine does not make extra work

The work you put into a machine is the input work. The work the machine does on the load is the output work.

input work = input force × input distance
output work = output force × output distance

In an ideal machine, with no friction, the input work equals the output work. A machine cannot give out more work than you put in.

So how does a ramp help? To raise a 600 N crate 1 m, you must do 600 J of work. Lifting straight up takes 600 N over 1 m. Pushing it up a 4 m ramp takes only 150 N, but over 4 m. The work is the same: 150 × 4 = 600 J. You trade a smaller force for a longer distance.

Real machines have friction, which turns some input work into heat. So real output work is always a little less than input work. Efficiency tells you how much of the input work becomes output work.

efficiency = output work ÷ input work × 100%
Remember: a machine changes the force and the distance, not the amount of work.

Model: push a 600 N crate up a ramp

Change the height and the length of the ramp. Then switch on friction to see a real ramp.

Lesson 5 · Mechanical advantage
F2.K5F2.K4F2.S2

Trading distance for force

Mechanical advantage (MA) tells you how many times a machine multiplies your input force.

MA = output force ÷ input force
ideal MA = input distance ÷ output distance

A machine gives a mechanical advantage when it lets you use a smaller input force by moving that force over a longer distance. On a lever, the side you push is the effort arm. The side holding the load is the load arm. The point it turns on is the fulcrum.

Move the fulcrum close to the load and the effort arm gets long. Your end moves a long way, the load moves a short way, and you need only a small force. That is how a crowbar lifts a heavy rock.

An MA of 4 means the machine pushes on the load with 4 times your force. Some tools, like a canoe paddle or a snow shovel, have an MA less than 1. They need more force, but they make the load move farther and faster.

Remember: more mechanical advantage means less force, but you push over a longer distance.

Model: lift a 400 N rock with a 2 m lever

Slide the fulcrum. Watch the input force and the distance your hand moves to lift the rock 10 cm.

Lesson 6 · Pulleys
F2.K3F2.K5F2.S5

Changing direction and changing size

A pulley is a wheel with a groove that holds a rope. A fixed pulley stays in place, like the one at the top of a flagpole. It only changes the direction of your force. You pull down and the flag goes up. Its MA is 1.

A movable pulley hangs from the rope and moves with the load. When you join fixed and movable pulleys, more rope segments hold up the load. Each segment shares the load.

ideal MA of a pulley system = number of rope segments holding up the load

With 3 supporting segments, you pull with one third of the force. But to lift the load 1 m, you must pull 3 m of rope. The work stays the same.

Remember: a fixed pulley changes direction. More supporting rope segments reduce the force.

Model: lift a 240 N hay bale 1 m

Choose a pulley setup. The drawing is a simple schematic.

Lesson 7 · Complex machines
F2.K6F2.K7F2.S5

Simple machines working together

A complex machine, also called a compound machine, is made of two or more simple machines working together. A wheelbarrow joins a lever (the handles and tray, with the wheel’s axle as the fulcrum) and a wheel and axle. A hand can opener joins levers (the handles), a wheel and axle (the turning key) and a wedge (the sharp cutting wheel).

When one simple machine drives the next, the output force of the first becomes the input force of the second. Each machine multiplies the force again. So the overall MA is the product of the MAs.

MA of complex machine = MA₁ × MA₂ × …
lever (MA 3) driving pulleys (MA 2): 3 × 2 = 6

This product rule works when the machines are linked in a chain. In a wheelbarrow, the wheel mostly makes rolling easier instead of multiplying force, so the wheelbarrow’s MA comes mainly from its lever.

Remember: in a chain of machines, multiply the mechanical advantages.

Lab Bench

Four investigations. Test a ramp, practise work calculations, compare the mechanical advantage of real machines, and analyze work done at places around your community.

Investigation 1 · Ramp trials
F2.S2F2.S4F2.K4

Does a longer ramp save work?

Question: when you push a 600 N crate up to a 1 m high platform, how do the force and the work change with the length of the ramp?

Method: choose a ramp length. Pull the crate up with a spring scale to measure the input force. Repeat for at least four lengths.

Analyze: input work = measured force × ramp length. Output work = 600 N × 1 m = 600 J. Efficiency = output work ÷ input work × 100%.

Worked exampleRamp 3 m long. Measured force 255 N.Input work = 255 N × 3 m = 765 JOutput work = 600 N × 1 m = 600 JEfficiency = 600 ÷ 765 × 100% ≈ 78%

Measurements are simulated with a little friction and random variation, so they are made up for practice.

Investigation 2 · Work calculator
F2.S3F2.S2F2.K2

Solve for work, force or distance

You do not need to memorize the formula. Use the triangle: cover what you want to find.

W = F × d    F = W ÷ d    d = W ÷ F
Worked exampleA farmer lifts a 250 N hay bale 1.2 m onto a wagon. How much work is done?Given: F = 250 N, d = 1.2 mW = F × d = 250 N × 1.2 m = 300 J
Worked examplePulling a toboggan 20 m takes 900 J of work. What force was used?F = W ÷ d = 900 J ÷ 20 m = 45 N

MA = output force ÷ input force

Teacher notes

  • F2.K1: force is taught as a push or pull drawn as an arrow, with magnitude in newtons and a direction. Weight (mass × 9.8 N/kg) is added as context so students can find forces for loads given in kilograms.
  • F2.K2: work is taught as W = F × d where d is the distance moved in the direction of the force. Holding an object still, pushing a wall, and carrying a box at a constant height are used as cases where the force does no work on the object. One joule is shown as one newton moving one metre.
  • F2.K3: all six machines are taught as changing the size of the force, its direction, or both. The screw is described as an inclined plane wrapped around a post, and the wedge as a moving inclined plane. Ideal MA formulas for each machine appear only in the gallery details, as extension.
  • F2.K4: the draft says input work equals output work. The lab teaches this as true for an ideal (frictionless) machine, and states that real machines always put out a little less work than goes in because friction turns some work into heat. Efficiency = output work ÷ input work × 100% is added. This keeps F2.S4 (work does not change) accurate: a machine never increases the work needed.
  • F2.K5: the draft describes mechanical advantage only as reducing the input force. The lab defines MA = output force ÷ input force, with ideal MA = input distance ÷ output distance, and notes that a fixed pulley has an MA of 1 (direction only) and that some tools, such as a canoe paddle or snow shovel, have an MA below 1 and trade force for distance and speed.
  • F2.K6 and K7: wheelbarrow and can opener are analyzed as complex machines. The product rule is taught for machines linked in a chain, where the output of one is the input of the next. The lab notes that a wheelbarrow’s wheel mainly reduces rolling friction, so its MA comes mainly from the lever. MA values for the chain builder and the comparison table are made up for practice.
  • F2.S1: local sites (school flagpole, school entrance ramp, community garden wheelbarrow, an oil field pump jack, loading a canoe) are general Alberta contexts with made-up numbers. No specific site or company is described.
  • Not taught: lever classes, gear ratios and power are left out because the statements do not name them.
