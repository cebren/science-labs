Temperature and Heating Lab

Understanding: Objects or systems experience temperature changes because of energy transfer. When something warms up or cools down, energy has moved into it or out of it. This lab shows what temperature really measures, the three ways heating happens, and how Earth’s temperature depends on the Sun’s radiation.

Learn

Six short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with what temperature measures, then see how heating moves energy, the three ways it happens, how we use it and slow it, and how it sets Earth’s temperature.

Lesson 1 · Particles in motion
EN2.K1EN2.K2

What temperature measures

All matter is made of tiny particles that never stop moving. In a solid they jiggle in place. In a liquid or a gas they move around and bump into each other.

Moving particles have energy of motion. Some particles move fast and some move slowly. Temperature is a measure of their average energy of motion.

The faster the particles move, the greater their average energy of motion, and the higher the temperature. We use temperature to describe how hot or cold something is. In Canada we measure it in degrees Celsius (°C).

Temperature is an average, not a total. A bathtub of warm water has a lower temperature than a cup of hot cocoa, but it holds far more energy in total because it has so many more particles.

Remember: higher temperature means faster particles on average.

Lesson 2 · Hotter to colder
EN2.K3

Heating is energy on the move

Heating is the transfer of energy from a higher‑temperature object or system to a lower‑temperature one.

When a hot mug touches cold hands, the fast particles in the mug bump the slower particles in your hands and speed them up. The mug cools down. Your hands warm up.

On its own, heating always goes from hotter to colder. It keeps going until both reach the same temperature. Scientists call this thermal equilibrium.

Cold is not a thing that flows. A metal railing on a −25 °C morning feels cold because energy leaves your warm hand and moves into the railing.

A fridge or a heat pump can move energy from a colder place to a warmer one, but only because it uses electricity to do work. Without that help, heating goes from hotter to colder.

Remember: energy moves from higher temperature to lower temperature until the temperatures are equal.

Lesson 3 · Three ways
EN2.K4EN2.S1

Conduction, convection and radiation

Heating happens in three ways.

Conduction happens when objects touch. Fast particles bump into their slower neighbours and pass energy along, like a line of people passing a bucket. It works best in solids, and metals are very good at it.

Convection happens in liquids and gases, which can flow. Warmed fluid spreads out, becomes less dense and rises. Cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place. This makes a loop called a convection current, which carries energy around.

Radiation is energy that travels as waves, such as light and infrared. It needs no particles at all, so it can cross empty space. That is how the Sun’s energy reaches Earth.

Remember: conduction needs touching, convection needs a flowing fluid, and radiation needs nothing in between.

Lesson 4 · Heating at work
EN2.K5EN2.S2

Technologies that use heating

People design technologies around each way of heating.

An electric stove uses conduction. The hot element touches the pot, and the pot touches the food.

A hot‑air balloon uses convection. A burner warms the air inside the balloon. Warm air is less dense than the cooler air outside, so it rises and lifts the balloon.

A microwave oven uses radiation. Microwaves are absorbed mostly by the water in food, which warms up. Conduction then spreads that energy deeper into the food.

Most real technologies use more than one process, but one is usually the main one. Tap a technology to see how it works.

Lesson 5 · Slowing the flow
EN2.K6EN2.S3

Insulation slows heat transfer

An insulator is a material that slows the rate of heat transfer. A conductor, such as a metal, lets energy move through it quickly.

Insulation does not stop heating. It slows it down, so hot things stay hot longer and cold things stay cold longer.

Many insulators work by trapping air. Still air is a poor conductor, and when it cannot flow it cannot carry energy by convection. Shiny surfaces also help by reflecting radiation.

Nature uses insulation too. A whale’s thick layer of blubber keeps its body warm in icy ocean water. Technology uses it in coolers, vacuum bottles, house walls and winter parkas.

Remember: insulation slows the rate of energy transfer. It never adds cold or heat.

Lesson 6 · Earth’s energy budget
EN2.K7EN2.K8EN2.K9EN2.S4

How the Sun sets Earth’s temperature

Radiation from the Sun travels through space to Earth. Think of it as 100 units of energy arriving.

About 30 units are reflected straight back to space by clouds, air, snow and ice. About 20 units are absorbed by the atmosphere. About 50 units are absorbed by Earth’s surface, which warms up.

The warm surface gives energy back mostly as invisible infrared radiation. Some also leaves by evaporating water and by rising warm air. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, such as water vapour and carbon dioxide, absorb some of this infrared and send part of it back down. This keeps Earth warmer than it would be without them.

So Earth’s temperature depends on how much radiation is absorbed and how much is reflected. More reflection, such as from fresh snow, means cooling. More absorption, such as from dark ground or more greenhouse gases, means warming.

Remember: Earth’s temperature depends on the balance of radiation absorbed and reflected by the atmosphere and the surface.

Investigation 1 · Mixing water
EN2.K3EN2.S1

Where do the temperatures meet?

When hot water and cold water mix, energy moves from the hot water to the cold water until they reach one temperature. If no energy escapes, the final temperature is a weighted average. You do not need to memorize this formula.

Tfinal = (m1 × T1 + m2 × T2) ÷ (m1 + m2)

Teacher notes

  • EN2.K1 and K2: temperature is taught as a measure of the average energy of motion of particles, kept separate from the total energy of an object (a warm bathtub holds more energy than a hot cup of cocoa but has a lower temperature). The speed readout scales with the square root of absolute temperature, so average speed changes less than students might expect; the animation exaggerates the change so it is visible. The Kelvin scale is not named.
  • EN2.K3: heating is taught as happening on its own from higher to lower temperature until thermal equilibrium. Students are told that cold does not flow. Fridges and heat pumps are mentioned once as devices that use work to move energy from colder to warmer. Investigation 1 adds a weighted-average mixing calculation as a worked example, assuming no energy escapes.
  • EN2.K5: each technology is taught by its main process. An electric coil also glows and radiates, but contact conduction is the main transfer to the pot. The hot-air balloon is taught as heated air becoming less dense and rising, the same idea that drives convection currents. Microwaves are taught as absorbed mainly by water in food, with conduction then spreading energy inward.
  • EN2.K6: insulation is taught as slowing the rate of transfer, never stopping it or adding cold. Trapped still air (limits conduction and convection) and shiny surfaces (reflect radiation) are named as the main ideas.
  • EN2.K7 to K9 and S4: budget numbers are rounded from published global averages: about 30 of 100 units reflected, about 20 absorbed by the atmosphere and about 50 by the surface. The draft says most absorbed energy “radiates back up”; the lab teaches that the surface gives energy back mostly as infrared radiation, with evaporation and rising warm air also named. Greenhouse gases are named with water vapour and carbon dioxide as examples. Climate change is mentioned only as more absorption meaning warming.
  • Model numbers: Earth’s thermostat is a one-layer energy balance model (about −18 °C with no greenhouse effect and about 15 °C with today’s values). Cocoa cooling data come from a simple cooling model with made-up rate values. Reflection values for snow, forest, asphalt and ocean are rounded typical values.
  • Alberta examples: Edmonton winter mornings, block heaters, furnaces, outdoor rinks, bison winter coats, hot-air balloons over the river valley and prairie snow cover are everyday contexts only. Water boiling at about 98 °C in Edmonton matches the Matter lab.
