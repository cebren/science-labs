Design Process Studio Lab

Understanding: Design processes can be used to solve problems. Designers start with the people who will use a solution, try ideas quickly, test them and improve them. This lab walks through design thinking, the steps of a design process, computational thinking, user interfaces and universal design, then lets you solve a real school problem yourself.

Learn

Six short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. One school problem runs through the whole lab: the school boot room gets jammed every winter recess. You will see how designers would tackle it.

Lesson 1 · Design thinking
CS3.K1

Start with the people, not the gadget

Design thinking is a way of solving problems that starts with the people who have the problem. It is called an innovative approach because it often leads to new ideas that nobody would think of from a desk.

A designer first watches and listens. What do people do? What gets in their way? What do they say they need? This is called building empathy: understanding how someone else experiences the problem.

Then the designer writes the problem as a question that opens up many answers. A common form is: “How might we…?”

Compare two starts. “We need more hooks” jumps straight to one solution. “How might we help 60 students get their winter gear on in five minutes?” leaves room for hooks, a new schedule, cubbies, or something nobody has tried yet.

Remember: design thinking asks who has the problem and what they really need before choosing a solution.

Model: from observations to a “How might we” question

Pick a person who uses the boot room. Read what a designer noticed about them. Then pick the need you want to focus on.

    The observations are made up for practice. A real design team would watch and interview real users.

    Lesson 2 · Design processes
    CS3.K2CS3.S1

    A design process is a loop

    A design process is a set of steps for solving a problem. Many versions exist. This curriculum uses five:

    1. Identify the problem. Who has it? What do they need?
    2. Research. Find out more: ask users, measure, look at what others have tried.
    3. Brainstorm solutions. List many ideas without judging them yet.
    4. Plan and develop a prototype. A prototype is a quick, early model of a solution, such as a sketch, cardboard model or practice run.
    5. Test and refine. Try the prototype with real users. Then improve it.

    Design processes are iterative. That means you go around more than once. A test might send you back to brainstorming, or even show that you misunderstood the problem.

    Design is also often collaborative. Teams share ideas, and users help by testing. Different people notice different things.

    Remember: each test teaches you something. Use it to loop back and improve.

    Model: the design loop

    Select a step to see what the boot room team did. Then pick a test result to see where the team loops back.

    Lesson 3 · What counts as a solution
    CS3.K3

    Needs, wants and problems; products, processes and services

    A solution can respond to a need (something people must have to be safe, healthy or able to take part), a want (something that would make life nicer) or a problem (something that is going wrong).

    A solution does not have to be an object. It can be:

    • a product: a thing you can hold or use, like a boot tray or an app;
    • a process: a new way or order of doing something, like letting classes into the boot room in waves;
    • a service: help that people provide, like Grade 7 buddies who help kindergarten students with zippers.

    Some solutions fit more than one box. A school app is a product, and it may also change a process. That is fine. The labels help you think of more kinds of ideas.

    Remember: when you brainstorm, ask “Could this be a product, a process or a service?”

    Model: the solution grid

    Each square holds an example solution. Select a square to read why it fits there.

    Lesson 4 · Computational thinking
    CS3.K4

    Four thinking tools that support design

    Computational thinking is a set of thinking tools that computer scientists use. You do not need a computer to use them. They help with any design problem.

    • Decomposition: breaking a big problem into smaller parts you can solve one at a time.
    • Pattern recognition: noticing what repeats or what is similar, so you can reuse a solution.
    • Abstraction: keeping the important details and leaving out the rest, so the problem is easier to think about.
    • Algorithms: writing clear, ordered steps that solve the problem every time.

    A transit map is a good example of abstraction. It shows the order of the stops and where lines meet. It leaves out the real curves of the track because riders do not need them.

    Remember: break it down, look for patterns, keep what matters, then write the steps.

    Model: thinking tools on the boot room problem

    Pick a tool to see how the team used it.

    Lesson 5 · User interaction
    CS3.K5

    Design for how people will use it

    User interaction is everything that happens when a person uses a solution: what they see, hear, touch and do, and how the solution responds.

    A user interface is the part a person uses to control a solution. On a screen it is the buttons, words and icons. On a door it is the handle. On an elevator it is the panel of buttons and the sound that tells you which floor you are on.

    Good interfaces are easy to understand, give feedback (a sound, light or message that tells you something worked) and let you fix mistakes, for example with a back or undo button.

    A solution can work perfectly inside and still fail if people cannot figure out how to use it.

    Remember: ask “Can every user see it, reach it, understand it and recover from a mistake?”

    Model: design a library self-checkout screen

    Turn interface features on or off. The preview changes, and the user cards show who can use the screen. The users are made-up examples.

    Lesson 6 · Universal design
    CS3.K6CS3.S2

    Design that works for as many people as possible

    Universal design means planning a solution from the start so that people with many different needs and abilities can use it, without needing a special version.

    This matters for accessibility: how easily people, including people with disabilities, can reach, use and understand something. Universal design also helps almost everyone. A curb cut was made for wheelchair users, but it also helps people with strollers, wagons, rolling suitcases and bikes. This is often called the curb cut effect.

    The architect Ronald Mace, who used a wheelchair, made the term popular in the 1980s. His team later wrote seven principles of universal design. You do not need to memorize them. Use them as a checklist when you design or judge a solution.

    Universal design supports the whole design process. When you research, include users with different abilities. When you test, test with them too.

    Remember: design for the widest range of users from the start. Fixing it later usually costs more and works less well.

    Model: who does it help?

    Pick a design feature. See who it helps and which principles it uses.

      Investigation 2 · Write an algorithm
      CS3.K4CS3.S1

      Put the steps in order

      An algorithm only works if its steps are clear and in the right order. You get a random task with its steps mixed up. Move steps up or down, then check.

      Worked example: making a phone call on speaker. 1. Unlock the phone. 2. Open the phone app. 3. Enter the number. 4. Tap call. 5. Tap the speaker button. Step 5 can only come after step 4 because the speaker button appears during a call. Look for steps that depend on other steps.
