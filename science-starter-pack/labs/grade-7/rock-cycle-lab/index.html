Rock Cycle Lab

Understanding: Transformation of rock through the rock cycle results from geological processes. In this lab you will follow rock as it melts, cools, breaks, settles and gets squeezed, test minerals the way geologists do, and sort Alberta rocks into igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic.

Learn

Eight short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Lesson 1 shows the whole rock cycle. Lessons 2 and 3 are about minerals and how to identify them. Lesson 4 shows how to read a rock. Lessons 5 to 7 explain how each type of rock forms. Lesson 8 compares how fast the cycle runs.

Lesson 1 · The big picture

Rocks are recycled

ES2.K1ES2.S1

Rocks seem permanent, but they are not. Over time, rock is formed, broken down and formed again. The processes that do this happen on Earth’s surface and deep below it. Together they are called the rock cycle.

There are three types of rock. Igneous rock forms when melted rock cools. Sedimentary rock forms from pieces of other rock that pile up and stick together. Metamorphic rock forms when heat and pressure change a rock that already exists.

The rock cycle is not a one-way loop. A rock can take many different paths. Igneous rock can become metamorphic rock without ever being broken into sediment. Any rock can melt.

Lesson 2 · What rocks are made of

Four common minerals

ES2.K6

A mineral is a natural, non-living solid with its own chemical makeup. Its particles are arranged in a regular, repeating pattern called a crystal. A rock is a natural solid made of one or more minerals.

Thousands of minerals exist, but a few make up most of the rock you will ever see. Four common ones are quartz, feldspar, mica and calcite.

Granite is a good example. Look closely at a piece and you can see three of these minerals: glassy grey quartz, pink or white feldspar, and small black or silver flakes of mica. Calcite is not in granite. It is the main mineral in limestone and marble.

Lesson 3 · Mineral detective

How to identify a mineral

ES2.K7

Geologists identify minerals by testing their properties. One property is rarely enough, so they use several together.

Colour is the easiest to see but the least reliable, because small impurities change it. Lustre is how the surface reflects light: metallic, glassy, pearly or dull. Streak is the colour of the powder left when you rub a mineral on a rough white tile. Streak is more reliable than colour.

Transparency describes how much light passes through: transparent, translucent or opaque. Hardness is how well a mineral resists being scratched. It is measured on the Mohs scale from 1 (softest) to 10 (hardest). Magnetism means a magnet sticks to it. Only a few minerals, like magnetite, are strongly magnetic.

Lesson 4 · Reading a rock

Texture, layering and minerals

ES2.K2

Rocks are classified by how they formed. You cannot watch a rock form, so you look for clues in its characteristics.

Texture is the size, shape and arrangement of the grains or crystals. Interlocking crystals that grew together point to igneous rock. Rounded grains glued together point to sedimentary rock. Layering is a clue too. Sedimentary rock often forms in flat layers called beds. Metamorphic rock often has minerals lined up in bands or sheets, called foliation. Mineral composition means which minerals are in the rock. Calcite, for example, points to limestone or marble.

Lesson 5 · Sedimentary rock

Layers that turn to stone

ES2.K3

Sediment is loose pieces of rock, minerals, shells or plant remains. Weathering breaks rock into sediment, and water, wind or ice carry it away. When the sediment is dropped, it settles in layers.

As more layers pile on top, their weight squeezes the lower layers. Water is pushed out and grains are packed tightly. This is compaction. Then minerals dissolved in the water, such as calcite or quartz, crystallize between the grains and glue them together. This is cementation.

Sedimentary rock forms on or just below Earth’s surface. Sandstone, shale, conglomerate, limestone and coal are all sedimentary. The badlands near Drumheller and the river valley in Edmonton show layers of sandstone, mudstone and coal.

Lesson 6 · Igneous rock

Rock from fire

ES2.K4

Deep underground, rock can get hot enough to melt. Melted rock below the surface is called magma. When it reaches the surface through a volcano or a crack, it is called lava.

As molten rock cools, its particles lock into crystals. This is crystallization. The result is igneous rock.

Cooling speed controls crystal size. Magma that cools slowly below the surface has time to grow large crystals, like granite. This is intrusive igneous rock. Lava that cools quickly on the surface forms tiny crystals, like basalt. This is extrusive igneous rock. Lava that cools in seconds can form glass with no crystals at all, like obsidian.

Lesson 7 · Metamorphic rock

Changed without melting

ES2.K5

Metamorphic means “changed form”. Metamorphic rock forms below Earth’s surface when great heat and pressure act on a rock that already exists. That starting rock is called the parent rock.

The rock does not melt. Instead, its minerals slowly change and regrow while the rock is still solid. Pressure can squeeze the minerals so they line up in bands or sheets, called foliation.

Shale can become slate, then schist, then gneiss as heat and pressure increase. Limestone becomes marble. Sandstone becomes quartzite. Big Rock near Okotoks is a huge block of quartzite that a glacier carried from the mountains.

Lesson 8 · Fast and slow

The rock cycle runs at many speeds

ES2.K1

The processes in the rock cycle happen at different rates. A lava flow can form a solid crust in hours. Freeze and thaw can crack rock in a single Alberta winter. Turning sediment into solid rock, or changing rock by metamorphism, usually takes thousands to millions of years.

Because times range from hours to millions of years, the timeline uses a special scale. Each step to the right is ten times longer than the one before.

        Station 5 · Rock clock

        How long did the layers take?

        ES2.K1ES2.S1

        If you know how fast sediment piles up, you can estimate how long a layer took to form.

        time = thickness ÷ rate   (t = h ÷ r)
        Worked exampleA mud layer is 3 m thick. Mud settles at 0.5 mm per year. First change metres to millimetres: 3 m = 3000 mm. Then t = 3000 mm ÷ 0.5 mm per year = 6000 years.

        Rates here are made up for practice. Real rates change from place to place.
