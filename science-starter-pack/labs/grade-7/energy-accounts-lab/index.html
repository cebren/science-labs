Science 7 · Energy: Heat and Temperature · EN1

Energy Accounts Lab

Understanding: Transfer of energy can be used to explain change in objects and systems. Scientists keep track of energy the way a careful bookkeeper tracks money: count what comes in, count what goes out, and the difference tells you how the system changed.

Learn

Five short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. First learn what energy is and how it is measured. Then draw a system, see how energy transfers cause change, and keep an energy account.

Lesson 1 · A number, not a stuff
EN1.K1

Energy is a quantity measured in joules

In everyday talk, people say energy as if it were a fluid or a fuel you could pour into a jar. In science, energy is not a substance. It is a quantity: a number that scientists calculate and keep track of.

Energy is measured in joules (J). One joule is a small amount. Lifting an apple from the floor to a table top takes about 1 J. Large amounts use kilojoules (1 kJ = 1000 J) and megajoules (1 MJ = 1 000 000 J).

A useful way to think about energy: it tells how much work could be done on an object or in a system. Work is done when a force moves something. A system with more energy can do more work, like lifting more apples.

Think of a bank account. Money is not a thing you can see inside the bank. It is a number that goes up when money comes in and down when it goes out. Energy is tracked the same way.

Remember: energy is an amount, measured in joules. It is never a material you can hold.

Lesson 2 · Draw a boundary
EN1.K2

Systems and surroundings

To study energy, scientists first choose a system: the part of the world they want to study. A system is described by its components (its parts) and the interactions between them, such as one part pushing, rubbing or warming another.

Everything outside the system is the surroundings. An imaginary line called the system boundary separates the two.

You get to choose where the boundary goes. A kettle can be the system, or just the water inside it can be. The choice changes what counts as energy coming in or going out.

Tip: always say what your system is before you describe any energy transfer.

Lesson 3 · Transfers cause change
EN1.K3

Energy transfer drives change

When energy moves into or out of an object, something about it changes. It may speed up, slow down, rise, bend, warm up or melt.

One way to transfer energy is by doing work. Work is done when a force moves an object in the direction of the force. The amount of work equals the energy transferred:

work = force × distance
W = F × d  (newtons × metres = joules)

So when you see a change caused by a push or a pull, you know work was done and energy was transferred. If the object does not move, no work is done, even if you push hard.

Work is not the only way. Energy also transfers by heating, from a hotter object to a colder one. A snowball melting in your glove changed because of heating, not a push. You will study heating in the next lesson set.

Lesson 4 · Keep the account
EN1.K4

Energy in, energy out

Energy is never created from nothing and never destroyed. It only moves. That means you can keep an energy account for any system:

change in the system’s energy = energy in − energy out
ΔE = Ein − Eout

The symbol Δ (delta) means “change in”.

  • More energy in than out: the system’s energy goes up.
  • More energy out than in: the system’s energy goes down.
  • Equal in and out: the system’s energy stays the same, even though energy is still flowing through it.

A house in an Edmonton January is a good example. The furnace transfers energy in. The house loses energy to the cold air outside. When the two are equal, the temperature inside stays steady.

Lesson 5 · Into, within, out of
EN1.K2EN1.K4EN1.S1

Energy transfer diagrams

An energy transfer diagram shows the whole story of a system:

  • Into the system: arrows crossing the boundary from the surroundings.
  • Within the system: transfers from one component to another. These do not change the total.
  • Out of the system: arrows crossing the boundary into the surroundings.

Wide arrows mean more joules. The change in the system is what came in minus what went out.

Much of the energy that leaves a system ends up warming the air and objects around it, often through friction or sound. It is not lost from the universe. It is just spread out and hard to use again.

Where does a bouncing ball’s energy go?

Teacher notes

  • EN1.K1: the draft says energy “indicates the amount of work that can be done”. The lab uses this as a helpful description (“how much work could be done”) and keeps the main idea that energy is a quantity in joules, not a substance. Strictly, energy is the capacity to do work or to heat, and not all of a system’s energy can be turned into work; thermal energy spread into the surroundings is hard to use again. This is mentioned in Lesson 5 without the word entropy.
  • EN1.K3: the draft says changes “indicate that work has been done”. The lab teaches that a change shows energy was transferred, and that the transfer can be by work (a force moving an object) or by heating (a temperature difference). Heating is named briefly so the next understanding (EN2) does not contradict this one. Students are shown that pushing without movement does no work.
  • EN1.K3 and EN1.S2: W = F × d is borrowed from Forces (F2.K2) as context so students can analyze work in joules. For lifting, the force equals the weight (about 9.8 N per kilogram, rounded to 10 N per kilogram only where stated). Gravitational, kinetic and other energy stores are not named as a list, since they belong to Science 8.
  • EN1.K4: taught as ΔE = Ein − Eout, including the steady state (in = out) and the idea that energy is conserved. Open, closed and isolated systems are not taught by name.
  • Numbers: joule ladder values are rounded estimates (apple 100 g lifted 1 m about 1 J; 45 kg student up 3 m about 1300 J; 10 W LED for 1 h 36 000 J; 1 L of water heated from 20 °C to boiling about 335 000 J; 100 food Calories about 420 000 J; 1 kWh = 3 600 000 J). Transfer diagram numbers, bouncing ball data and practice problems are made up for practice and labelled as such.
  • Alberta examples: tobogganing, an outdoor rink, a block heater on a cold Edmonton morning, a furnace in January and cycling in the river valley are everyday contexts only.

Indigenous ways of knowing, doing and being

Sun, wind and the qulliq

This resource was made in Edmonton, in Treaty 6 territory. We acknowledge the nêhiyaw (Cree), Dene, Anishinaabe (Saulteaux), Nakota Isga (Nakota Sioux) and Niitsitapi (Blackfoot) peoples, the Métis, whose homeland this is, and the Inuit, one of whose largest communities south of the 60th parallel lives in Edmonton. We also honour all First Nations, Métis and Inuit peoples whose knowledge shapes these lands.

Indigenous nations and organizations in Edmonton, across Alberta and in Inuit Nunangat are building, owning and naming energy systems. Each one can be drawn like the systems in this lab: energy comes in from the surroundings, moves within the system and goes out. These connections use the names that nations themselves have given, with a source for each name.

Edmonton · Cree: Enoch Cree Nation

kīsikāw pīsim: daylight sun in the river valley

Beside Edmonton's E.L. Smith Water Treatment Plant, EPCOR's solar farm uses 30,350 solar panels to turn sunlight into electricity. EPCOR says the farm makes more than 20,000 megawatt hours of energy each year. In 2022 Enoch Cree Nation gifted the farm its Cree name, kīsikāw pīsim, which EPCOR says means "daylight sun." The farm is in the city's sipiwiyiniwak ward. The City says sipiwiyiniwak means the River Cree, a name Enoch Cree Nation members were known by because they lived close to the river. Chief Billy Morin said the name acknowledges EPCOR's desire "to steward these lands."

Alberta · Dene: K’ai Tailé Dené (Athabasca Chipewyan First Nation), Cree: Mikisew Cree First Nation, Métis: Fort Chipewyan Métis Association

Fort Chipewyan: sunlight in, less diesel

Fort Chipewyan, in Treaty 8 territory, is not connected to Alberta's main power grid. CBC reports its diesel power station burned three million litres of fuel a year, trucked in on ice roads or brought by river barge. In 2020 Three Nations Energy opened a 2.2 MW solar farm there. It is owned by the Athabasca Chipewyan First Nation, who call themselves K’ai Tailé Dené, "people of the land of the willow," the Mikisew Cree First Nation and the Fort Chipewyan Métis Association. CBC reports it should replace about 800,000 litres of diesel a year. Mikisew Cree Chief Peter Powder said: "we're switching over to renewable energy."

Source: CBC News: Indigenous-owned solar farm opens in remote northern Alberta community (owners, 2.2 MW, diesel figures, quotes) · CTV News Edmonton: Fort Chipewyan opens solar farm designed to provide 25 per cent of community's power (not on Alberta's main grid) · Source of the name K’ai Tailé Dené (people of the land of the willow) and of Treaty 8 signed at Fort Chipewyan in 1899: Athabasca Chipewyan First Nation, About Us

Canada · Inuit

Tarquti: tending energy in Nunavik

Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami calls Inuit Nunangat the Inuit homeland in Canada, including Nunavik in Northern Quebec. Makivvik says all of Nunavik's electricity is produced by diesel generators. Tarquti Energy, which Makivvik says will be 100% Inuit-owned, aims to "allow Inuit to control the long-term development of renewable energy in Nunavik." Nunatsiaq News explains that a tarquti is the tool used to tend to a qulliq, the traditional oil lamp. In Iqaluit, Alacie Joamie said, "We survived because of the qulliq," the most reliable way to make heat while hunting and camping. Hydro-Québec says Tarquti's planned wind projects would supply about 60% of the electricity needs of Quaqtaq and Puvirnituq.

Source: Source of the names Inuit Nunangat (the Inuit homeland in Canada) and Nunavik: Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami, Inuit Nunangat Map · Makivvik: Tarquti Energy Corporation (100% Inuit-owned; Nunavik electricity from diesel) · Source of the name tarquti (the tool used to tend to a qulliq): Nunatsiaq News, Nunavik's new renewable energy firm makes its first move · Source of the name qulliq (traditional oil lamp) and Alacie Joamie's words: Nunatsiaq News, Iqaluit residents gather for an elder's qulliq lesson · Hydro-Québec: A collaboration agreement and two new wind power projects in Nunavik (Quaqtaq and Puvirnituq)

Talk and think

  • Draw an energy transfer diagram with the kīsikāw pīsim solar farm and the water treatment plant together as one system. What energy comes in from the surroundings, what moves within, and what goes out? Now move the boundary so the system is only the plant. Which arrow now crosses the boundary?
  • One kilowatt hour is 3 600 000 J, so one megawatt hour is 3 600 000 000 J. Change EPCOR's "more than 20,000 megawatt hours" a year into joules. Then use the Fort Chipewyan numbers: if the station burned three million litres of diesel a year and the solar farm replaces about 800,000, about what fraction of the fuel coming into the power station's account is no longer needed?
  • Make a qulliq your system. Name its components, the energy that comes in with the fuel, and where energy goes out to the surroundings. As the oil burns, does the energy of the system go up or down? Then look at a wind turbine in Quaqtaq: where is a force moving something, so that work is done?
