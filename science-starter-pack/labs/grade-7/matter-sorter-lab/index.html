Science 7 · Matter: Pure Substances and Mixtures · M1

Matter Sorter Lab

Understanding: Matter can be classified based on physical properties and separated according to its particles. Everything around you is either a pure substance or a mixture. You can tell them apart, identify them by their properties, and pull mixtures apart without changing the particles inside.

Learn

Seven short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. First sort matter into two big groups, then learn the properties that identify pure substances, and finish with the ways people separate mixtures.

Lesson 1 · Two big groups
M1.K1M1.K2M1.K5

Pure substance or mixture?

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. All matter is made of tiny particles that are far too small to see.

Scientists sort matter into two groups by looking at its particles:

  • A pure substance has only one type of particle. Copper wire and distilled water are pure substances.
  • A mixture has two or more types of particles mixed together. Salt water, air and trail mix are mixtures.

The particles in a mixture keep their own identity. That is why a mixture can be separated by physical techniques, such as filtering or boiling, which do not change what the particles are.

Remember: one type of particle means pure substance. Two or more types means mixture.

Particle pictures are models. Real particles are far smaller and move all the time.

Lesson 2 · One type of particle
M1.K2

Pure substances stay together

Every particle in a pure substance is the same. In pure copper every particle is a copper atom. In pure water every particle is a water molecule.

Because there is only one type of particle, there is nothing to sort out. If you filter pure water, pure water comes through. If you boil it and cool the steam, you collect pure water again. Physical techniques cannot separate a pure substance into anything else.

Pure substances come in two kinds. An element, such as copper or oxygen, is made of one kind of atom. A compound, such as water or table salt, is made of two or more kinds of atoms that are joined together. Each water particle is two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom.

A compound can only be broken into its elements by a chemical change, such as passing electricity through water. That makes new substances, so it is not a physical separation. You will study chemical changes in later grades.

Lesson 3 · Fingerprints for substances
M1.K3

Physical properties identify pure substances

A physical property is a feature you can observe or measure without changing the substance into something new. Each pure substance has its own set of values, like a fingerprint. Useful ones include:

  • Appearance: colour, and lustre, which is how shiny a surface is. Copper is reddish and shiny. Sulfur is yellow and dull.
  • Density: how much mass is packed into each unit of volume (Lesson 4).
  • Melting point: the temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid.
  • Freezing point: the temperature at which a liquid turns into a solid.
  • Boiling point: the temperature at which a liquid boils and turns into a gas.

For a pure substance, the melting point and the freezing point are the same temperature. Water melts at 0 °C and freezes at 0 °C. A pure substance also melts and boils at one sharp temperature. Mixtures usually melt or boil over a range of temperatures instead.

Boiling points in this lab are measured at sea level. In Edmonton, about 670 m above sea level, the air pressure is a little lower, so water boils at about 98 °C.

Lesson 4 · Mass per volume
M1.K4M1.K3

Density

Density is the mass per unit volume of a substance. It tells you how tightly matter is packed.

d = m ÷ V    (density = mass ÷ volume)

Mass (m) is measured in grams (g). Volume (V) is measured in cubic centimetres (cm³) for solids or millilitres (mL) for liquids. One millilitre equals one cubic centimetre. So density is given in g/cm³ or g/mL.

Worked exampleAn aluminum block has a mass of 54 g and a volume of 20 cm³.d = m ÷ V = 54 g ÷ 20 cm³ = 2.7 g/cm³

The density of a pure substance stays the same no matter how big the sample is. A paper clip of iron and a whole iron beam both have a density of about 7.9 g/cm³. That is why density helps identify a substance.

Objects less dense than water (1.0 g/cm³) float in it. Objects more dense than water sink.

Density changes a little with temperature, because most substances expand when heated. That is why ice (0.92 g/cm³) floats on liquid water.

Lesson 5 · Can you see the parts?
M1.K5M1.K6

Heterogeneous and homogeneous mixtures

A mixture has two or more types of particles. Mixtures come in two kinds.

A heterogeneous mixture is not the same all the way through. Different parts of it have different particles. A mechanical mixture is a heterogeneous mixture where you can see the separate parts, like trail mix, gravel or a salad. Muddy water is also heterogeneous: if it sits, the mud settles to the bottom.

A homogeneous mixture looks the same all the way through, even under a microscope. Its particles are spread out evenly. A solution is a homogeneous mixture, such as salt water, apple juice, vinegar or air. Some solids are solutions too: brass is a solution of copper and zinc.

Quick test: if one scoop of the mixture could have different stuff in it than another scoop, it is heterogeneous. If every scoop is identical, it is homogeneous.

Model: mixture magnifier

Lesson 6 · Size, density and magnets
M1.K7

Separating heterogeneous mixtures

Heterogeneous mixtures can be pulled apart with physical techniques. Each technique uses a difference between the parts.

  • Mechanical separation uses particle size and density.
    • Sorting: picking pieces out by hand or by machine, like picking raisins out of trail mix.
    • Sieving: shaking the mixture through a mesh. Small pieces fall through and big ones stay on top.
    • Filtration: pouring a liquid through filter paper. The liquid and anything dissolved pass through. Solid bits are too big and stay behind.
    • Settling and decanting: letting denser solids sink, then pouring off the liquid on top. Panning for gold uses density too.
  • Magnetic separation uses magnetism. A magnet pulls out iron, nickel or steel and leaves everything else behind.

Alberta uses these ideas every day. Recycling centres use big magnets to pull steel cans out of mixed recycling. Water treatment plants on the North Saskatchewan River let silt settle, then filter the water. In the oil sands, hot water and air bubbles help bitumen float to the top while sand sinks.

Lesson 7 · Boil, cool, collect
M1.K8M1.K3

Distillation separates solutions

A solution cannot be filtered: its particles are spread out evenly and are small enough to pass through filter paper. Instead, we use differences in boiling point.

Distillation works in three steps:

  1. Heat the solution. The part with the lowest boiling point boils first and turns into a gas.
  2. The gas travels into a cool tube called a condenser, where it turns back into a liquid.
  3. That liquid, called the distillate, drips into a clean container.

Distilling salt water collects pure water and leaves the salt behind in the flask. Salt boils at 1465 °C, so it never gets near boiling.

Refineries near Edmonton use a bigger version called fractional distillation to separate crude oil into parts like gasoline, diesel and asphalt, each with its own boiling range.

When two liquids have boiling points close together, such as water (100 °C) and ethanol (78 °C), one distillation gives a liquid that is richer in ethanol but not pure. Repeating the process makes it purer.
