Energy Flow and Cycles Lab

Understanding: Ecosystem stability is maintained through the processes of cycling of matter and transfer of energy. Matter goes around and gets reused. Energy comes in from the Sun, passes along, and leaves as heat.

Learn

Seven short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with the big idea, then follow water, carbon and energy through an Alberta ecosystem.

Lesson 1 · The big idea

Matter cycles. Energy flows.

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space: water, carbon, oxygen, the atoms in a leaf. Earth does not get new matter delivered, so ecosystems reuse the same atoms again and again. That reuse is a cycle.

Energy is different. It arrives as sunlight, gets stored in food, passes from one living thing to the next, and at every step some of it leaves as heat. Heat spreads out into the surroundings and cannot be used again by living things. So energy moves in one direction. That is a flow.

An ecosystem stays stable when both keep going: matter keeps being recycled, and new sunlight keeps arriving to replace the energy that leaves.

Remember: atoms go around in loops; energy goes through in one direction and needs a constant supply from the Sun.

Model: follow matter or follow energy

Lesson 2 · Water
LS2.K1

The water cycle keeps life going

Every cell needs water. Water carries nutrients, helps control body temperature, and is a raw ingredient of photosynthesis. The water cycle moves the same water between the air, the land, lakes, oceans and living things, over and over.

The Sun powers it. Sunlight warms water so it evaporates. Plants release water vapour from their leaves by transpiration. Vapour cools and condenses into clouds, then falls as precipitation (rain or snow). It runs off into streams and rivers, or soaks into the ground to become groundwater. Roots pull it up, and animals drink it.

Edmonton's drinking water comes from the North Saskatchewan River. Much of that water starts as snow and glacier ice in the Rocky Mountains, which melts each spring and summer and flows east through Treaty 6 territory.

Lesson 3 · Carbon
LS2.K2

The carbon cycle

Carbon is the backbone of every living thing. Sugars, proteins and fats are all built around carbon atoms. Carbon moves between the air, living things, the ocean and rocks through six main processes:

  • Photosynthesis: producers take carbon dioxide (CO₂) from the air or water and build it into sugar.
  • Respiration: living things break sugar down for energy and release CO₂.
  • Decomposition: fungi and bacteria break down dead matter and waste, releasing CO₂ and returning nutrients to the soil.
  • Emission: carbon is released into the air by burning, such as fossil fuels in vehicles or forests in a wildfire.
  • Ocean exchange: CO₂ dissolves into ocean water and also escapes back out.
  • Geological processes: over millions of years, buried remains become coal, oil or limestone, and volcanoes release carbon back into the air.

Eating moves carbon too: when a hare eats aspen bark, the carbon in the bark becomes part of the hare.

Lesson 4 · The Sun and producers
LS2.K3

Producers capture the Sun's energy

The Sun is the main source of energy for most ecosystems. Producers are living things that make their own food. Plants, algae and some bacteria capture light energy and store it as chemical energy in sugar. This is photosynthesis:

carbon dioxide + water + light energy → glucose (sugar) + oxygen
6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

Every other living thing in the ecosystem depends on that stored energy, either by eating producers or by eating something that ate them. Producers capture only a small share of the sunlight that hits them, often around 1 percent, yet that small share powers the whole food web.

Why "most" ecosystems? Deep on the ocean floor, near hot-water vents, there is no sunlight. Bacteria there make food from chemicals coming out of the vents. This is called chemosynthesis.

Lesson 5 · Who eats whom
LS2.K4

Consumers and trophic levels

Consumers get energy by eating other living things. Each feeding step is a trophic level ("trophic" means feeding).

  • Primary consumers eat producers. Example: a snowshoe hare eating willow.
  • Secondary consumers eat primary consumers. Example: a boreal chorus frog eating a grasshopper.
  • Tertiary consumers eat secondary consumers. Example: a garter snake eating that frog.
  • Apex predators are at the top of a food chain. Adults have no natural predators.

An apex predator is defined by being at the top, not by a level number. A grey wolf eating a moose is a secondary consumer and also the apex predator. Many animals also feed at more than one level: a red fox eats saskatoon berries (primary consumer) and mice (secondary consumer).

Lesson 6 · Energy leaves at every step
LS2.K5

Why there are more producers than consumers

When a hare eats bark, only a small part of the energy in that bark ends up stored in the hare's body. The rest is used up or lost. Most of the energy is used to stay alive (breathing, moving, keeping warm) and eventually becomes heat. Some leaves in droppings, which decomposers then use. Parts like bones and fur may never be eaten.

Where 100 kJ from aspen bark goes in a hare (example numbers; real values vary)

50 heat
40 waste
10

Only about 10 kJ is stored as new body tissue, which is all a lynx could get by eating the hare.

Scientists often use a rough rule: about 10 percent of the energy at one level is passed to the next. The real number is usually somewhere from about 5 to 20 percent. Because so much is lost each step, an ecosystem needs a large amount of producer energy to support a few top predators. Food chains rarely have more than four or five levels.

energy at next level = energy at this level × transfer efficiency
E₂ = E₁ × 0.10 (using the 10 percent rule)

Lesson 7 · Putting it together
LS2.K6

Food webs and energy pyramids

A food chain shows one path. Real ecosystems have many connected chains, so scientists draw a food web. Each arrow points from the food to the eater, showing which way energy moves.

Decomposers such as fungi, bacteria and earthworms feed on dead matter and waste from every level. They release the last of that energy as heat and return carbon and nutrients to the air and soil, where producers can use them again.

An energy pyramid shows the same ecosystem a different way. Each layer is a trophic level, and its size shows how much energy it holds. The pyramid is always widest at the bottom because energy is lost at every step.

A pyramid that counts numbers of organisms can look different. One aspen tree can feed thousands of caterpillars, so a "pyramid of numbers" can be narrow at the bottom. An energy pyramid never is.

Lab Bench

Five investigations. Change a variable, make a prediction, and check your thinking with new practice questions each time.

Investigation 1
LS2.S1

Dry year, wet year

Change how much rain and snow falls on the North Saskatchewan River watershed in a year. Watch how the water cycle's supply to living things changes.

Think about it: which living things depend on rivers, which depend on soil moisture, and which depend on wetlands?

Ways the water cycle supports life
  • Precipitation refills soil water that roots take up.
  • Runoff and snowmelt keep rivers flowing for fish, beavers and towns.
  • Groundwater feeds springs and wetlands during dry months.
  • Evaporation and transpiration return water to the air so it can fall again somewhere else.
  • Water in lakes and rivers moderates temperature and holds dissolved oxygen for fish.

    Investigation 4
    LS2.S4

    How much energy got through?

    Ecologists measure how much energy is stored in each level to find the transfer efficiency: the percentage passed on to the next level.

    efficiency (%) = energy at higher level ÷ energy at lower level × 100
    energy lost = energy at lower level − energy at higher level
    Worked example Willow shrubs in a wetland store 24 000 kJ. The moose that eat them store 2 160 kJ. 2 160 ÷ 24 000 × 100 = 9% Lost: 24 000 − 2 160 = 21 840 kJ (91%) Most of the lost energy became heat from the moose's body and the decomposers that broke down its droppings.
