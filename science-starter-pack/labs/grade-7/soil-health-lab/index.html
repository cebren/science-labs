Understanding: Geological processes and human activity can affect the nature of soil. In this lab you will watch soil form from bare rock, compare sand, clay and loam, find out what makes soil healthy, and test ways that people in Alberta protect it.

Learn

Six short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Lesson 1 shows how soil forms. Lesson 2 compares soil types. Lesson 3 explains what shapes a soil. Lessons 4 and 5 are about healthy soil and why it matters. Lesson 6 shows how people protect soil.

Lesson 1 · Where soil comes from

A thin layer that takes a long time to make

ES3.K1

Soil is the thin layer of loose material that covers much of Earth’s land. It is a mix of minerals (tiny bits of rock) and organic matter (material from living things, like dead leaves, roots and animal waste).

Three processes build soil. Weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces. Erosion moves those pieces by water, wind, ice or gravity and drops them somewhere new. Decomposition is when bacteria, fungi and small animals break down dead things into dark, rich material called humus.

Soil is thin. In most places it is less than two metres deep. If Earth were an apple, the soil would be much thinner than the peel. It also forms slowly. A few centimetres can take hundreds of years or more, so soil lost in one storm can take lifetimes to replace.

Much of Alberta’s soil formed on material left behind by glaciers at the end of the last ice age, roughly 10 000 to 12 000 years ago.

Lesson 2 · Sand, silt, clay and loam

Soil types and their properties

ES3.K2

Mineral bits in soil come in three sizes. Sand grains are the biggest (0.05 to 2 mm). You can see them and feel them as gritty. Silt is smaller (0.002 to 0.05 mm) and feels smooth like flour. Clay particles are the smallest (less than 0.002 mm). Wet clay feels sticky.

A sandy soil is mostly sand. A clay soil is mostly clay. A loam is a balanced mix of sand, silt and clay, usually with plenty of organic matter. Most garden plants grow best in loam.

Scientists describe soil by its physical properties. Composition is what it is made of. Colour gives clues: dark soil usually has more organic matter. Odour gives clues too: a sweet, earthy smell comes from living soil microbes, while a sour or rotten smell often means waterlogged soil with little oxygen. Pore size is the size of the spaces between particles.

Try it: choose a soil to see its particles under a magnifier and compare its properties.

Lesson 3 · What shapes a soil

Parent material, climate and living things

ES3.K3ES3.S2

No two places have exactly the same soil. Three main factors decide what a soil is like.

Parent material is the rock or loose deposit that the soil formed from. It sets the minerals and the texture. Sandy river deposits make sandy soil. Fine glacial deposits can make soil with more clay.

Climate means the usual temperature and precipitation. Rain and freeze-thaw cycles speed up weathering. Heavy rain can also wash nutrients deep into the soil. Very dry places build soil slowly.

Biotic components are the living things. Grass roots grow and die every year and add lots of organic matter deep in the soil. Forests drop leaves and needles on top. Earthworms, insects, fungi and bacteria mix and decompose it all.

Soil scientists also count time and the shape of the land. Soil on a steep slope stays thin because it erodes, while soil at the bottom of the slope builds up.

Lesson 4 · Inside healthy soil

Healthy soil is a living mixture

ES3.K4

Healthy soil is not just dirt. It is a complex mixture of minerals, organic matter, water, nutrients and gases such as oxygen and carbon dioxide. It is also home to many organisms. A handful of healthy soil can hold more tiny living things than there are people on Earth.

About half of a good soil is solid: mostly minerals with a little organic matter. The other half is pore space filled with water and air. Roots and soil organisms need the oxygen in that air. As they use it, they give off carbon dioxide, so soil air has more carbon dioxide than the air we breathe.

Nutrients are chemicals that plants need, such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium. They come from weathered minerals and from decomposed organic matter.

Lesson 5 · Why healthy soil matters

From soil parts to big benefits

ES3.K5ES3.S3

Healthy soil improves six big things.

Food security: people can reliably get enough healthy food. Climate resilience: land copes better with drought, floods and heat. Nutrient cycling: nutrients move from dead things back into living things. Air and water quality: soil filters water and stores carbon that would otherwise add to the air. Biodiversity: soil is home to a huge variety of life. Sustainable agriculture: farms can keep producing food for many generations.

Each part of healthy soil leads to some of these benefits. To explain one, follow the chain: a component does a job, and that job leads to an improvement.

Lesson 6 · Caring for soil

Six ways people protect soil

ES3.K6ES3.S4

Bare soil erodes. Rain splashes it loose and runoff carries it away. Wind blows dry topsoil off fields. In the 1930s, drought and heavy tilling let wind strip topsoil from many prairie farms. People call that time the Dirty Thirties.

Today farmers and ranchers use practices that keep soil covered, held by roots and full of organic matter. Cover crops are plants grown to cover the soil between main crops. Mulching means covering soil with straw, wood chips or leftover crop stalks. Conservation tillage means plowing less or not at all and leaving crop stubble in place. Buffer strips are bands of grass, shrubs or trees along streams and field edges that trap soil and slow runoff.

Rotational grazing moves animals from one pasture to the next so plants and roots can recover. Controlled burning is a planned, carefully managed fire on grassland or pasture. It clears old dead grass, returns some nutrients to the soil and helps new grass grow. First Nations peoples have used fire to care for prairie and parkland for a very long time. Burning must be planned carefully, because bare burnt ground can erode until plants grow back.

Station 1 · Controlled experiment

Which soil grows the tallest bean plants?

ES3.S1

A controlled experiment changes one thing on purpose and keeps everything else the same. That way you know the change caused the result.

The manipulated variable is the one thing you change. The responding variable is what you measure. Controlled variables are everything you keep the same. Using several pots for each soil, called repeated trials, makes your results more reliable.

Worked example: average height average = (sum of heights) ÷ (number of pots) Three loam pots grew 11.2 cm, 12.6 cm and 10.9 cm. average = (11.2 + 12.6 + 10.9) ÷ 3 = 34.7 ÷ 3 ≈ 11.6 cm

Results come from a made-up model with natural variation, for practice only.

Station 2 · Jar test

What is this soil made of?

ES3.K2

Shake soil with water in a jar and let it settle for a day. Sand settles first, at the bottom. Silt settles next. Clay settles last, on top. Bits of organic matter float. Measure each layer to find the composition.

percent of a layer = (layer thickness ÷ total thickness) × 100
Worked example Sand 24 mm, silt 16 mm, clay 10 mm. Total = 50 mm. sand = 24 ÷ 50 × 100 = 48% silt = 16 ÷ 50 × 100 = 32% clay = 10 ÷ 50 × 100 = 20% A mix with no single size in charge, like this one, is a loam.

Simplified soil type rule used here: clay 40% or more is a clay soil. Sand 70% or more with little clay is a sandy soil. Silt 80% or more is a silty soil. A balanced mix is a loam. Practice jars are made up.

Station 3 · Soil detective

Identify the mystery soil

ES3.K2ES3.S2

Read the field notes for each sample. Use the physical properties to decide what type of soil it is. Then decide which soil-forming factor best explains it.

Remember the clues. Gritty and fast draining points to sand. Sticky, slow draining and cracking when dry points to clay. Dark, crumbly and earthy points to loam with plenty of organic matter.

Station 5 · Alberta soil cases

Human activity and local soil health

ES3.S4ES3.K6

People change soil every day. Some activities harm it by leaving it bare, packing it down or removing topsoil. Others improve it by adding cover, roots and organic matter.

For each case, decide whether the activity mostly helps or harms the soil. Then pick the practice that would help most. Think about what the activity does to soil cover, roots, organic matter and erosion.

Cases are general examples, not reports about real sites.
