Moon and Tides Lab

Understanding: Interactions of celestial bodies are observed and experienced on Earth. In this lab you will model how the Sun, Earth and Moon move and pull on each other, and use those models to explain the phases of the Moon, the tides and solar and lunar eclipses.

Learn

Six short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Lesson 1 sets up the Sun, Earth and Moon. Lessons 2 and 3 explain the Moon’s phases and the tides. Lessons 4 to 6 explain eclipses.

Lesson 1 · Sky events

Three bodies, three things we notice on Earth

SP1.K1

A celestial body is a natural object in space, such as a star, a planet or a moon. The three that matter most in this lab are the Sun, Earth and the Moon.

These bodies interact. Gravity holds Earth in its orbit around the Sun and holds the Moon in its orbit around Earth. An orbit is the curved path one body follows around another. Sunlight shines on Earth and the Moon, and each one casts a shadow into space.

Those interactions cause three natural events that people on Earth can see or feel: tides, eclipses and lunar phases. People have watched all three for thousands of years.

Lesson 2 · Lunar phases

The Moon is always half lit

SP1.K5SP1.S3

The Moon makes no light of its own. We see it because it reflects sunlight. At every moment, the half of the Moon facing the Sun is lit and the other half is dark.

As the Moon orbits Earth, the angle between the Sun, Earth and the Moon changes. So we see a different amount of the lit half each night. These observed changes are called lunar phases. The full cycle repeats about every 29.5 days.

The four main phases are new moon, first quarter, full moon and last quarter. Between them are crescent and gibbous phases. While the lit part grows, the Moon is waxing. While it shrinks, the Moon is waning.

Common mix-up: phases are not caused by Earth’s shadow. Earth’s shadow only falls on the Moon during a lunar eclipse.

From Alberta, a waxing Moon is lit on the right side and a waning Moon is lit on the left. In the Southern Hemisphere the view is flipped. Many cultures, including First Nations, Métis and Inuit peoples, have long followed the Moon’s cycle to mark time through the year.

Lesson 3 · Tides

The Moon and the Sun pull on the oceans

SP1.K2SP1.S1

Tides are the regular rise and fall of the ocean surface. They result from the gravitational forces of the Moon and the Sun.

The Moon pulls hardest on the ocean on the side of Earth nearest to it, and weakest on the far side. That difference stretches the oceans into two bulges: one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side. As Earth spins, a coast passes through both bulges. So most coasts get two high tides and two low tides about every 24 hours and 50 minutes.

The Sun pulls on the oceans too. Its effect on tides is about half as strong as the Moon’s, because the Sun is so far away. When the Sun and Moon line up, at new moon and full moon, their effects add up to bigger spring tides. When they are at right angles, at the quarter moons, the effects partly cancel and we get smaller neap tides.

The Bay of Fundy, between New Brunswick and Nova Scotia, has some of the highest tides in the world, up to about 16 m. Alberta has no ocean coast, and tides in our lakes are far too small to notice.

Lesson 4 · Solar eclipses

When the Moon blocks the Sun

SP1.K3SP1.S2

A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun. The Moon casts its shadow on a small part of Earth. This can only happen at new moon.

A shadow has two parts. The umbra is the dark centre, where all of the Sun’s light is blocked. The penumbra is the lighter outer part, where only some light is blocked. People in the umbra see a total solar eclipse. People in the much wider penumbra see a partial eclipse. Everyone else sees no eclipse at all.

The umbra makes a narrow path across Earth, usually less than about 270 km wide, and totality lasts only a few minutes in any one place. On April 8, 2024, a total solar eclipse crossed parts of Ontario, Quebec and Atlantic Canada.

Eye safety: never look straight at the Sun, even when most of it is covered. Use certified eclipse glasses or a pinhole projector.

Lesson 5 · Lunar eclipses

When Earth’s shadow falls on the Moon

SP1.K4SP1.S2

A lunar eclipse happens when Earth is directly between the Sun and the Moon. Earth casts its shadow on the Moon. This can only happen at full moon.

If the whole Moon passes through Earth’s umbra, it is a total lunar eclipse. If only part of the Moon enters the umbra, it is a partial lunar eclipse.

During totality the Moon often looks coppery red. Earth’s air bends a little sunlight into the shadow, and the air scatters away most of the blue light, just like at sunset. So reddish light still reaches the Moon.

A lunar eclipse can be seen from anywhere on Earth’s night side, so many more people see one than see a total solar eclipse. It is safe to look at with your eyes.

Lesson 6 · Why not every month?

The Moon’s orbit is tilted

SP1.K3SP1.K4SP1.S2

There is a new moon and a full moon every month, but there is not an eclipse every month. Why?

The Moon’s orbit is tilted about 5° compared with Earth’s orbit around the Sun. Most months, the new moon passes a little above or below the Sun, and the full moon passes above or below Earth’s shadow. So nothing is blocked.

An eclipse can only happen when a new moon or full moon falls near the place where the Moon’s orbit crosses the plane of Earth’s orbit. Then the Sun, Earth and the Moon line up closely. This happens during eclipse seasons about every six months. At least two solar eclipses happen somewhere on Earth every year.

Remember: solar eclipse means Sun, Moon, Earth in a line (new moon). Lunar eclipse means Sun, Earth, Moon in a line (full moon).

Investigation 1 · Phase predictor

What will the Moon look like?

SP1.S3SP1.K5

Each main phase is about one quarter of the cycle after the one before.

time between main phases ≈ 29.5 days ÷ 4 ≈ 7.4 days
Worked example Tonight is a first quarter moon. What will it look like in about 15 days? 15 days ≈ 2 × 7.4 days, so move two main phases forward. first quarter → full moon → last quarter.

Investigation 2 · Tide table

Read a day of tides

SP1.S1SP1.K2

Tide tables list the water level at a harbour through the day. The tidal range is the difference between high tide and the low tide next to it.

tidal range = high tide height − low tide height
Worked example High tide is 10.4 m. The next low tide is 1.8 m. tidal range = 10.4 m − 1.8 m = 8.6 m The next high tide comes about 12 h 25 min after this one, because Earth spins under the two bulges as the Moon moves along its orbit.

Investigation 4 · Total or annular?

A lucky size match

SP1.S2SP1.K3

The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon, but it is also about 400 times farther away. So the two look almost the same size in our sky. How big something looks depends on its width divided by its distance.

apparent size ≈ diameter ÷ distance
Worked example Sun: 1 392 700 km ÷ 149 600 000 km ≈ 0.00931 Moon at 380 000 km: 3 474 km ÷ 380 000 km ≈ 0.00914 0.00914 is smaller than 0.00931, so the Moon looks a bit smaller than the Sun. A thin ring of Sun shows around it: an annular eclipse.

The Moon’s orbit is an oval, so its distance changes from about 356 500 km to about 406 700 km. You do not need to memorize these numbers.
