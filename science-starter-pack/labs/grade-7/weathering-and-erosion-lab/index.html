Weathering and Erosion Lab

Understanding: Earth’s surface is constantly changing due to the dynamic interactions of various geological processes. In this lab you will break rock with frost and roots, follow a grain of sand from a Rocky Mountain peak to a delta, grow a hoodoo, test slopes for landslides and look at real mass‑wasting events in Alberta.

Learn

Ten short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Lessons 1 and 2 describe Earth and its crust. Lessons 3 to 7 follow rock as it breaks, moves and wears away. Lessons 8 and 9 are about landslides and rockfalls. Lesson 10 shows where sediment ends up.

Lesson 1 · A dynamic planet

Earth’s surface never stops changing

ES1.K1ES1.S2

Earth is a dynamic planet. Dynamic means always changing. Mountains rise, rivers cut valleys and slopes slide downhill. Most of these changes are too slow to notice in one lifetime, but some happen in seconds.

A geological process is a natural process that forms or changes Earth’s rock and land. Scientists sort them into two groups.

Internal processes happen inside Earth and are powered by Earth’s inner heat. Examples are volcanoes, earthquakes and mountain building. They mostly build up land.

External processes happen at the surface and are powered by the Sun’s energy, which moves water, wind and ice, along with the pull of gravity. Examples are weathering, erosion and landslides. They mostly wear down land.

Try it: tap each numbered spot on the landscape. Decide if it is an internal or an external process before you read the answer.

Lesson 2 · Rocks and minerals

What the crust is made of

ES1.K4

The crust is Earth’s thin, rocky outer layer. Under the soil, water and ice, the crust is made of rocks and minerals.

A mineral is a natural, non‑living solid with its own chemical makeup and its particles arranged in a regular pattern called a crystal. Quartz, feldspar and calcite are minerals.

A rock is a natural solid made of one or more minerals, or sometimes of other natural materials such as the remains of ancient plants. Granite is a mix of several minerals. Limestone is made mostly of one mineral, calcite.

Weathering acts on the minerals in a rock. Some minerals are hard and resist change. Others are softer or dissolve in weak acids, so rocks made of them wear away faster.

Try it: look at each rock through the hand lens. Tap a mineral grain to learn about it.

You will learn how rocks form and how to identify minerals in the next lessons of this unit (the rock cycle).

Lesson 3 · Weathering

Breaking rock where it sits

ES1.K5ES1.S1

Weathering breaks rocks and minerals into smaller pieces called sediments, such as boulders, gravel, sand, silt and clay. Weathering happens in place. The pieces do not travel yet.

Physical weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing what it is made of. In frost wedging, water seeps into a crack, freezes and expands by about 9%. The ice pushes the crack wider. Then it melts, more water seeps in, and the cycle repeats.

Chemical weathering changes the minerals into new substances. Rainwater mixed with carbon dioxide forms a weak acid that slowly dissolves limestone. Oxygen and water turn iron minerals into rust.

Biological weathering is caused by living things. Roots grow into cracks and pry them open. Lichens and mosses release acids. Burrowing animals expose rock to air and water.

Try it: run freeze‑thaw cycles on the rock. Edmonton has many days in fall and spring when the temperature goes above and below 0 °C. Then tap the examples below the model.

Lesson 4 · Four linked processes

The journey of one grain of sand

ES1.K3ES1.S2ES1.S3

Four external processes work together like a relay to change Earth’s surface.

Weathering breaks rock into sediment. Erosion removes the sediment from where it formed. Transport carries it away in water, ice or wind, or by gravity alone. Deposition drops it in a new place.

Each process depends on the one before it. Without weathering, there is little loose sediment to erode. Without deposition, sediment would never pile up to build new land such as deltas and beaches.

Over millions of years, these processes wear high mountains down and fill low places up.

Try it: step a single grain of quartz from a peak in the Rocky Mountains to a lake far downstream.

Lesson 5 · Water at work

Waves, ice and groundwater

ES1.K2ES1.S1

Hydrological processes are the ways water moves and changes on and under Earth’s surface. Water is the most powerful agent of change at the surface, in all three of its states.

Wave action pounds shorelines. Waves throw sand and rock against a cliff and wear a notch at its base until the rock above falls.

Glacial processes are the work of glaciers, which are huge masses of ice that slowly flow downhill. Glaciers pluck and scrape rock, carve U‑shaped valleys and carry boulders far from home.

Groundwater flow is water moving slowly through cracks and spaces underground. Slightly acidic groundwater dissolves limestone and can form caves and sinkholes. Water underground also makes slopes heavier and weaker.

Running water in rivers and streams is another hydrological process. It moves more sediment than any other agent on land.

Try it: choose each water process and tap the labels to see an Alberta example.

Lesson 6 · Landforms of erosion

Hoodoos above ground, caves below

ES1.K6ES1.S1

Erosion removes and carries away weathered sediment and debris. Erosion does not wear land evenly. Hard rock resists while soft rock wears away, which leaves behind interesting features.

Hoodoos are tall columns of soft rock topped by a harder cap rock. Near Drumheller in the Alberta badlands, the cap rock is a harder sandstone sitting on softer sandstone and mudstone. Rain and runoff wash away the soft rock around it, but the cap protects the rock directly underneath. Once the cap falls, the hoodoo wears away quickly.

Caves form beneath the surface. Groundwater that is slightly acidic seeps through cracks in limestone and dissolves the rock, then carries the dissolved minerals away. Over many thousands of years, cracks grow into passages. Rat’s Nest Cave near Canmore formed this way.

Try it: grow a hoodoo, then remove its cap rock. Then switch to the cave.

Lesson 7 · What controls erosion

Why some land wears away faster

ES1.K7ES1.S3

The same rain can wash away a lot of soil on one hill and almost none on another. Erosion depends on many factors.

  • Weather and climate: heavy rain, strong wind and many freeze‑thaw cycles all speed up erosion.
  • Hydrological processes: more runoff, waves or flowing ice means more sediment is moved.
  • Topography, the shape of the land: water runs faster down steep slopes and carries more sediment.
  • Vegetation cover: leaves soften raindrops and roots hold soil in place.
  • Human activities: clearing land, construction and trails can expose bare soil. Planting and careful building can protect it.
Try it: change one factor at a time and watch the soil that collects at the bottom of the hill.

Lesson 8 · Mass wasting

When gravity takes over

ES1.K8ES1.K9

Mass wasting is a type of erosion in which rocks, soil or debris move downhill because of gravity. No river or glacier has to carry the material. Gravity pulls it down the slope.

Some mass wasting is sudden, like a rockfall or a landslide. Some is so slow that you only notice it from tilted fences and trees. That slow movement is called creep.

A slope stays put while the forces holding it (friction and the strength of the rock or soil) are bigger than the pull of gravity down the slope. Mass wasting starts when something triggers the slope to fail:

  • Water from heavy rain, snowmelt or rising groundwater adds weight and lowers friction.
  • Earthquakes shake loose material and break rock.
  • Human activity such as cutting into the base of a slope, mining, or adding buildings at the top.
Try it: choose each type of mass wasting to see how fast it moves and where it happens in Alberta.

Lesson 9 · Mass wasting in Alberta

Learning from real events

ES1.S4ES1.S5ES1.K9

Alberta’s mountains, foothills and steep river valleys all have slopes that can fail. Studying past events helps people plan safer communities.

The best known is the Frank Slide in the Crowsnest Pass. Early on the morning of April 29, 1903, a huge mass of limestone broke off Turtle Mountain and buried part of the coal mining town of Frank in about 100 seconds. At least 70 people died.

Scientists still watch Turtle Mountain today with instruments that measure tiny movements, so people can be warned if another large slide begins.

Be thoughtful: some of these events caused deaths and loss of homes. Some students may have family who lived through floods or slides.

Lesson 10 · Deposition

Where sediment comes to rest

ES1.K10ES1.S2

Deposition happens when wind, water or ice carrying sediment slows down or melts and drops it in a new place.

Faster water can carry bigger pieces. When a river slows, the biggest, heaviest pieces drop first. Fine silt and clay stay floating the longest. That is why sediment gets sorted by size.

Deposition builds landforms. A delta forms where a river enters a lake or ocean, slows down and drops its sediment in a fan shape. The Peace‑Athabasca Delta in northern Alberta is one of the largest freshwater deltas in the world. Dunes are hills of sand dropped by the wind. Melting glaciers leave ridges of mixed rock called moraines.

Try it: slow the river down and see which sediments drop out. Then tap each landform.

Lab Bench

Four investigations. Run a fair test on erosion, test slopes for failure and design fixes, calculate how fast land changes, and describe changes you can see around Edmonton.

Investigation 1 · Erosion test trays

Which factor moves the most soil?

ES1.S3ES1.K7

In a classroom stream table, students pour the same amount of water over trays of soil and weigh the soil that washes into a catch cup.

A fair test changes one variable on purpose (the independent variable) and measures the result (the dependent variable, here the soil lost). Everything else stays the same (the controlled variables).

Choose a factor to test. Run the three trays a few times, look at the averages and then answer the question.

The results are simulated practice data with random variation, like a real lab.

Investigation 2 · Slope stability tester

Will this slope fail?

ES1.K9ES1.S5

Engineers compare the forces that hold a slope in place with the force pulling it downhill. The result is called the factor of safety.

factor of safety = resisting force ÷ driving force
FS = Fresist ÷ Fdrive

If FS is greater than 1, the slope holds. If FS falls below 1, the slope fails. Engineers usually want FS of at least 1.3 for safety, since nobody knows every detail inside a hill.

Add triggers to make the slope weaker. Then add solutions to make it safer.

You do not need to memorize this formula. The tester uses a simplified, made‑up model.

Investigation 3 · Rate of change

How fast is the land changing?

ES1.S2ES1.K3

Geologists measure how far a riverbank, cliff or glacier has moved over a number of years. Then they find the rate, which is the change for each year.

rate = change ÷ time    r = Δd ÷ t
time = change ÷ rate    t = Δd ÷ r

Rates let us compare processes and predict the future. A riverbank losing 0.5 m each year will lose about 5 m in 10 years, if nothing changes.

Practice values are made up but realistic in size. Rates in nature change from year to year.
