Structures and Loads Lab

Understanding: Structures can be designed and constructed to withstand different types of forces. Bridges, towers, eggshells and beaver dams all hold up loads. This lab shows the parts and kinds of structures, the forces that push and pull on them, why some fail, and how designers build them to last.

Learn

Eight short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with the parts and kinds of structures, then look at loads and the forces inside a structure, why structures fail, and how designers plan them.

Lesson 1 · Parts of a structure
F1.K1F1.S2

Beams, columns and arches

A structure is anything that has a shape and holds up a load, from a bridge to a bird’s nest. Most structures are built from parts, called components, that work together.

A beam is a part that lies across a gap and carries a load. A floor joist and a bridge deck are beams.

A column is an upright part that holds weight from above and carries it down to the ground. Pillars and fence posts are columns.

An arch is a curved part. It carries a load around its curve and pushes out and down at its ends. Solid supports at the ends, called abutments, keep the arch from spreading.

The components interact. In the bridge model, the deck beam rests on columns, and the columns pass the load to the arch.

Remember: beams span gaps, columns hold weight from above, and arches carry loads around a curve to their ends.

Lesson 2 · Kinds of structures
F1.K8F1.K9F1.S2

Mass, frame and shell

Natural and human-made structures can be sorted into three categories.

A mass structure, also called a solid structure, is made by piling up or shaping material. Its own weight holds it in place. A dam and a beaver dam are mass structures.

A frame structure is a skeleton of parts joined together. The frame carries the load. Your skeleton and the wall framing of a house are frames.

A shell structure is a thin, curved outer layer that holds its shape and encloses a space. It spreads a load over its whole surface. An egg and a bike helmet are shells.

Many structures combine categories to withstand a load better. A house has a mass concrete foundation, a wooden frame and an outer shell of sheathing.

Remember: mass structures use their weight, frames use a skeleton, and shells use a thin curved surface.

Lesson 3 · Learning from nature
F1.K2F1.S4

Natural structures inspire designs

Living things have had a very long time to develop strong, light shapes. Designers study these natural structures and copy the ideas in human-made structures. This is sometimes called biomimicry.

Natural and human-made structures often use the same shapes, such as hexagons, tubes, domes and triangles. They also differ. Natural structures grow from living material and can often repair themselves. Human-made structures are built from chosen materials and need people to repair them.

Lesson 4 · Loads
F1.K3F1.S3

Permanent and non-permanent loads

A load is a force that acts on a structure. Forces are measured in newtons (N).

A permanent load is always there. The biggest one is the weight of the structure itself. Engineers call this the dead load.

A non-permanent load comes and goes or changes size. Wind, the weight of snow, the weight of people and furniture, and the ground shifting under a building are all non-permanent loads. Engineers call many of these live loads.

In Alberta, snow on a roof can be a large load. Wet spring snow weighs much more than fluffy fresh snow.

mass of snow: m = density × area × depth
weight (a force): W = m × 9.8 N/kg
Remember: permanent loads stay the same. Non-permanent loads change.

    Lesson 5 · Forces inside
    F1.K4

    Internal forces and deformation

    External forces act on a structure from outside. Loads are external forces.

    Internal forces act inside the materials of a structure as its parts push and pull on each other. There are four main kinds. Compression squeezes or pushes together. Tension stretches or pulls apart. Shear pushes parts to slide past each other. Torsion twists.

    When a beam bends, its top is squeezed in compression and its bottom is stretched in tension. Bending is compression and tension at the same time.

    Deformation is a change in a structure’s shape. A small deformation springs back. A large one can bend a part for good or break it, which damages the structure’s integrity, its ability to hold together and do its job.

    Remember: loads from outside cause pushes, pulls, slides and twists inside, and these can change a structure’s shape.

    Lesson 6 · Fatigue
    F1.K5

    Small loads, repeated many times

    Structural fatigue is damage that builds up a little at a time when a load is put on and taken off again and again. This is called repeated or cyclic loading.

    Each cycle can make a tiny crack grow a bit longer. The load may be much smaller than what would break the part in one go. Over time, the crack can grow until the part fails.

    Bridges with daily traffic, airplane wings that flex on every flight and playground swings are all loaded in cycles. Engineers inspect them for small cracks before they grow.

    Remember: fatigue is progressive damage from repeated loading that can lead to failure over time.

    Lesson 7 · Why structures fail
    F1.K6F1.S5

    Four causes of failure

    When a structure deforms too much or breaks, we say it has failed. Engineers study failures so they do not happen again.

    Flawed design means the plan itself was wrong. Poor construction means the plan was fine but it was built badly. Improper load means the structure carried more load, or a different load, than it was designed for. Structural fatigue means repeated loading slowly damaged it.

    A failure can have more than one cause. Investigators look at the evidence, such as where it broke, how it was built and what loads acted on it.

    Remember: design, construction, load and fatigue are the four causes to check.

    Lesson 8 · Designing a structure
    F1.K7F1.S1F1.S6

    What shapes a design

    Before building, designers think about four factors.

    Function: what job must the structure do? Available resources: what materials, money, tools, time and skilled workers are there? Safety: how will it protect people? Designers make structures stronger than the biggest load they expect. This extra strength is called a safety factor. Intended lifespan and sustainability: how long must it last, and how can it use materials and energy wisely?

    Two structures can have the same function but different designs. We compare how well they work, for example by how much load they hold for their mass.

    Remember: function, resources, safety, and lifespan and sustainability all shape a design.

    Investigation 1 · Craft stick bridge test
    F1.S1F1.S6

    Build a bridge that supports a load

    Your class is building bridges from craft sticks to span a 40 cm gap. Choose a design and the number of sticks. Then add load in the middle until the bridge fails.

    Every bridge has the same function, so you can compare designs fairly. Change only one thing at a time.

    To compare designs, use efficiency: how many times its own mass the bridge held.

    Investigation 2 · Strength for its mass
    F1.S6

    Which design is more effective?

    The strongest bridge is not always the best design. A bridge that uses less material for the same job saves resources.

    Worked example Bridge A has a mass of 60 g and holds 4.2 kg (4200 g). Efficiency A = 4200 g ÷ 60 g = 70 Bridge B has a mass of 90 g and holds 5.4 kg (5400 g). Efficiency B = 5400 g ÷ 90 g = 60 Bridge B is stronger, but Bridge A is more efficient. It holds 70 times its own mass.

    Remember to change kilograms to grams first: 1 kg = 1000 g.

    Teacher notes

    • F1.K1: beams, columns and arches are taught as the named components, with abutments named as the supports an arch needs. Trusses are mentioned as beams joined in triangles. Foundations appear only as part of the house example.
    • F1.K3: loads are taught as forces in newtons. Permanent loads are linked to the engineering term dead load, and non-permanent loads to live loads (people, furniture) and environmental loads (wind, snow, ground shifting). Snow densities in the estimator are rounded typical values (about 100, 300 and 450 kg/m³), and W = m × 9.8 N/kg is shown as context only.
    • F1.K4: the draft names internal and external forces without defining them. The lab defines external forces as loads acting from outside and internal forces as compression, tension, shear and torsion inside materials, with bending taught as compression and tension together. Elastic (springs back) and permanent deformation are distinguished. The beam model is qualitative.
    • F1.K5: fatigue is taught as cracks that grow a little with each load cycle, even when each load is well below the one that would break the part at once. Paper clip cycle counts are made up and randomized.
    • F1.K6 and S5: two real cases are used. Quebec Bridge (1907): the weight of the bridge was underestimated and not rechecked, and the partly built span collapsed, killing many workers. Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940, Washington State): a thin, flexible deck twisted in moderate wind until it broke; no people died. Please verify these summaries. The Iron Ring tradition is mentioned only as a reminder of responsibility; the myth that rings were made from the bridge’s steel is not repeated. Other case files are invented and labelled as practice. Deaths in real failures may be sensitive for some students.
    • F1.K8 and K9: mass is taught as the same category as solid. The tipi is described generally as a frame of poles with a cover (frame plus shell), and the Inuit snow house (iglu) as a dome-shaped shell of snow blocks. No specific community knowledge is described; please review this wording and consider a community partner.
    • F1.K2 and S4: nature-inspired pairs (honeycomb and honeycomb panels, hollow bird bones and tube frames, eggshell and domes, tree trunks and towers, spider silk and cables, beaver dams and earth dams) are described as shared ideas. The lab does not claim that any one building was copied from a specific organism.
    • F1.S1 and S6: the craft stick bridge test is a made-up model with random variation of about 10 percent. Efficiency (load held ÷ mass) is added as a way to compare designs with a common function.
    • Alberta examples: the High Level Bridge in Edmonton (a steel truss frame), grain bins, prairie snow loads and frost heave are everyday contexts only.
