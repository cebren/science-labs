Lesson 1

Observation: gather information

SL1.K2

An observation is information you gather with your senses (sight, hearing, touch, smell) or with tools such as a thermometer, ruler, balance or hand lens.

Tools make observations more exact and let you notice things your senses miss. A thermometer gives a number in degrees Celsius. Your hand only tells you "cool" or "warm".

A good observation describes only what is there. It does not explain why.

Safety: in a science lab you never taste samples, and you smell by gently wafting air toward your nose.

Careful, long-term observation is also part of Indigenous knowledge systems. Knowledge keepers on Treaty 6 territory have watched the land for generations, noting things like when lakes freeze, when berries ripen and when animals move.

Lesson 2

Inference: explain what you observe

SL1.K3

An inference is an explanation or conclusion based on observations plus your prior knowledge (what you already know).

observation + prior knowledge → inference

Two people can make the same observations and different inferences, because they know different things. An inference can also change when new observations arrive. That is why scientists test inferences.

  • Observation The sidewalk is wet.
  • Inference It rained last night.

Lesson 3

Qualitative and quantitative data

SL1.K4

Qualitative data describes qualities with words: colour, texture, smell, shape, behaviour. Think quality.

Quantitative data uses numbers from counting or measuring, usually with units. Think quantity.

Both kinds can support a scientific explanation. Quantitative data is easier to compare and graph. Qualitative data can capture things numbers miss, like an animal's behaviour or a strange smell.

Lesson 4

Critical thinking with data

SL1.K5

Interpreting data means thinking critically about what it shows. Scientists use five thinking skills: categorizing and recognizing patterns, predicting outcomes, analyzing relationships such as cause and effect, supporting or contradicting hypotheses, and justifying conclusions.

A class timed how long a sugar cube took to dissolve in 200 mL of water at different temperatures. Choose a thinking skill to see it applied.

Lesson 5

Scientific explanations

SL1.K1SL1.K4

A scientific explanation answers a question about the natural world. It is built on observations and on interpretations of data, not on opinion or guesses.

A strong explanation has three parts:

  • Claim: your answer to the question.
  • Evidence: observations and data that support the claim.
  • Reasoning: the science idea that connects the evidence to the claim.

Scientific explanations can change when new evidence appears.

Build up an explanation

Question: Why did the puddles on the school field disappear faster on Tuesday than on Monday?

Lesson 6

Bias in data

SL1.K6

Bias is anything that pushes results in one direction so they no longer give a fair picture. Bias can sneak in when data is collected and when it is interpreted.

  • Sampling bias: the sample does not represent the whole group.
  • Measurement bias: a tool or method gives readings that are always off in the same direction.
  • Confirmation bias: noticing only the results you expected and ignoring the rest.
  • Interest bias: someone who gains from a result interprets data in their own favour.

Scientists reduce bias with large, random samples, checked tools, repeated trials, and by having other people review their work.

Station 2

Data interpreter: bean seedlings

SL1.S2

A Grade 7 class grew bean seedlings for 14 days. Five plants got plant food and five got plain water. The class recorded heights in millimetres and wrote notes about each plant.

Worked example: the mean

mean = (sum of values) ÷ (number of values)

Example: heights 40, 44, 46, 50, 45 mm. Sum = 225 mm. 225 ÷ 5 = 45 mm.

Station 4

Bias simulator: walleye in Pigeon Lake

SL1.S5

Biologists want the average length of walleye in a lake. They cannot measure every fish, so they catch a sample. How they catch the fish can bias the answer. This lake is a model with 400 virtual fish.

Bars show how many fish of each length were in your sample. The dashed line is your sample average.
