Learning Machines Lab

Understanding: Machines can learn to perform tasks. In this lab you will watch programs change their behaviour from data, train a bird call sorter, test how the amount and quality of data matter, and compare the narrow AI we use every day with the idea of general AI.

Learn

Six short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. All the data in these models is made up for practice.

Lesson 1 · What AI is
CS2.K1

AI changes what it does because of data

Artificial intelligence, or AI, is the ability of a machine to adapt its actions. It adapts in response to three things:

  • received data: information it takes in, like sounds, photos or numbers
  • prior interactions: what happened the last times it was used
  • its environment: the conditions around it right now

To adapt means to change to fit new conditions. An ordinary program does the same thing every time unless a person rewrites it. An AI system can change its own behaviour from experience.

Try the model. Both heaters warm a house in Edmonton before the family wakes up. One follows a fixed rule. One learns from the wake-up times it has seen.

Big idea: AI is not about looking smart. It is about a machine changing what it does based on data and experience.

Pick today’s wake-up time, then press Next day. The fixed heater always starts at 6:30. The learning heater starts 30 minutes before the average of the last 5 wake-ups.

Lesson 2 · AI vs other programs
CS2.K1CS2.S1CS2.S2

Rules written by people, or patterns found in data

In an ordinary program, a programmer writes every rule. The same input always gives the same output. A calculator is a good example.

In many AI programs, people do not write the rules for the task. They give the program examples. The program finds its own patterns in those examples and uses them to decide what to do.

The model shows two spam filters. Spam is unwanted junk messages. The rule filter has one rule a person wrote. The learning filter gives each word a score from messages you label.

To decide if a technology uses AI, ask three questions:

  1. Was it trained on data or examples?
  2. Does it find patterns to make predictions or decisions?
  3. Does its behaviour change with new data or experience?

If the answer to all three is no, it is an ordinary program, even if it seems clever. Practise this on the Lab Bench.

Lesson 3 · Machine learning
CS2.K2CS2.S3

Machine learning is trained from data

Machine learning is a type of AI that is trained from data. Training means showing the program many examples so it can find patterns.

Here a sorter learns to tell two Edmonton birds apart by their calls: the black-capped chickadee and the red-breasted nuthatch. Each dot is one recorded call with a known answer, called a label.

The sorter uses a simple method. To label a mystery call, it finds the 3 training calls closest to it and lets them vote. The shaded areas show what it would predict anywhere on the plot.

  • It identifies patterns: chickadee calls cluster in one area.
  • It makes predictions: it labels calls it has never heard.

The pitch and length scores are made up for practice. Real bird sound apps use far more features and far more examples.

Model: bird call sorter

Move the mystery call with the sliders, or focus the plot and use the arrow keys. To add training data, pick a label and click the plot or press Add at mystery.

Lesson 4 · Learning over time
CS2.K2CS2.S3

More data, better data, new data

Machine learning can improve its performance over time. Usually this happens because it gets more examples to learn from. Nobody writes new rules. The program updates itself from the data.

We measure performance with accuracy: the percent of test examples it gets right.

accuracy = correct ÷ total × 100%

Data matters in three ways:

  • Amount: a few examples give a rough guess. More examples usually help, up to a point.
  • Quality: wrong labels teach wrong patterns.
  • Fit: the training data must match the real world. If the world changes, the program must adapt based on new data.

When training data leaves out some kinds of examples, the program can be unfair or wrong for those cases. This is called bias in data.

Lesson 5 · Narrow AI
CS2.K3CS2.K1

Narrow AI does one kind of job

Narrow AI is designed to perform a specific task. Almost all the AI you meet is narrow AI:

  • voice recognition turns speech into words or commands
  • recommendation algorithms suggest songs, videos or books from what you liked before
  • spam filters, face unlock, map apps that predict traffic

An algorithm is a set of steps for solving a problem. A recommender learns from your prior interactions, like the things you liked or skipped.

Narrow AI can be very good at its job. A team at the University of Alberta in Edmonton built Chinook, a checkers program that could not be beaten. But Chinook could not play chess or recognize a voice. Edmonton is also home to Amii, the Alberta Machine Intelligence Institute, where people research machine learning.

Model: library book recommender

Press Like or Skip on any book. The recommender adds +1 or −1 to each of the book’s tags, then suggests the unrated books with the highest total.

Lesson 6 · General AI
CS2.K4CS2.S4

General AI would work across many tasks

General AI is AI designed to show human-like intelligence, reasoning and adaptability across a wide range of tasks. It would learn a new kind of task the way a person can, and use what it knows in new situations.

General AI is a goal that researchers talk about. Today, no system is widely accepted as general AI. Experts disagree about whether or when it will be built. Some chatbots can help with many kinds of writing and questions, but they still make mistakes a thinking person would not, and they are not widely accepted as general AI.

Narrow AI

  • One specific task
  • Exists and is used every day
  • Trained for its job only

General AI

  • A wide range of tasks
  • A goal, not widely accepted as built
  • Would reason and adapt like a person

Teacher notes on wording

  • CS2.K1: The draft definition is broad. A simple thermostat also responds to data from its environment. The lab teaches AI as a machine that changes its own behaviour from data and experience, beyond fixed rules that a person wrote. Definitions of AI vary; many also mention tasks that usually need human intelligence.
  • CS2.K2: “Without additional programming” is taught as without a programmer writing new rules for the task. People still collect data, retrain and update models. Many apps use a model that stays the same until its makers retrain it, so improvement is not always automatic.
  • CS2.K3: Voice recognition and recommendation algorithms are taught as narrow AI. Chinook (University of Alberta checkers program) and Amii (Alberta Machine Intelligence Institute, Edmonton) are named as local context; please verify wording.
  • CS2.K4: General AI is taught as a goal that no system is widely accepted as reaching. Experts disagree about whether or when it will be built. Chatbots that handle many writing tasks are mentioned as harder to classify. This is a fast-changing area, so please re-check before teaching.
  • CS2.S3: Bias in data, wrong labels and data that no longer fits the world are added to explain the role of data. The k-nearest-neighbour sorter (k = 3), bird call scores, spam messages, books and accuracy results are all made up for practice.
