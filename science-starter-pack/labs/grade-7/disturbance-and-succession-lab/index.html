Science 7 · Life Science: Ecosystems · LS3

Disturbance and Succession Lab

Understanding: Ecosystems can maintain stability despite disturbances. A fire, flood or bulldozer can knock an ecosystem back. Living things then return in a pattern, and people can help or harm that recovery.

Learn

Seven short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with what a disturbance is, watch an ecosystem rebuild itself, then look at why biodiversity is falling and how people can care for ecosystems.

Lesson 1 · Knocked down, then back up
LS3.K2Understanding

What is a disturbance?

A disturbance is an event that suddenly changes an ecosystem. It kills or removes living things, or changes their habitat. Disturbances can be small, like one tree blowing down, or huge, like a wildfire that burns thousands of hectares.

Disturbances come from three main sources:

  • Geological processes: landslides, rockfalls, earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and rivers cutting new channels.
  • Extreme weather events: floods, droughts, tornadoes, hailstorms, ice storms, and wildfires started by lightning.
  • Human activity: clearing land for farms, roads and homes, mining, draining wetlands, pollution, and bringing in species from elsewhere.

An ecosystem is stable when it can keep its structure, biodiversity and processes over time. Stability does not mean nothing ever changes. It means the ecosystem can take a hit and recover. Ecosystems with many species usually recover better, because if one species is lost, others can fill its role.

Remember: a stable ecosystem bends and bounces back. Most natural disturbances are part of how ecosystems work. Trouble comes when disturbances are too big, too frequent, or permanent.

Lesson 2 · Rebuilding in a pattern
LS3.K1

Ecological succession

Ecological succession is the pattern of change in the species and habitat of an area over time. After a disturbance, the first living things to arrive change the place. Those changes make room for new species, which change the place again.

Primary succession starts where there is no soil at all: bare rock left by a retreating glacier, a fresh lava flow, or a new landslide surface. The first arrivals are pioneer species such as lichens and mosses. They help break down rock, and when they die they add organic matter. Slowly, soil forms. This can take hundreds of years.

Secondary succession starts where a disturbance removed most living things but left the soil behind, along with roots and seeds. Examples are a burned forest, a flooded field, or a farm field that is no longer used. It is much faster than primary succession.

At the Columbia Icefield in Jasper National Park, the Athabasca Glacier has pulled back more than a kilometre since the late 1800s. Walking up the trail, you pass from forest to shrubs to mosses to bare rock: you are walking backward through primary succession.

Succession is predictable in its general pattern: small, fast-growing plants first, then shrubs, then trees. The exact species and timing vary from place to place, and another disturbance can restart the clock.

Lesson 3 · Bending versus breaking
LS3.K2LS3.K1

How stability survives a disturbance

Picture an ecosystem's health as a line on a graph. A disturbance makes the line drop. Then succession pulls it back up. Two things decide what the graph looks like:

  • How big the disturbance is. A windstorm that drops a few trees is a small dip. A severe fire is a deep one.
  • How much biodiversity the ecosystem has. More species means more backups. Some species survive the event, some seeds wait in the soil, and some animals move back in from nearby. Recovery is faster.

Some disturbances are so large or so lasting that the ecosystem cannot return to what it was. A wetland drained and paved for a parking lot is not going to rebuild itself. In that case, the area becomes a different system.

Lesson 4 · The global picture
LS3.K3

Why biodiversity is declining

Around the world, biodiversity is falling, and the loss is speeding up. The Living Planet Index tracks thousands of wildlife populations. Since 1970, the monitored populations have dropped by about 73 percent on average. A 2019 United Nations science report estimated that about one million plant and animal species are at risk of extinction.

Four main causes are behind the decline:

  • Habitat destruction: forests, wetlands and grasslands are cleared, drained or broken into small pieces. Much of Alberta's native prairie has been plowed or built on.
  • Pollution: fertilizer runoff feeds algae blooms in lakes, and plastics, pesticides and oil harm wildlife.
  • Invasive species: species brought from somewhere else outcompete or prey on local species. Prussian carp now spread through Alberta rivers. Alberta inspects boats to keep zebra and quagga mussels out.
  • Climate change: warming shifts where species can live. Warmer winters helped mountain pine beetle spread east into Alberta's pine forests.

Scientists also name overexploitation, such as overfishing and overhunting, as a major cause worldwide.

Lesson 5 · Losing species
LS3.K4

Endangered, extirpated, extinct

Biodiversity drops when species slide toward disappearing. Scientists use clear words for how much trouble a species is in:

  • Endangered: the species is likely to disappear soon if nothing changes.
  • Extirpated: the species no longer lives in the wild in one area, such as Canada or Alberta, but still lives somewhere else.
  • Extinct: the species no longer exists anywhere on Earth.

In Canada, a committee of scientists called COSEWIC assesses species. It also uses threatened (likely to become endangered) and special concern (sensitive and worth watching).

Extinction is permanent. Extirpation can sometimes be reversed by bringing a species back from where it still lives. Even before a species disappears, a shrinking population loses genetic diversity, so it has fewer ways to adapt to change.

Lesson 6 · Everyone's job
LS3.K5

Caring for ecosystems

Caring for ecosystems is a shared responsibility. Governments, scientists, landowners, First Nations and Métis communities, farmers, companies, schools and volunteers each hold a piece. Good care usually has three parts:

  • Collaboration: groups plan and work together, because animals and water cross property lines.
  • Monitoring data: people count species and test water year after year, so they can tell whether things are getting better or worse.
  • Interventions: actions that help, such as species relocation (moving animals to restore a population), habitat conservation (protecting land from being cleared), and protecting water resources (keeping rivers, lakes and wetlands clean and full).

In 2017, Parks Canada moved 16 plains bison from Elk Island National Park, east of Edmonton, to Banff National Park. Many First Nations have long worked to bring bison back to the land, and several Indigenous Guardian programs in Alberta now help monitor wildlife and water.

Lesson 7 · Working forests
LS3.K6

Forest management

More than half of Alberta is covered by forest, much of it boreal forest. Fire is a natural part of this ecosystem. Lodgepole pine and jack pine even have cones that are sealed shut with resin and open in the heat of a fire, dropping seeds onto fresh ash.

Forest managers try to get wood, protect communities and keep the forest healthy at the same time. They use practices such as:

  • Selective harvesting: cutting only some trees and leaving the rest standing.
  • Reduced impact logging: careful planning of roads and cutting to protect soil, streams and young trees.
  • Reforestation: planting or seeding trees after harvest. In Alberta, companies that harvest on public land must regrow the forest.
  • Controlled burning: trained crews set small, planned fires in safe weather to clear dead fuel and renew habitat.
  • Wildfire risk reduction: thinning trees, clearing deadfall and making fuel breaks near towns. FireSmart is Alberta's program for this.

    Indigenous ways of knowing, doing and being

    Bringing back bison, caribou and fire

    This resource was made in Edmonton, in Treaty 6 territory. We acknowledge the nêhiyaw (Cree), Dene, Anishinaabe (Saulteaux), Nakota Isga (Nakota Sioux) and Niitsitapi (Blackfoot) peoples, the Métis, whose homeland this is, and the Inuit, one of whose largest communities south of the 60th parallel lives in Edmonton. We also honour all First Nations, Métis and Inuit peoples whose knowledge shapes these lands.

    This lab asks how ecosystems recover after a disturbance, and who helps them recover. In the Edmonton area, across Alberta and in northeastern British Columbia, First Nations and Métis communities are leading work to bring back bison, caribou and fire, using their own knowledge alongside science. These connections name the nations doing that work and point to them as the people who hold the knowledge.

    Edmonton · nêhiyaw (Cree), Métis

    Elk Island bison return to Indigenous communities

    Elk Island National Park, east of Edmonton, sits on the Beaver Hills plateau, known as Amiskwaciy (Beaver Hills) to Cree peoples. The park's bison herds grow quickly, so animals are moved out to keep the herds healthy. Parks Canada reports that over five years it transferred 212 bison to their traditional homelands in nine Indigenous communities. In 2022, Métis Crossing near Smoky Lake received 20 wood bison, and Beaver Lake Cree Nation received four. Seven calves were born at Métis Crossing in 2023. Its CEO, Juanita Marois, says they are "trying to re-establish the natural environment that would have been there."

    Source: Parks Canada: Elk Island National Park Management Plan, 2023 · APTN News: Bison from Elk Island National Park transferred to First Nations and Métis communities · CBC News: Birth of bison calves at Métis park welcomed as a connection to culture

    Alberta · Niitsitapi (Blackfoot): Siksika, Kainai, Piikani, Tsuut'ina, Stoney Nakoda, Métis

    Banff's bison: knowledge and science together

    The 16 plains bison moved to Banff National Park in 2017 grew to over 130 animals by 2024. Parks Canada says much of the project's success comes from weaving Indigenous ceremonies and cultural knowledge with western science. Its Indigenous Advisory Circle includes the Siksika, Kainai, Piikani and Tsuut'ina Nations, the Stoney Nakoda Nations (Bearspaw, Chiniki and Goodstoney) and the Otipemisiwak Métis Government. The Stoney Nakoda Nations wrote their own cultural monitoring report and recommended that cultural monitoring fieldwork continue. In 2014, Kainai, Siksika, Piikani and Tsuut'ina were among the first eight nations to sign the Buffalo Treaty, which recognizes buffalo as "an important part of the ecological system."

    Source: Parks Canada: Plains bison reintroduction, Banff National Park · Parks Canada: The Indigenous Advisory Circle for Banff National Park (news release, January 2025) · CTV News Calgary: Stoney Nakoda Nation says traditional knowledge key to success for Banff bison · International Buffalo Relations Institute: The Buffalo Treaty

    Alberta · nêhiyaw (Cree): Beaver Lake Cree Nation, Métis: East Prairie Métis Settlement, Niitsitapi (Blackfoot): Kainai Nation

    Communities returning fire to the land

    Some Indigenous communities in Alberta are leading the return of planned fire. Beaver Lake Cree Nation, about 215 km northeast of Edmonton, and East Prairie Métis Settlement are working with Alberta Wildfire to burn stretches of land at risk of catching fire. Shane Bair, fire chief at Beaver Lake Cree Nation, trained in traditional burning through the We Are Fire program. He says burns "rejuvenate the land, helps get the medicines back." In Waterton Lakes National Park, members of Kainai Nation have worked side by side with Parks Canada fire crews to return fire to the land. How cultural burning is done is for these communities to teach.

    Source: CBC News: Indigenous communities turn to prescribed burns to protect land from wildfires · Parks Canada: Indigenous fire stewardship

    Canada · Saulteau First Nations, West Moberly First Nations

    Klinse-Za caribou: a herd brought back

    Near Chetwynd, in northeastern British Columbia, Saulteau First Nations and West Moberly First Nations lead the recovery of the Klinse-Za mountain caribou herd. Saulteau First Nations is a Treaty 8 Nation. In 2013, fewer than 40 caribou were left. The Nations use three tools together. Maternal penning protects pregnant females and their calves from predators. Predator numbers are reduced. Damaged habitat is restored. Saulteau First Nations reports the herd passed 140 animals in 2024. A 2020 partnership also helped create a much larger Klinse-za Park to protect caribou habitat. Naomi Owens-Beek of Saulteau First Nations says: "Indigenous conservation is real, it's happening."

    Source: Saulteau First Nations: Treaty Rights and Environmental Protection · Saulteau First Nations: Our Nation · CBC News: Indigenous-led recovery effort triples caribou herd · CBC News: Klinse-za Park expansion near Chetwynd

    Talk and think

    • Banff's bison herd grew from 16 in 2017 to over 130 in 2024. The Klinse-Za caribou herd grew from fewer than 40 in 2013 to over 140 in 2024. A head count is one kind of monitoring data. The Stoney Nakoda Nations also asked for cultural monitoring. What might people who know the land notice that a head count would miss?
    • Roads and other cleared lines through a forest are a human disturbance. Rachel Plotkin of the David Suzuki Foundation, speaking about Klinse-za Park, says these "linear disturbances increase the sightlines for predators." How do maternal penning, predator reduction and habitat restoration each deal with a different cause of the caribou decline? Which one helps the ecosystem most over the long term, and why?
    • Parks Canada says cultural burning enhances biodiversity and reduces wildfire risk by reducing vegetation. Use your results from the forest manager simulation to explain why planned fire can increase habitat variety. Why does it matter that the communities who hold this knowledge are the ones who teach how it is done?
