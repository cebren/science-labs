Solar System Patterns Lab

Understanding: Celestial bodies move in predictable patterns. In this lab you will see how gravity makes orbits, compare Earth‑centred and Sun‑centred models of the sky, measure the solar system in astronomical units and build a scale model you could walk.

Learn

Six short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Lesson 1 explains orbits. Lesson 2 is about models. Lessons 3 and 4 compare the Earth‑centred and Sun‑centred models. Lessons 5 and 6 measure distances and describe how the planets move.

Lesson 1 · Gravity and orbits

Falling around a planet

SP2.K1SP2.S3

Gravity is a force of attraction between objects that have mass. Every object pulls on every other object. The pull is stronger when the objects have more mass, and weaker when they are farther apart.

A celestial body is a natural object in space, such as a star, a planet or a moon. An orbit is the curved path one body follows around another.

Gravity alone would pull a planet straight into the Sun. A planet stays in orbit because it is also moving sideways very fast. Gravity keeps bending its path toward the Sun, so the planet keeps falling around the Sun without ever falling in.

Try it: this is Isaac Newton’s famous thought experiment. Launch a ball sideways from a very tall mountain. Change the speed and see if the ball falls back, orbits or escapes.

Gravity pulls both ways. The Moon pulls on Earth too, so Earth and the Moon both circle a shared balance point. That point is inside Earth, about 4700 km from its centre.

Lesson 2 · Models

Every model shows some things and hides others

SP2.K2SP2.S2

A model is a simpler version of something real that helps us explain or predict. Scientists use models for things that are too big, too small, too far or too slow to study directly. The solar system is all four.

Models of the solar system can show movement patterns, such as which way planets travel, or distances, such as how far each planet is from the Sun. A scale model shrinks every size or distance by the same amount.

No single picture can show both the sizes and the distances of the planets to scale. If the planets were big enough to see, the page would need to be hundreds of metres wide.

Try it: switch between the two scale views. Then tap each type of model to see what it does well and what it leaves out.

Lesson 3 · Two models, one sky

Earth at the centre, or the Sun?

SP2.K3SP2.S1

For about 2000 years, most astronomers used a geocentric model. Geo means Earth. In this model Earth sits still at the centre and the Sun, the Moon, the planets and the stars all move around it. It made sense: we do not feel Earth moving, and the sky seems to turn around us.

Planets caused a puzzle. Every so often Mars seems to stop, move backward against the stars for a few weeks, then go forward again. This is called retrograde motion. The astronomer Ptolemy, around the year 150, explained it by putting each planet on a small circle called an epicycle, whose centre moves around Earth on a big circle.

In a heliocentric model, helio means Sun. The Sun is at the centre and Earth is one of the planets moving around it. Earth, on its faster inside orbit, catches up to Mars and passes it. For a while Mars seems to slide backward, the way a slower car seems to move backward when you pass it on the highway.

Try it: move the time slider in each view. Both models make the same loop in the sky strip. To choose between them, scientists needed more evidence (Lesson 4).

Lesson 4 · Evidence

How the evidence changed minds

SP2.K3SP2.S1

The switch to the heliocentric model did not happen in one day. It took ideas and careful measurements from many people and places over hundreds of years.

A big turning point came around 1610. Galileo Galilei looked through an early telescope and saw moons orbiting Jupiter, which showed that not everything orbits Earth. He also saw that Venus shows a full set of phases, from a thin crescent to nearly full.

In Ptolemy’s model Venus always stays between Earth and the Sun, so we would only ever see crescents. In the heliocentric model Venus can go behind the Sun and look nearly full. Galileo saw what the heliocentric model predicted.

Later, Johannes Kepler showed that orbits are slightly stretched circles called ellipses, and Isaac Newton showed that gravity explains why planets orbit the Sun.

Lesson 5 · Measuring the solar system

The astronomical unit

SP2.K4SP2.S3

Distances in space are huge. Writing them in kilometres gives numbers that are hard to read and compare. So astronomers use a bigger unit.

One astronomical unit, or AU, is the average distance from Earth to the Sun.

1 AU ≈ 150 000 000 km

Earth is 1 AU from the Sun. Jupiter is about 5.2 AU away, so it is about 5 times as far from the Sun as Earth. Neptune is about 30 AU away. Even sunlight takes time to cross these distances: about 8.3 minutes to reach Earth and about 4 hours to reach Neptune.

Other stars have their own planets. These planetary systems are measured in AU too. The distances between stars are so large that astronomers use the light‑year, the distance light travels in a year, about 63 000 AU.

distance in km = distance in AU × 150 000 000 km

Lesson 6 · Movement patterns

Patterns we can predict

SP2.S3SP2.K1SP2.K2

In the heliocentric model, the eight planets move in clear patterns:

  • Same direction. Seen from above Earth’s North Pole, every planet orbits the Sun counterclockwise.
  • Nearly flat. The orbits lie close to one flat plane, like grooves on a record.
  • Nearly round. The orbits are ellipses that are very close to circles.
  • Farther means slower. The Sun’s pull is weaker far away, so outer planets move more slowly and have much longer paths. Their orbital period, or year, is longer.

Moons follow patterns too. The Moon orbits Earth about once a month while Earth carries it around the Sun. Because these patterns repeat, astronomers can predict where a planet will be years ahead.

From the Beaver Hills Dark Sky Preserve east of Edmonton, you can see five planets without a telescope: Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn.

Bench 1 · Scale model builder

Shrink the solar system to fit Edmonton

SP2.S2SP2.K2SP2.K4

Pick an object to stand in for the Sun. The builder shrinks every size and every distance by the same amount, so the model is to scale.

scale factor = model Sun size ÷ real Sun size
model distance = real distance × scale factor
Worked example A basketball is about 24 cm wide. The real Sun is about 1 391 000 km wide. Earth is 1 AU, about 149 600 000 km, from the Sun. model distance = 149 600 000 km × (24 cm ÷ 1 391 000 km) ≈ 2580 cm ≈ 26 m Earth itself would be about 2 mm wide, the size of a peppercorn.

Bench 3 · Orbit pattern finder

Does distance predict the length of a year?

SP2.S3SP2.K1

Plot each planet’s distance from the Sun against its orbital period or its speed. Look for the pattern, then use it to predict.

Kepler found a rule that fits every planet. You do not need to memorize it.

P² = a³
P = period in Earth years, a = distance in AU
Worked example The dwarf planet Ceres orbits about 2.77 AU from the Sun. a³ = 2.77 × 2.77 × 2.77 ≈ 21.3 P = √21.3 ≈ 4.6 years Ceres is farther out than Mars (1.9 years) and closer than Jupiter (11.9 years), so 4.6 years fits the pattern.
