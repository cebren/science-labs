Science 7 · Stewardship Capstone · SL2

Stewardship Action Lab

Understanding: Environmental stewardship can address present needs that consider the environment and human well-being. People meet today's needs while they protect, restore and wisely use the land, water and living things that everyone shares.

Learn

Six short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with what stewardship means, look at the actions people take, zoom in on the circular economy, then ask who benefits, why people act, and how caring for something is different from owning it.

Lesson 1 · A shared job
SL2.K1Understanding

What is environmental stewardship?

Environmental stewardship is the shared responsibility to look after the environment and its resources. A steward is someone who takes care of something for others, including people not yet born.

Stewardship has three parts:

  • Protect: keep healthy land, water and living things from being damaged.
  • Restore: help damaged places and populations recover.
  • Use sustainably: use resources such as water, wood and soil in a way that leaves enough for the future.

It is a shared responsibility because rivers, birds and air cross every border. Individuals and groups act at four levels: local (your home, school and city), provincial (Alberta), national (Canada) and international (many countries working together).

Remember: stewardship does not mean leaving nature untouched. People still need water, food and homes. Stewardship meets those needs today while caring for the environment and for people's well-being.

Lesson 2 · What stewards do
SL2.K2SL2.K1

Four kinds of stewardship action

People protect, restore and sustainably use the environment through many actions. Four big ones are:

  • Protected areas: parks and reserves where land and water are set aside to conserve biodiversity. They need ongoing care, such as monitoring wildlife and managing visitors.
  • Waste reduction and circular economy practices: making less waste in the first place, and keeping materials in use longer.
  • Responsible water management and conservation: sharing water fairly, keeping it clean, and not wasting it.
  • Reintroduction of native species: bringing a species back to a place where it used to live, after it disappeared there.

A native species is one that lived in a place naturally before people moved it. Plains bison and swift foxes are native to Alberta's grasslands.

Many actions do more than one job. A national park protects habitat, and it can also be a place where a native species is reintroduced.

Lesson 3 · Closing the loop
SL2.K3SL2.K2

The circular economy

In a linear economy, we take materials, make a product, use it, and throw it away. The materials end up in a landfill.

A circular economy is a way of designing and using products so that materials are reused, repaired or recycled instead of being thrown away. This reduces waste and protects the environment, because fewer new materials need to be dug up, grown or pumped out of the ground.

Clothing rental and resale programs are good examples. A party dress or a hockey jacket can be rented for a season. Outgrown clothes can be sold, swapped or handed down so another person wears them.

The inner loops save the most. Keeping a product in use, sharing it and repairing it keeps all its value. Recycling is the outer loop: it breaks the product down into material, which takes energy, and the material is often lower quality. So reduce, reuse and repair come first, and recycle comes last.

Design matters: a circular economy starts before a product is made. Products designed to last, to be fixed and to come apart easily are easier to keep in the loop.

Lesson 4 · Who benefits
SL2.K4

Benefits for ecosystems and for people

Stewardship has two big benefits.

Enhanced ecosystem stability. Recall that a stable ecosystem can keep its structure, biodiversity and processes over time, and recover after disturbances. Protecting habitat, keeping water clean and bringing back native species all add biodiversity, which helps ecosystems stay stable.

Improved human well-being. Well-being means being healthy, safe and able to live a good life. Healthy ecosystems give people clean water and air, food, pollination of crops, protection from floods, places to play and rest, jobs, and cultural connections to the land.

The two benefits are linked. When an ecosystem is stable, it keeps giving people what they need. When people's needs are met, they are better able to care for the environment.

      Lesson 5 · Why people act
      SL2.K5

      Three motivations for stewardship

      A motivation is a reason that makes someone want to act. Stewardship can be motivated by:

      • Future generations: a desire to take care of the environment so children and grandchildren have clean water, wildlife and wild places too.
      • Monetary incentives: money reasons. People may save money, earn money, or get a refund or rebate. Alberta's deposit on drink containers is an example: you pay a small deposit and get it back when you return the container.
      • Government regulations: rules and laws made by governments. Breaking them can bring fines. Examples are laws that protect species at risk and city rules about sorting waste.

      Most actions have more than one motivation. A family might fix a leaky tap because it saves money on the water bill and because they want to save water for the future.

      Lesson 6 · Caring and owning
      SL2.K1SL2.S5

      Caring for something versus owning it

      Owning something means it belongs to you by law. An owner can usually use it, sell it, and keep others from using it.

      Protecting and caring for something means you take responsibility for keeping it safe and healthy. You can care for something you do not own, like a park, a river, or a borrowed library book.

      Similarities: both involve making decisions about something, and both can involve feeling responsible for it. Your actions affect it either way.

      Differences: owning is about rights, and caring is about responsibility. Owning does not guarantee caring: an owner can let something fall apart. Caring does not need owning: volunteers clean ravines that the city owns.

      Much of the environment, such as rivers, air, wildlife and most of Alberta's land, is not owned by any one person. Stewardship asks everyone to act like a caretaker of what we share.

      Edmonton is on Treaty 6 territory. Many First Nations and Métis people describe their relationship with the land as one of responsibility and respect rather than ownership. Ask a local Knowledge Keeper or Elder to learn more about these perspectives.

      Investigation 3 · The jacket loop
      SL2.S4SL2.K3

      How many winter jackets does a class need?

      Kids outgrow winter jackets fast. In this model, each student wears a jacket about 100 times in one winter, then outgrows it. A jacket can last about 300 wears before it wears out, or 450 if small rips and broken zippers are repaired. All numbers are made up for practice.

      Circular choices
      Wears needed9000
      New jackets made90
      Sent to landfill90 kg
      Recycled0 kg

      Worked example 30 students, 3 winters, 100 wears per winter: 30 × 3 × 100 = 9000 wears needed. Linear (each jacket used one winter, then thrown out): 9000 ÷ 100 = 90 jackets. Passing jackets on until they wear out: 9000 ÷ 300 = 30 jackets. Percent fewer jackets = (90 − 30) ÷ 90 × 100 ≈ 67%.

      percent fewer = (old − new) ÷ old × 100

      Classroom waste audit

      A waste audit means sorting and weighing what a group throws away. Here is one made-up day of garbage from one classroom bin. Choose where each item should really go, then check. Sorting rules differ from city to city, so always check your own city's guide.

      Choose a place for each item. Reduce means the item could be avoided next time, such as a single-use item replaced by a reusable one. Today it still goes to landfill.

      diversion rate = mass kept out of landfill ÷ total mass × 100
      water saved = (old flow − new flow) × minutes × number of times
      Worked example A bin holds 4000 g of waste. 2600 g is composted or recycled. Diversion rate = 2600 ÷ 4000 × 100 = 65%. A shower head uses 9 L per minute. A low-flow one uses 6 L per minute. For 5-minute showers, 7 times a week: (9 − 6) × 5 × 7 = 105 L saved each week.
