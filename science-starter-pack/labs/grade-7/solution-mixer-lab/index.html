Science 7 · Matter: Pure Substances and Mixtures · M2

Solution Mixer Lab

Understanding: Particles can interact to form solutions. When a solute dissolves, its particles spread evenly among the particles of a solvent. How fast that happens, how much is dissolved, and how much could ever dissolve are three different ideas. This lab helps you keep them apart.

Learn

Seven short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with what a solution is, watch particles dissolve, then learn the words for how much is dissolved and how much can dissolve.

Lesson 1 · Three words
M2.K1

Solute, solvent, solution

In the last unit you learned that a solution is a homogeneous mixture. It looks the same all the way through because its particles are evenly mixed.

Every solution has two kinds of parts:

  • The solute is the substance that dissolves. In salt water, the salt is the solute.
  • The solvent is the substance that does the dissolving. In salt water, the water is the solvent. The solvent is usually the part there is more of.

Water dissolves so many substances that a solution in water has its own name: an aqueous solution. Solutions can also be gases, like air, or solids, like brass.

Sand stirred into water is not a solution. The sand does not dissolve. It stays in grains you can see, and it settles to the bottom.

Remember: solute + solvent = solution. The solute dissolves in the solvent.

Lesson 2 · Watch it happen
M2.K2

Dissolving spreads particles evenly

Dissolving is the process where particles of solute break away from each other and become evenly spread out among the particles of the solvent.

Solvent particles are always moving. They bump into the solid solute and pull particles off its surface. Those solute particles then drift and mix until they are spread evenly through the whole solution.

Dissolved particles are far too small to see, so a solution is usually clear. It can still have colour, like tea or a drink made from powder.

Dissolving is not melting. Melting needs heat to turn a solid into a liquid. Sugar dissolves in cold water without melting. The particles are also not destroyed: 10 g of sugar stirred into 100 g of water makes 110 g of sugar water.

Lesson 3 · Faster dissolving
M2.K3

Stirring and surface area speed it up

The rate of dissolving is how fast a solute dissolves. Two things you can do make it faster:

  • Stirring moves the solution. It carries dissolved particles away from the solute, so fresh solvent keeps touching the surface.
  • Increasing surface area means breaking the solute into smaller pieces. Only particles on the outside can be pulled away. Crushing a sugar cube puts many more particles on the outside.

Warming the solvent usually speeds up dissolving too, because the particles move faster.

Careful: stirring and crushing change how fast a solute dissolves. They do not change how much can dissolve. That amount is the solubility (Lesson 5).

Lesson 4 · How strong is it?
M2.K4

Concentration: dilute, concentrated, saturated

The concentration of a solution tells how much solute is dissolved in a certain amount of solution. One common way to write it:

concentration = mass of solute ÷ volume of solution
c = m ÷ V  (in g/100 mL or g/L)

Two pairs of words describe concentration:

  • Dilute means only a little solute is dissolved. Concentrated means a lot is dissolved. These are comparison words. Weak juice is dilute compared with juice made from more powder.
  • Saturated means the solution holds as much solute as it can at that temperature. Extra solute just sits on the bottom. Unsaturated means more could still dissolve.

Adding water makes a solution more dilute. This is called diluting. The same amount of solute is spread through more solvent.

Lesson 5 · A property of each substance
M2.K5

Solubility

Solubility is the greatest amount of a solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a specific temperature. It tells you how much is needed to make a saturated solution.

Solubility is a physical property, like density or boiling point. Each substance has its own value, so solubility can help identify a substance.

A solubility is only complete when it names three things: the amount of solute, the amount of solvent and the temperature. For example: 36 g of table salt in 100 mL of water at 20 °C.

A substance that dissolves well is soluble. One that barely dissolves at all, like sand or chalk in water, is called insoluble. Solubility also depends on the solvent. Cooking oil does not dissolve in water, but it does dissolve in some other solvents.

Alberta example: a thick layer of salt lies deep underground near Fort Saskatchewan. Companies pump water down to dissolve some of it and bring the salt water back up. This leaves large caverns that are used to store fuels.

Lesson 6 · Turn up the heat
M2.K6

Temperature changes solubility

For most solids, more solute can dissolve as the solvent gets warmer. That is why you can dissolve far more sugar in hot tea than in iced tea.

A solubility curve is a graph of solubility at each temperature. The curve for potassium nitrate rises steeply. The curve for table salt is almost flat: hot water dissolves only a little more salt than cold water.

If a warm saturated solution cools, it can no longer hold all its solute. The extra solute comes out as solid crystals. This is called crystallization. Rock candy is made this way.

Gases work the other way. Oxygen and carbon dioxide are less soluble in warm water. A warm can of pop goes flat faster, and trout in Alberta's mountain streams need cold water that holds more oxygen.

Lesson 7 · Putting it together
M2.K2M2.K4M2.K5M2.K6

Dissolving, concentration and solubility

These three ideas are linked, but they answer different questions:

DissolvingWhat is happening? Solute particles are spreading out among solvent particles.
ConcentrationHow much is dissolved right now, for each amount of solution?
SolubilityWhat is the most that could dissolve at this temperature?

When the concentration reaches the solubility, the solution is saturated and dissolving seems to stop. Warming the solution raises the solubility, so dissolving can start again.

Teacher notes

  • M2.K4: the draft pairs "dilute or saturated". The lab teaches two separate scales: dilute and concentrated describe how much solute is dissolved (comparison words with no fixed cut-off), while saturated and unsaturated describe whether more can dissolve at that temperature. A saturated solution of a barely soluble substance is still very dilute. Concentration is shown as c = m ÷ V in g/100 mL; students are not asked to memorize it.
  • M2.K6: "can increase" is taught as true for most solids. Table salt changes very little, and gases such as oxygen and carbon dioxide become less soluble as temperature rises (warm pop, trout and cold water). Crystallization on cooling is included as brief context.
  • M2.K5: solubility is taught as complete only when it names solute amount, solvent amount and temperature, and as depending on the solvent. Values are rounded reference values in g per 100 g of water (about 100 mL): table salt 36.0, potassium nitrate 31.6, sucrose about 204, baking soda 9.6 at 20 °C; oxygen about 9.1 mg/L at 20 °C. Please spot check against your preferred data book.
  • M2.K2 and M2.K3: dissolving is distinguished from melting, and mass is conserved on dissolving. Warming is mentioned once as another way to speed dissolving; the draft lists only stirring and surface area. The lab stresses that stirring and surface area change the rate, not the solubility.
  • Alberta examples: salt caverns near Fort Saskatchewan made by dissolving underground salt with water, Edmonton road brine and trout in mountain streams are described in general terms only.
  • Lab Bench: Investigation 1 is a simulation built from the reference curves, and the dissolving race times in Lesson 3 are invented; both are labelled as practice data.

Indigenous ways of knowing, doing and being

Medicines, birch sap and sea ice

This resource was made in Edmonton, in Treaty 6 territory. We acknowledge the nêhiyaw (Cree), Dene, Anishinaabe (Saulteaux), Nakota Isga (Nakota Sioux) and Niitsitapi (Blackfoot) peoples, the Métis, whose homeland this is, and the Inuit, one of whose largest communities south of the 60th parallel lives in Edmonton. We also honour all First Nations, Métis and Inuit peoples whose knowledge shapes these lands.

Dissolving, concentration and the effect of temperature are part of everyday work on the land for many First Nations and Inuit communities. These connections come from Cree lands at Edmonton, a Woodland Cree birch syrup camp in Treaty 8 and Inuit sea ice in Nunatsiavut. Each one links to the solutions, concentrations and solubility you explore in this lab.

Edmonton · Cree: the maskêkosak of Enoch Cree Nation (maskêkosihk)

maskêkosihk: the Land of Medicines

Big Island lies in the North Saskatchewan River valley in southwest Edmonton. A Government of Alberta fact sheet says the traditional lands of the maskêkosak, the people of the Land of Medicines, encompassed Big Island. Their lands are known as maskêkosihk, or Enoch Cree Nation, in Treaty No. 6 Territory. The fact sheet says plants provided plentiful remedies, and some say more than 200 medicines were gathered and used. Making a plant tea is one kind of dissolving: hot water draws substances out of leaves. The Cree Literacy Network says that in Saskatchewan and Alberta, Labrador tea is known as maskêkopakwa, muskeg tea.

Alberta · Cree: Woodland Cree First Nation (Treaty 8)

waskwayâpoy: birch sap becomes syrup

Woodland Cree First Nation, in the Peace River region and Treaty 8, makes syrup from waskwayâpoy (birch sap) each spring. An Alberta Historic Places article written with the Nation describes Knowledge Keepers Nancy Williams and Sara Williams tapping birch trees, with Elders, youth and community members taking part. A tapped birch is left to rest for at least three seasons. The sap is strained and simmered over low heat, so water evaporates and the sugar becomes more concentrated. Birch sap holds much less sugar than maple sap. The article says birch takes about 120 parts sap for one part syrup, compared with about 40 to 1 for maple.

Source: Alberta Historic Places RETROactive: Season of the Birch: Continuing Practices of Woodland Cree First Nation (2024, written in collaboration with Woodland Cree First Nation). Source of the Cree name waskwayâpoy (birch sap)

Canada · Inuit (Nunatsiavut, Labrador)

Sikuk: sea ice that pushes out salt

Chaim Christiana Andersen, from Nain, Nunatsiavut, wrote with Geoff Rayner-Canham in the chemistry magazine Chem 13 News about Sikuk (sea ice). He explains that Inuit use sea ice mainly for transportation. As sea water freezes, dissolved salt particles are pushed out, so the ice itself is essentially fresh water. In spring, freshwater pools form on its surface, and a photo shows him going to imittak (fetch water). In the Indigenous Peoples Atlas of Canada, Joey Angnatok of Nain and Rodd Laing of the Nunatsiavut Government write that pockets of brine form within sea ice, and that Inuktut has dozens of words for sea ice.

Source: University of Waterloo, Chem 13 News (reprint of February 2019 issue): Chemistry and Inuit Life and Culture: Sea ice, essential for northern survival, by Chaim Christiana Andersen and Geoff Rayner-Canham. Source of the Inuttitut words Sikuk (sea ice) and imittak (fetch water) · Indigenous Peoples Atlas of Canada: Sea ice, by Joey Angnatok and Rodd Laing

Talk and think

  • Woodland Cree First Nation simmers birch sap until it becomes syrup. If 120 L of sap makes about 1 L of syrup, did the mass of sugar change, or only the amount of water? Use the words dilute, concentrated and evaporate to explain. The article warns that too strong a boil can evaporate all the water. What would be left behind?
  • When sea water freezes, dissolved salt is pushed out of the ice. Predict how the concentration of the salt water just under new sea ice compares with ordinary sea water. Link your answer to the brine pockets that Angnatok and Laing describe, and to crystallization in Lesson 6.
  • Labrador tea, or maskêkopakwa, is one of many plants made into tea. Using a store bought tea bag, plan a fair test of how water temperature affects how quickly the colour spreads. Which variable do you change, which do you measure and which must stay the same? Is this a test of rate of dissolving, or of solubility?
