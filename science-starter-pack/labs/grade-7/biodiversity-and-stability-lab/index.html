1 of 8

Ecosystems keep changing

LS1.K1

An ecosystem is all the living things in an area plus the non-living things around them, such as water, soil, sunlight and temperature.

Ecosystems are dynamic. That means their characteristics change over time. Some changes are quick and repeat every year, like Edmonton's river valley turning from green summer forest to snowy winter woods. Other changes take years or decades, like a wetland filling up in wet years and shrinking in dry years.

A beaver pond is a good example. Beavers can turn a small creek into a pond full of new life. Years later the pond can drain and become a meadow.

Many First Nations and Métis families have watched the same lands for generations, noticing changes in berry crops, water levels and animal numbers. Long-term knowledge like this helps everyone see how ecosystems change over time.

Model: a beaver pond over 40 years

2 of 8

From species to ecosystem

LS1.K2

Ecologists describe nature in levels. Each level is built from the one before it.

  • Species: one type of organism. Members of a species can mate and produce offspring that can also reproduce.
  • Population: all the members of one species living and interacting in the same area.
  • Community: all the populations of different species living and interacting in that area.
  • Ecosystem: the community interacting with abiotic (non-living) factors such as water, soil, air, light and temperature.

Living parts of an ecosystem are biotic factors. Non-living parts are abiotic factors.

Model: Elk Island National Park, east of Edmonton

3 of 8

Three kinds of biodiversity

LS1.K3

Biodiversity is the variety of life. Scientists look at it in three ways.

  • Genetic diversity: variation among organisms of the same species. Not every snowshoe hare or aspen tree has the same genes.
  • Species diversity: the number of different species (called richness) and how evenly individuals are spread among them (called evenness or abundance).
  • Ecosystem diversity: the number of different habitat types in an area, like aspen groves, sloughs, grassland and spruce bog all in one landscape.

Scientists also use "ecosystem diversity" for the variety of whole ecosystems across a region. Alberta has six natural regions: Grassland, Parkland, Foothills, Rocky Mountain, Boreal Forest and Canadian Shield.

Model: compare two meadows

4 of 8

What stability means

LS1.K4

Ecosystem stability is the ability of an ecosystem to keep its structure, its biodiversity and its processes over time.

  • Structure: its layers and parts, such as tree canopy, shrubs, ground plants, and who eats whom.
  • Biodiversity: the variety of species it holds.
  • Processes: the jobs it keeps doing, such as photosynthesis, decomposition, recycling nutrients and filtering water.

A stable ecosystem still changes. It can bend during a drought, a hard winter or a bad insect year, then return to about the same condition. Think of a ball in a bowl. A deep bowl lets the ball roll back after a push. A shallow bowl lets it roll out.

Niches: a role and a place

LS1.K5LS1.K6LS1.K8

A habitat is where a species lives. An ecological niche is the role a species plays in its habitat: what it eats, where and when it feeds, where it nests, and what conditions it needs.

When species have different niches, they can share the same environment. Their needs overlap less, so competition for food and space is reduced.

6 of 8

Adaptations fit the environment

LS1.K7LS1.K6

An adaptation is a feature that makes a species well suited to a specific environment. Adaptations can be:

  • Structural: a body part or shape, like the lynx's wide furry paws.
  • Behavioural: something the organism does, like a ground squirrel hibernating.
  • Physiological: how the body works inside, like a wood frog making its own antifreeze.

Adaptations also shape a species' niche. The lynx's paws let it hunt hares in deep snow where other predators struggle.

Model: Alberta species field guide

Predators help keep balance

LS1.K9

Predation is when one organism (the predator) hunts and eats another (the prey). Predators keep prey populations from growing so large that they eat up their food supply.

Snowshoe hares and Canada lynx in the boreal forest are a famous example. Fur-trading records from the Hudson's Bay Company showed that both populations rise and fall in a cycle of about 10 years. Food supply and predators both drive the cycle.

Living together: symbiosis

LS1.K10LS1.K6

Symbiosis is a close, long-term relationship between two different species. There are three kinds. The signs show who benefits (+), who is harmed (−) and who is not affected (0).

Predation is not usually called symbiosis because the predator and prey do not live together for a long time. A parasite usually harms its host without killing it quickly, because the parasite needs the host to stay alive.

Bench 2

Quadrat survey: measure biodiversity

LS1.S2

Ecologists cannot count every plant in a park. They lay down square frames called quadrats in random spots and count what is inside. Then they calculate:

  • Species richness: how many different species were found.
  • Simpson's index of diversity: a number from 0 to 1 that combines richness and evenness. Closer to 1 means more diverse.
D = 1 − Σ (n ÷ N)²

n is the count of one species. N is the total count of all individuals. Σ means "add up for every species". You do not need to memorize this. Use it as a tool.

Bench 5

Drought test: does biodiversity help?

LS1.S5LS1.K4

In a long experiment in Minnesota grasslands, ecologist David Tilman's team found that plots with more plant species lost less plant cover in a drought and recovered faster. This model works the same way.

Each plot starts full of plants. In years 4 and 5 a drought hits. Every plant species handles drought differently. Choose how many species the plot has, then run 20 plots and compare.
