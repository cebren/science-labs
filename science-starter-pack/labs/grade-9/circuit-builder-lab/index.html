Circuit Builder Lab

Understanding: Energy can be transferred by a current within a circuit. Build circuits, open and close switches, measure volts and amperes, and use Ohm’s law to predict what a circuit will do.

Learn

Eleven short lessons in order. Start with what current is, close a circuit, and see what voltage does. Then learn the parts and symbols, compare series and parallel circuits, measure with meters, test materials, explore resistance and Ohm’s law, and finish with electrical safety.

Lesson 1 · Electric current
E1.K1E1.K2

Moving charge carries energy

Metal wires are full of electrons, tiny particles with a negative charge that are free to move. When a wire is connected to a battery, the electrons all start drifting the same way. This flow of charge is an electric current.

The current transfers energy from the battery to the devices in the circuit. The electrons drift slowly, often less than a millimetre each second. The energy still arrives almost instantly, because every electron in the wire starts moving at the same moment, like water already filling a hose.

Current is the rate at which charge passes a point. It is measured in amperes (A), often called amps. One ampere means one coulomb of charge passes each second. The unit is named after André-Marie Ampère, a French physicist who showed in the 1820s how electric currents push and pull on each other.

current = charge ÷ time    I = Q ÷ t    1 A = 1 C/s
Remember: current is how much charge passes a point each second. More charge per second means a bigger current.

Model: count the charge

Change the current. Watch how many electrons cross the checkpoint each second. Each dot stands for a huge number of real electrons.

Lesson 2 · Closed circuits and current direction
E1.K3E1.K9

A complete loop, and which way it flows

A circuit is a closed path that current can flow around without a break. Open a switch or break a wire, and the path has a gap. Current stops everywhere in that loop at once.

Scientists agreed on a direction for current in the 1700s, long before anyone knew about electrons. They chose the way a positive charge would move: out of the positive terminal, around the circuit, and back into the negative terminal. This is called conventional current.

Electrons were discovered in 1897. They are negative, so they actually drift the other way, from the negative terminal toward the positive terminal. Circuit diagrams still use conventional current, and both descriptions predict the same results.

Remember: conventional current goes from + to −. Electron flow goes from − to +.

Model: close the loop

Tap the switch (or focus it and press Enter) to open or close the circuit. Then choose which direction to show.

Lesson 3 · Voltage
E1.K4

The push that makes current flow

Inside a battery, chemical reactions push electrons toward one terminal. That terminal gets extra negative charge and the other is left positive. This separation of charge creates a potential difference, also called voltage, between the two terminals.

Voltage tells how much energy each coulomb of charge gains from the source, or gives up to a device. It is measured in volts (V). One volt means one joule of energy for every coulomb of charge.

When a closed path joins the terminals, the potential difference makes charge flow. A bigger voltage pushes a bigger current through the same bulb. A standard AA cell gives about 1.5 V. Joining cells end to end in a battery adds their voltages.

voltage = energy ÷ charge    1 V = 1 J/C
Remember: no potential difference, no current. Voltage is measured between two points.

Model: stack the cells

Add 1.5 V cells to the battery. The bulb in this model acts like a 10 Ω resistor.

Lesson 4 · Parts of a circuit
E1.K5

Four jobs in every circuit

Most circuits have parts that do four different jobs:

  • Energy store: a cell or battery stores energy chemically and supplies it to the circuit.
  • Conductor: wires let charge move between the parts.
  • Load device: a device that transfers energy into another store, such as a bulb (light and heat), a motor (motion) or a speaker (sound).
  • Control device: a part that manages the current, such as a switch that turns it on and off, or a resistor that limits how much flows.

A flashlight has all four: batteries, metal strips, a bulb or LED, and a switch.

A resistor also warms up as current passes through it, so it transfers some energy too. Its main job in a circuit is to control the current.

Lesson 5 · Schematic diagrams
E1.K6E1.S1

Drawing circuits with symbols

A schematic diagram is a map of a circuit. Each part is drawn as a simple symbol, and wires are drawn as straight lines with square corners. Anyone who knows the symbols can build the circuit from the diagram.

Parts can be joined in two ways. In a series circuit, the parts are connected one after another in a single loop. In a parallel circuit, the parts sit on separate branches, and each branch connects across the same two points.

In a cell symbol, the long line is the positive terminal and the short thick line is the negative terminal.

Lesson 6 · Series and parallel
E1.K7E1.K8E1.S4

One path or many paths

In a series circuit, current flows along a single path. The current is the same at every point in the loop. If one bulb is removed or burns out, the loop is broken and every bulb goes out. Adding more bulbs makes the current smaller, so each bulb gets dimmer.

In a parallel circuit, current flows along several paths. The current splits at a junction, and the total current equals the sum of the branch currents. Each branch gets the full voltage of the source. A branch can be opened while the others keep working, which is why the outlets and lights in a house are wired in parallel.

series: I is the same everywhere
parallel: Itotal = I1 + I2 + I3   and   Vbranch = Vsource

This model ignores the small resistance of the wires and the battery, as most school diagrams do.

Model: series or parallel bulbs

Choose the arrangement and the number of bulbs. In series, tap a bulb to unscrew it. In parallel, tap a branch switch to open it.

Lesson 7 · Measuring circuits
E1.K10E1.S3

Ammeters, voltmeters and multimeters

  • An ammeter measures current in amperes. It goes in series, so the current passes through it. You break the loop and put the ammeter in the gap.
  • A voltmeter measures potential difference in volts. It connects across a part, in parallel, with one lead on each side.
  • A multimeter can measure volts, amperes and ohms. You turn the dial to choose. To measure resistance, the part must have no power connected.

Always start on the highest range and work down, and check the dial before you connect the leads.

Model: connect the meter

Choose a meter and a way to connect it to the bulb. Then check.

Lesson 8 · Conductors and insulators
E1.K11E1.K12

Which materials let charge through?

A conductor is a material that lets charge move through it easily. Metals such as copper and aluminium are excellent conductors because they have electrons that are free to move. Copper is used in house wiring, and aluminium is used in many overhead power lines because it is light.

An insulator is a material that resists the movement of charge. Rubber, plastic, glass and dry wood are insulators. Wires are coated in plastic or rubber so the current stays inside the copper.

Water is a special case. Tap water conducts because it has dissolved minerals that form charged particles called ions. Deionized water has had those ions removed, so it is a poor conductor. As soon as anything dissolves in it, it starts to conduct again.

Remember: treat all water, and wet skin, as a conductor around electricity.

Model: conductor tester

Pick a material to place across the gap in the tester circuit.

Lesson 9 · Resistance
E1.K13E1.K14E1.K15

What slows the current down

Resistance measures how much a part reduces the current. It is measured in ohms (Ω). The higher the resistance, the smaller the current for the same voltage.

Three properties of a conductor change its resistance:

  • Material: copper has low resistance. Nichrome, used in toaster wires, has much higher resistance.
  • Length: a longer wire has more resistance. Double the length, double the resistance.
  • Cross-sectional area: a thicker wire has less resistance, like a wider hallway letting more people through.

A resistor is a part made to have a set resistance. Resistors control current in devices: they protect an LED from too much current, and a variable resistor dims a light or sets a fan speed.

Temperature also matters. Most metal wires have more resistance when they are hot.

Model: wire builder

Change the material, length and thickness of a wire. Compare its resistance with a copper wire 1 m long and 0.5 mm across.

Lesson 10 · Ohm’s law
E1.K16E1.S6

Voltage, current and resistance together

Ohm’s law connects the three quantities:

V = I × R    I = V ÷ R    R = V ÷ I

V is voltage in volts, I is current in amperes, and R is resistance in ohms. You do not need to memorize it. Cover the quantity you want in the triangle, and the other two show you what to do.

  • Raise the voltage, keep resistance the same: the current goes up by the same factor.
  • Raise the resistance, keep voltage the same: the current goes down.

For a resistor at a steady temperature, a graph of current against voltage is a straight line through zero.

A bulb filament gets very hot, so its resistance rises as the current grows. Its graph curves. The bulbs in this lab are modelled as fixed resistors to keep the numbers simple.

Lesson 11 · Electrical safety
E1.K17E1.S8

Staying safe around current

Current through the body can burn tissue, make muscles lock so you cannot let go, and stop the heart from beating normally. Less than one tenth of an ampere across the chest can be deadly. Household outlets in Canada supply about 120 V, which is enough to push a dangerous current through a person, especially with wet skin.

  • Keep electrical devices away from water. Outlets near sinks and bathtubs use a GFCI (ground fault circuit interrupter) that cuts the power in a fraction of a second if current leaks.
  • Fuses and circuit breakers open a circuit when the current gets too large, which prevents wires from overheating and starting fires.
  • Never use cords with cracked insulation, and pull a plug by the plug, not the cord.
  • Stay far away from power lines and downed lines. Keep kites, drones and ladders clear. In Alberta, use Click Before You Dig to locate buried lines before digging.
  • In the lab, use low-voltage cells, check circuits with your teacher before connecting power, and disconnect power before changing a circuit.

Calculate V, I or R

Use the calculator to check any two values, study the worked example, then solve random practice problems.

Calculator

Worked example

Problem: A toaster element has a resistance of 12 Ω and is plugged into a 120 V outlet. What current flows?

Given: V = 120 V, R = 12 Ω. Find I.

I = V ÷ R = 120 V ÷ 12 Ω = 10 A

Answer: about 10 A flows through the element.

Teacher notes

  • E1.K1: current is taught as a flow of charge that transfers energy. Electrons drift slowly while energy is transferred almost instantly. In liquids such as tap water the moving charges are ions, not electrons (noted in Lesson 8).
  • E1.K2: the coulomb is named so that “rate at which charge passes” has a unit (1 A = 1 C/s); I = Q ÷ t is shown as context only. Ampère is described briefly as a French physicist whose 1820s work showed currents push and pull on each other.
  • E1.K4: the draft says voltage arises from “a difference in electric charge between two points”. The lab teaches the more precise idea: a battery separates charge, creating a difference in electric potential energy per coulomb between two points (1 V = 1 J/C). The draft wording is kept as the cause of the potential difference.
  • E1.K5: the draft lists a resistor as a control device. The lab keeps that and notes once that a resistor also transfers some energy to the thermal store.
  • E1.K7 / E1.K8: series voltages adding to the source voltage is shown as brief context in the measuring investigation. “Not every branch needs to be closed” is taught as: other branches keep working when one is open. Wire and internal battery resistance are ignored, so each parallel branch gets exactly the source voltage.
  • E1.K9: conventional current is dated to the 1700s (Franklin’s choice of positive and negative is not named) and the electron to 1897.
  • E1.K11 / E1.K12: tap water is taught as a conductor because of dissolved ions, weaker than metals but enough to be dangerous. Deionized water is taught as a poor conductor that becomes conductive once anything dissolves in it.
  • E1.K14: wire resistances use rounded room-temperature resistivities (copper 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m, aluminium 2.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m, nichrome 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m). The formula is not shown; students see the proportions only. Temperature is mentioned once.
  • E1.K16: Ohm’s law is taught for resistors at steady temperature. Bulb filaments are not ohmic; the lab models every bulb as a fixed 10 Ω resistor and says so.
  • E1.K17 / E1.S8: safety facts are general (about 120 V household supply, GFCI outlets, fuses and breakers, power lines, Click Before You Dig). Check them against your local utility’s guidance. Electrical injury can be a sensitive topic if a student has a personal connection.
  • Data: all meter readings, fault cases and practice problems are simulated practice data. They support, but do not replace, hands-on circuits with low-voltage cells.
