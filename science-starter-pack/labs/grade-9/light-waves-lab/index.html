Science 9 · Waves: Sound and Light · W2

Light Waves Lab

Understanding: Light waves transfer energy from the source of the light wave. Sunlight on a cold prairie morning, the northern lights over northern Alberta and the lamp on your desk all send energy outward from a source. Light needs no medium, travels in straight lines and behaves in ways you can predict.

Learn

Nine short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. First see where light comes from and how it travels. Then learn what sets brightness and colour, and finish with how we see objects and why shadows form.

Safety: never look straight at the Sun, even through a prism, sunglasses or a camera. Never shine a laser pointer at anyone's eyes.

Lesson 1 · Light carries energy
W2.K1W2.S1

Every light wave starts at a source

A light source is an object that gives off its own light. The Sun, a campfire, an LED bulb and a firefly are all light sources. Each one changes some other kind of energy into light.

Light then carries that energy away from the source. When light lands on something, the energy can be transferred again. A solar panel turns it into electricity. A leaf uses it to make food. Dark pavement warms up.

Remember: no source, no light. The light wave carries energy from the source to whatever it reaches.

Model: from source to target

Pick a source and a place for the light to land. Follow the energy.

Lesson 2 · No medium needed
W2.K1W2.S1

Light can cross empty space

Sound is a mechanical wave. It needs particles to pass the vibration along. Light is different. Light is an electromagnetic wave: a wave made of changing electric and magnetic fields. It does not need a medium, which is a material for a wave to travel through.

That is how sunlight crosses about 150 million km of nearly empty space to reach Earth. In a vacuum, light travels at about 300 000 km every second, written 3.00 × 108 m/s. Nothing travels faster.

time = distance ÷ speed
Sun to Earth: 150 000 000 km ÷ 300 000 km/s = 500 s ≈ 8.3 minutes

You do not need to memorize these formulas. Use them as tools.

Model: a bell and a lamp in a jar

Pump the air out of the jar. What happens to the sound? What happens to the light?

Lesson 3 · Straight lines, every direction
W2.K1W2.S1

Light travels outward in straight lines

A bulb sends light out in all directions. That is why everyone in the room can see it. We draw the path of light as a ray: a straight arrow pointing away from the source.

In one material, such as air, light travels in straight lines. You can test this with three cards that each have a small hole. You only see the lamp when all three holes line up.

Light does change direction when it reflects off a surface or passes into a new material, like air into water. You will study that bending in W3.

Model: rays from a bulb

Model: the three-card test

Lesson 4 · Brightness and colour
W2.K2W2.S1

Amplitude sets brightness. Frequency sets colour.

A light wave has the same parts as any wave. The amplitude is the height of a crest above the middle line. A bigger amplitude carries more energy, so the light looks brighter.

The frequency is how many waves pass a point each second. For light it is huge, so we measure it in terahertz (THz): one trillion waves each second. Frequency sets the colour. Red light has the lowest frequency we can see. Violet has the highest.

The wavelength is the distance from one crest to the next. Light with a high frequency has a short wavelength.

c = f × λ
speed = frequency × wavelength
Shortcut: λ (nm) = 300 000 ÷ f (THz)

Most colours you see are mixes. A screen makes yellow by mixing red and green light. A single frequency gives one pure colour.

Model: tune a light wave

Lesson 5 · The electromagnetic spectrum
W2.K4W2.S1

We see only a thin slice

Light is one member of a big family of electromagnetic waves called the electromagnetic spectrum. From lowest to highest frequency: radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. All of them travel at the same speed in a vacuum.

Human eyes detect only a small part, the visible light spectrum. It runs from red (about 700 nm) to violet (about 400 nm). A nanometre (nm) is one billionth of a metre. Some animals see a bit more. Bees can see some ultraviolet.

Higher frequency waves carry more energy. That is why ultraviolet can burn skin and X-rays need careful shielding.

Model: explore the spectrum

Select a band (tap, or Tab to it and press Enter).

Lesson 6 · White light and a prism
W2.K5W2.S3

White light is every colour together

Sunlight looks white, but it contains all the colours of the visible spectrum. A prism is a block of glass with flat, angled faces. When white light passes through it, each colour bends by a slightly different amount. Violet bends the most and red bends the least. The colours spread apart into a band called a spectrum. This spreading is called dispersion.

In 1666 Isaac Newton showed that a prism does not add colour to light. It separates colours that were already there. Raindrops do the same thing to make a rainbow. On very cold days, ice crystals in the air can make bright spots beside the Sun called sundogs, often seen over Alberta.

Remember the order: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet. Red has the lowest frequency and the longest wavelength.

Model: shine light into a prism

Lesson 7 · Transparent, translucent, opaque
W2.K3W2.S2

Materials let light through in different ways

When light reaches a material, it can pass through (transmission), bounce off (reflection) or be taken in (absorption). Absorbed light energy usually warms the material.

  • Transparent materials let almost all light pass straight through. You see clear images through them. Example: window glass.
  • Translucent materials let some light through but scatter it in many directions. Images look blurry. Example: wax paper.
  • Opaque materials let no light through. They reflect or absorb all of it. Example: cardboard.

Model: shine a beam at a material

Percentages are rough values for a model, not measurements.

Lesson 8 · How we see things
W2.K6W2.S1

Light must reach your eye

You see an object only when light from it enters your eye. A luminous object makes its own light, so you see it directly. A lamp and the Sun are luminous.

Most objects are non-luminous. You see them because they reflect light from a source into your eyes. The Moon is non-luminous. Moonlight is sunlight reflected off the Moon's surface.

In a room with no light at all, you see nothing, even though the objects are still there.

Model: a lamp, a mug and an eye

Lesson 9 · Shadows
W2.K7W2.S4

A shadow is where light is blocked

Light travels in straight lines, so it cannot bend around an opaque object to fill the space behind it. The object absorbs or reflects the light that hits it. That leaves a dark area behind the object: a shadow.

A small source makes a sharp shadow. A wide source, like a long fluorescent light, makes a shadow with two parts. The umbra is the fully dark centre, where no light reaches. The penumbra is the partly lit edge, where only some of the source is blocked.

Move an object closer to the source and its shadow gets bigger. Translucent objects make faint shadows because some light still gets through.

Model: make a shadow

Lab Bench

Four investigations. Describe light waves with numbers, test materials with a light meter, repeat Newton's prism experiment and predict the size of shadows. Each one has a worked example and new practice each time.

Investigation 1 · Describe a light wave
W2.S1W2.K2W2.K4

From frequency to wavelength and colour

Every colour of visible light has its own frequency and wavelength. Use the shortcut to find the wavelength, then use the colour table to name the colour.

Worked example A light wave has a frequency of 500 THz. λ = 300 000 ÷ f = 300 000 ÷ 500 = 600 nm 600 nm falls in the orange range, so the light is orange.

Investigation 2 · Light meter test
W2.S2W2.K3

How much light gets through?

Question: how do different materials affect light passing through them?

A lamp shines 1000 lux at a light meter. (Lux measures how much light lands on a surface.) Put a material in the way and read the meter. Then look through it at a sign. Is the image clear or blurry?

Worked example Wax paper: meter reads 420 lux. 420 ÷ 1000 × 100% = 42% gets through. The sign looks blurry. Some light passes, but it is scattered, so wax paper is translucent. Tinted glass lets less light through, but the sign is still clear. It is transparent.

Meter readings are simulated practice data, with a little random variation like a real meter.

Investigation 3 · Newton's two prisms
W2.S3W2.K5

Does a prism make colours, or separate them?

The first prism spreads white light into a spectrum. A card with a narrow slit lets only one colour through to a second prism.

Predict: will the second prism split that colour into more colours?

Worked example Green light passes the slit. The second prism bends it, but it stays green. So the prism did not add colours. White light already held all of them, and the prism separated them.

Quick check

Investigation 4 · Predict a shadow
W2.S4W2.K7

How big will the shadow be?

Light from a small source travels in straight lines past the top and bottom of an object. Those lines spread out, so the shadow on a screen is bigger than the object. Similar triangles give a formula.

shadow height = object height × (source to screen) ÷ (source to object)
H = h × D ÷ d
Worked example A 10 cm puppet is 50 cm from a small flashlight. The wall is 200 cm from the flashlight. H = 10 × 200 ÷ 50 = 40 cm. The shadow is 4 times as tall as the puppet. Move the puppet to 100 cm: H = 10 × 200 ÷ 100 = 20 cm. Farther from the source means a smaller shadow.

Teacher notes

  • W2.K1: “travel in straight lines” is taught as straight paths through one even material. A note says light changes direction when it reflects or passes into a new material (left for W3), so students are not confused later. “Require a light source” is taught as needing a source that changes another form of energy into light. Speed is given as about 3.00 × 108 m/s in a vacuum, as context.
  • W2.K2: brightness is taught as set by amplitude because bigger waves carry more energy. Strictly, the energy carried grows with the square of the amplitude, and brightness also falls with distance as light spreads out. Frequency is taught as setting the colour of a single-frequency light. A note says most colours we see are mixes (for example, screens make yellow from red and green).
  • W2.K4: the visible spectrum is given as about 400 to 700 nm (about 430 to 750 THz). Colour band edges are rounded and vary between sources. c = f × λ and the shortcut λ (nm) = 300 000 ÷ f (THz) are tools, not for memorizing.
  • W2.K5: “white light contains all colours” is taught for sunlight. Many white LEDs and screens make light that looks white from only a few colours, so this is not mentioned as universal. Newton's prism experiment (1666) is summarized generally.
  • W2.K6: taught with luminous and non-luminous objects. Transparent objects such as glass are seen mainly because they reflect and bend a little of the light.
  • W2.K7: the draft says a shadow forms when light is “absorbed by a body”. The lab teaches the more precise idea: a shadow forms where an opaque object blocks light, by absorbing or reflecting it (a mirror also casts a shadow). Umbra and penumbra are added to explain shadow edges. Please review whether this precision suits the final wording.
  • Context: sundogs and the northern lights are general Alberta examples. Material percentages, light meter readings and shadow problems are made-up practice data and are labelled that way. Safety notes cover looking at the Sun and laser pointers.
