Science 9 · Space · SP1

Space Exploration and Law Lab

Understanding: The advancement of space exploration needs to ensure safety, sustainability, and equitable use. People explore space with rockets, computers, telescopes, rovers and stations. Canada builds robots and satellites for that work. Exploring brings benefits and costs, and international law sets some rules, but not all of them.

Learn

Nine short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. First meet the tools of space exploration and Canada's part in it. Then weigh the benefits and costs. Finish with the laws that guide space, and the gaps they leave.

Lesson 1 · The toolkit
SP1.K1

Technology makes space exploration possible

Space exploration is the study of space using spacecraft, instruments and people. Humans cannot travel far into space on their own, so exploration depends on technology.

Five kinds of technology do most of the work:

  • Rockets lift spacecraft off Earth and push them into orbit or beyond.
  • Computers guide rockets, control spacecraft and process the data they send back.
  • Telescopes collect light to study objects far away, from the ground or from orbit.
  • Rovers are robot vehicles that drive across another world and study it up close.
  • Space stations are labs in orbit where people live and work for months.
Big idea: each tool solves a problem. Rockets solve getting there. Computers solve control and data. Telescopes see far. Rovers go where people cannot yet go. Stations let people stay.

Model: open the toolkit

Lesson 2 · Getting off the ground
SP1.K1

Rockets and the computers that fly them

A rocket burns fuel and pushes hot gas out the bottom very fast. The gas pushes back on the rocket, so the rocket goes up. This is an example of Newton's third law: every push has an equal push in the opposite direction.

Most of a rocket's mass at launch is fuel. To save weight, rockets are built in stages. When a stage runs out of fuel, it drops away. The rocket no longer has to carry that empty tank, so the next stage can go faster with less fuel.

Computers steer the rocket many times each second, decide when to drop each stage, and check thousands of sensors. On the ground, computers run simulations before launch and help mission control track the flight.

Some rocket stages are now built to land and fly again. Reusing rockets lowers the cost of each launch.

Lesson 3 · Seeing far, roving, staying
SP1.K1

Telescopes, rovers and space stations

Space telescopes orbit above the air, which blurs and blocks some light. The James Webb Space Telescope, launched in 2021, sees infrared light from some of the first galaxies.

Rovers such as Curiosity and Perseverance drive on Mars. They carry cameras, drills and labs to test rocks for signs of past water and life.

Radio signals travel at the speed of light, about 300 000 km every second. Mars is so far away that a message takes several minutes to arrive. A driver on Earth cannot steer a rover in real time, so rovers use onboard computers to drive themselves around rocks.

time = distance ÷ speed of light   (t = d ÷ c)

The International Space Station (ISS) has had people on board since the year 2000. It orbits about 400 km up and circles Earth about every 90 minutes. Astronauts there run experiments on health, materials and plants.

Lesson 4 · Canada in space
SP1.K2

Canada's contributions: robots and satellites

Canada is known around the world for two kinds of space technology.

Robotics. Canada built the Canadarm for the Space Shuttle, then Canadarm2 and the two-armed robot Dextre for the ISS. Canadarm2 catches cargo ships and helped build the station. Canada is building Canadarm3 for Gateway, a planned station that will orbit the Moon.

Satellite technology. In 1962, Alouette 1 made Canada one of the first countries to design and build its own satellite. Anik A1 (1972) carried TV and phone signals across Canada, including to the North. The RADARSAT satellites use radar to see Earth's surface through cloud and darkness.

Canadian astronauts and scientists take part too. Marc Garneau, a Francophone from Quebec, was the first Canadian in space in 1984. In April 2026, Jeremy Hansen flew around the Moon on Artemis II. Canada also built a guidance sensor and a science instrument for the James Webb Space Telescope.

Alberta link: University of Alberta students built Ex-Alta 1, a small satellite called a CubeSat that went to orbit in 2017.

Lesson 5 · What we gain
SP1.K3

Benefits: data for life on Earth, and jobs

An implication is a result or effect that follows from a choice. Space exploration has positive and negative implications. Two big positive ones are data and jobs.

Data that benefits life on Earth. Satellites watch weather, wildfires, floods, crops and sea ice. Satellite navigation (GPS) guides tractors, planes and phones. Experiments on the ISS teach doctors about bones, muscles and the heart.

Employment. The space industry needs engineers, robotics technicians, software developers, data analysts, scientists, doctors, lawyers and many trades. Many of these jobs use space data on Earth, not in a rocket.

Alberta link: neuroArm, a surgical robot developed at the University of Calgary with the company that built Canadarm, used ideas from space robotics to help doctors operate on the brain.

Lesson 6 · What it costs
SP1.K3

Costs and space debris

Long-term costs. Space projects take many years and a lot of money. The ISS cost more than 100 billion US dollars, shared by many countries, and it needs constant repairs, supplies and crew. It is planned to be retired around 2030 and brought down safely.

Space debris is human-made junk in orbit: dead satellites, used rocket stages, and fragments from explosions and collisions. In low Earth orbit, objects travel at about 7 to 8 km every second. At that speed, even a fleck of paint can chip a window.

The European Space Agency reported about 40 000 tracked objects in orbit in 2025, and an estimated 1.2 million pieces larger than 1 cm. Every collision makes more fragments, which can cause more collisions. Scientists call this runaway chain the Kessler syndrome.

Sustainability: using orbits in a way that leaves them safe and usable for future generations. That means bringing satellites down at the end of their life and avoiding events that make debris.

Lesson 7 · The body in space
SP1.K3

Effects on the human body

Human bodies are built for Earth's gravity. In orbit, astronauts are in free fall, so they feel weightless. This is called microgravity. It changes the body in several ways:

  • Bones lose minerals, because they are not holding up the body's weight.
  • Muscles shrink, especially in the legs and back.
  • Fluids shift toward the head. Faces look puffy and eyesight can change.
  • Balance is confused at first, which can cause space sickness.
  • Radiation is much higher than on the ground, which raises the risk of cancer later in life.
  • Isolation and a strange sleep schedule can affect mood and focus.

Astronauts on the ISS exercise about two hours each day to slow bone and muscle loss. A trip to Mars would take years, so these effects are a major safety question.

Lesson 8 · Rules for space
SP1.K4

International space law

A treaty is a written agreement between countries. Countries that join a treaty promise to follow it. International space law is the set of treaties and agreements that guide what countries do in space.

The most important one is the Outer Space Treaty of 1967. Canada and more than 110 other countries have joined it. Four of its main goals:

  • Prohibit space appropriation. No country can claim the Moon, a planet or any part of space as its own territory.
  • Promote peaceful exploration. Nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass destruction are banned from orbit, and military bases are banned on the Moon and planets.
  • Benefit humankind. Space is open to all countries, and exploration should benefit every country.
  • Reduce contamination. Countries must avoid harmful contamination of other worlds, and of Earth by anything brought back.

Later treaties added rules for rescuing astronauts (1968), paying for damage caused by space objects (1972), and registering every object launched (1975). Countries are responsible for what their companies do in space too.

Lesson 9 · The gaps
SP1.K5Understanding

What space law does not cover yet

The main space treaties were written in the 1960s and 1970s, when only a few governments could reach space. Today thousands of satellites launch each year, and private companies run rockets, tourist flights and plans to mine the Moon.

Many of these new activities are not regulated by international space law, or the rules are unclear. Some countries have made their own national laws, and some have signed voluntary agreements such as the Artemis Accords (2020), which Canada signed. Voluntary agreements are not binding treaties, and not every country agrees with them.

This is where the understanding for this lab comes in. As space exploration grows, people need new rules to keep it safe, sustainable and equitable. Equitable means fair, so countries that cannot yet afford rockets still get a share of the benefits and of the sky.

Many cultures, including First Nations, Métis and Inuit peoples, have long relationships with the night sky. Growing numbers of bright satellites change the sky that everyone shares, and no international rule limits that yet.

Investigation 2 · SP1.S1

Signal delay calculator

Radio signals travel at the speed of light. Use the formula to find how long a message takes. You do not need to memorize the formula.

t = d ÷ c    t = time (s), d = distance (km), c = speed of light ≈ 300 000 km/s
Worked example

Question: The Moon is about 384 000 km from Earth. How long does a radio message take to reach a rover there?

t = d ÷ c

t = 384 000 km ÷ 300 000 km/s

t = 1.28 s

Answer: about 1.3 seconds one way. A driver on Earth could steer a Moon rover with only a short delay.

Teacher notes

  • SP1.K2: Canadian milestones are taught with dates as known in 2026 (Alouette 1, 1962; Anik A1, 1972; Canadarm, 1981; Marc Garneau, 1984; Roberta Bondar, 1992; RADARSAT-1, 1995; Canadarm2, 2001; Dextre, 2008; Chris Hadfield as ISS commander, 2013; RADARSAT Constellation Mission, 2019; Webb's Fine Guidance Sensor and NIRISS, 2021; Jeremy Hansen on Artemis II, April 2026). Alberta links (Ex-Alta 1 CubeSat, 2017; neuroArm at the University of Calgary) should be verified before sharing. "One of the first countries to design and build its own satellite" is used instead of the common "third country" claim, which depends on how it is counted.
  • SP1.K3: debris figures come from the ESA Space Environment Report 2025 (about 40 000 tracked objects, an estimated 1.2 million larger than 1 cm). The ISS cost (more than 100 billion US dollars) and retirement around 2030 are widely reported estimates. Health rates in Investigation 5 are rounded (bone loss about 1 to 1.5 percent per month in hip and spine without countermeasures; ISS radiation about 100 times the ground dose) and the exercise effect is simplified; please check before sharing.
  • SP1.K4: taught more precisely than the draft list. "Prohibit space appropriation" is taught as no national claim of sovereignty (Outer Space Treaty, Article II); whether companies can own resources they extract is taught as unsettled (K5). "Promote peaceful exploration" is taught as the treaty actually states it: weapons of mass destruction are banned from orbit and military bases from the Moon and other bodies; other military uses of space are not banned. "Reduce contamination" is taught as avoiding harmful contamination of other worlds and of Earth (Article IX).
  • SP1.K5: the list of unregulated or unclear areas (resource ownership, traffic management, debris cleanup, satellite brightness, tourism passenger safety, conventional weapons and anti-satellite tests, settlements, heritage sites) reflects 2026 and may change. The Artemis Accords are taught as a voluntary set of principles that Canada signed in 2020.
  • Night sky note: First Nations, Métis and Inuit relationships with the night sky are mentioned only in general terms in Lesson 9. Please review the wording, and consider inviting a Knowledge Keeper if you want to explore sky knowledge further.
  • Practice data: rocket masses, the debris simulator, the health planner and the mission budgets are made up or rounded for practice and are labelled on the page.
