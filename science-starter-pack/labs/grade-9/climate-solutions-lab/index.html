Science 9 · Environmental Capstone · SL3

Climate Solutions Lab

Understanding: Complex environmental issues can be addressed by environmental stewardship and sustainability. In this lab you will look at climate change and other big environmental issues, the data that helps people make decisions, and the choices that farmers, governments, industries and families can make.

Learn

Ten short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with what makes an environmental issue complex and how we measure it. Then look at climate change, farming, minerals and government actions. Finish with population and the footprint of digital technology.

Lesson 1 · Big, tangled problems
SL3.K1SL3.S1

Complex environmental issues

A complex environmental issue has many causes, affects many people and places, and has no single fix. Climate change is one. Others include plastic pollution, scarce resources such as water and minerals, food insecurity, and poor water and air quality.

Environmental stewardship means taking responsible care of the environment so it stays healthy. Sustainability means meeting our needs today without making it harder for future generations to meet theirs.

These issues are tangled together. A drought caused partly by a warming climate can make water scarce, which can lower crop harvests and make food insecurity worse.

Big idea: stewardship and sustainability efforts can help address climate change and the other complex issues at the same time.

Model: issue explorer

Lesson 2 · Measure before you decide
SL3.K2

Monitoring air and water quality

Monitoring means measuring something again and again over time. Air and water monitoring stations collect data on pollution levels. The data helps people make informed decisions, from a coach moving practice indoors to a city changing how it treats drinking water.

In Canada, the Air Quality Health Index (AQHI) turns air pollution data into a number, usually from 1 to 10. Higher numbers mean higher health risk. Wildfire smoke can push Edmonton's AQHI above 10.

Water quality is checked with measurements such as dissolved oxygen (oxygen fish can breathe), pH (how acidic or basic), turbidity (how cloudy) and nitrate (a nutrient from fertilizer and sewage).

Many First Nations and Métis communities in Alberta run their own land and water monitoring programs, often combining scientific measurements with long observation of the land.

Lesson 3 · Speeding up a natural process
SL3.K3

Humans accelerate climate change

Earth's climate has changed before, slowly, over thousands of years. Today it is changing much faster. Scientists agree that human activity is the main cause of the warming since the 1800s.

Burning coal, oil and natural gas releases carbon dioxide (CO₂), a greenhouse gas. Clearing forests and some farming practices add more greenhouse gases. These gases absorb energy that Earth's surface radiates, so more of them traps more energy and warms the planet.

CO₂ in the air is measured in parts per million (ppm). It was about 280 ppm before the industrial era and is now more than 420 ppm.

Big idea: human activity is speeding up climate change. That also means human choices can slow it down.

Lesson 4 · Swap and improve
SL3.K3SL3.S2

Slowing climate change

Climate change can be slowed in two main ways.

  • Replace or improve inefficient technologies. An inefficient device wastes energy. LED bulbs, high-efficiency furnaces, heat pumps and better insulation do the same job with less energy.
  • Transition to renewable and low-carbon energy. Renewable resources refill naturally: sunlight, wind, moving water and Earth's heat. Low-carbon resources release little CO₂ when making electricity. All renewables are low-carbon, and so is nuclear power, which is not renewable.

Alberta's electricity has changed quickly. The last coal power plants were converted to natural gas by 2024, and southern Alberta now has many wind and solar farms.

Lesson 5 · Farming for a changing climate
SL3.K4SL3.K5

Climate-smart agriculture

Climate-smart agriculture is farming that aims for three goals at once:

  • Productivity: grow more food efficiently, with less waste of water, fuel and fertilizer.
  • Resilience: cope with droughts, floods, heat and new pests as the climate changes.
  • Mitigation: lower greenhouse gas emissions, or store carbon, wherever possible.

Advanced technologies and innovations help. Tap each one in the model to see which goals it supports.

Carbon capture and storage is mostly used at large industrial sites. In Alberta, CO₂ captured at a fertilizer plant and other sites near Edmonton is piped away and stored deep underground. Making fertilizer is a big part of farming's emissions.

Lesson 6 · Carbon underfoot
SL3.K6

Healthy soils

Soil holds a huge amount of carbon. Plants take CO₂ from the air during photosynthesis. When roots, leaves and stems break down, some of that carbon becomes soil organic matter, the dark, crumbly part of soil.

Healthy soil stores carbon, holds water during droughts, and has the nutrients and living things that crops need. Healthy crops can be nutrient dense, which means rich in vitamins, minerals and protein for the food they provide.

Prairie farmers use practices that build soil: zero tillage (planting without ploughing), cover crops, crop rotations with peas or lentils that add nitrogen, leaving crop leftovers on the field, and careful grazing.

Lesson 7 · Minerals for clean energy
SL3.K7SL3.S4

Critical minerals in Alberta

Critical minerals are minerals a country needs for its economy and security, and whose supply could be at risk. Many are needed for clean energy technologies.

Lithium is used in rechargeable batteries for electric vehicles and for storing wind and solar power. Alberta has lithium dissolved in salty underground water called brine, in the same deep rock layers that oil companies have drilled for decades.

Uranium is the fuel for nuclear power plants, a low-carbon energy source. Northeastern Alberta includes part of the Athabasca Basin, one of the richest uranium regions in the world.

Alberta is developing new extraction methods, such as direct lithium extraction. Like any mining, these must be weighed against their effects on land, water and communities.

Model: mineral files

How direct lithium extraction works

  1. Pump brine up from a well about 2 to 3 km deep.
  2. Pass the brine over a material that grabs lithium and lets most other substances pass.
  3. Wash the lithium off and process it into lithium compounds for batteries.
  4. Pump the leftover brine back underground.
Lesson 8 · Rules, limits and rewards
SL3.K8SL3.K9SL3.S3

Government environmental actions

Governments can support sustainability in three main ways:

  • Regulations for resource extraction: rules for how mines and wells are approved, run and cleaned up afterwards.
  • Pollution limits for industry: the most of a pollutant a factory or plant may release into air or water.
  • Incentives: rewards, such as rebates or grants, for developing or using eco-friendly technologies and transportation.

An action works better when people support it. Governments build public support with educational campaigns that explain the reasons, or with monetary incentives that make the choice cheaper.

People disagree about which actions are best and how much they should cost. In this lab you look at how each type of action works, not which one is right.

Lesson 9 · More people, more demand
SL3.K10SL3.S5

Population and resources

The world population was about 2.5 billion in 1950 and is about 8.2 billion today. The United Nations projects about 9.7 billion by 2050.

More people need more water, food, energy and minerals. Total demand depends on two things:

total demand = number of people × use per person

If the population grows and use per person stays the same, total demand grows. Stewardship and sustainability efforts, such as saving water and wasting less food, lower the use per person. That is why they become more important as the population increases.

Model: demand meter

Lesson 10 · The cloud is on the ground
SL3.K11SL3.S6

AI, digital technology and the environment

Digital technology feels weightless, but it runs on real machines. "The cloud" is made of data centres: buildings full of computers that run apps, store photos and train and run AI models.

Environmental considerations include the electricity data centres use, the water some use for cooling, the minerals mined to build devices, and e-waste (thrown-out electronics).

Digital tools can also help the environment, for example AI that spots crop stress from drone images or smarter controls that save energy in buildings. Good decisions weigh both sides.

Investigation 2 · SL3.S2

Emissions calculator

Emissions from an activity can be estimated with one formula. Emission factors here are rounded practice values.

emissions (kg CO₂) = amount of activity × emission factor
Worked example A gasoline car drives 15 000 km in a year. Its emission factor is 0.2 kg CO₂ per km. emissions = 15 000 km × 0.2 kg/km = 3000 kg CO₂ An electric car charged on a practice grid has a factor of 0.08 kg/km: 15 000 × 0.08 = 1200 kg CO₂. Savings = 3000 − 1200 = 1800 kg CO₂ per year, a 60% cut.

Investigation 5 · SL3.S5

Demand and stewardship

Population growth pushes demand up. Stewardship can pull use per person down. Find the change in total demand.

new total ÷ old total = (population factor) × (use per person factor)
Worked example A town's population grows by 25%, so the population factor is 1.25. Water saving lowers use per person by 20%, so the use factor is 0.80. 1.25 × 0.80 = 1.00. Total water demand stays the same even though the town grew.

Use a negative number for a decrease.

Renewable and low-carbon are taught as different categories: nuclear is low-carbon but not renewable.
