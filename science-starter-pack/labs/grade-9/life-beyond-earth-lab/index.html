Science 9 · Space · SP2

Life Beyond Earth Lab

Understanding: Space exploration seeks to answer questions about life in the universe. Astrobiologists ask what life needs and look for places that could have it, in our solar system and around other stars. Bases in lava tubes on the Moon or Mars could one day help people explore further and stay longer.

Learn

Eight short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. First meet the science of astrobiology and what life needs. Then visit places that might have life, near and far. Finish with lava tubes and the bases people may build in them.

Lesson 1 · A science made of sciences
SP2.K1SP2.S1

What is astrobiology?

Astrobiology is the scientific study of life in the universe. It asks three big questions: How does life begin? Does life exist anywhere other than Earth? Where could it be, and how is it spread out across space?

Astrobiology is interdisciplinary. That means it joins many fields of science together. No single field can answer these questions alone.

  • Biologists study how living things survive, even in extreme places.
  • Chemists look for the molecules life uses.
  • Geologists read rocks for signs of past water and past life.
  • Astronomers and physicists find planets and study starlight.
  • Engineers and computer scientists build the spacecraft, rovers and software that do the searching.
Big idea: astrobiology explores space to find out whether life exists elsewhere, and if it does, where.

Lesson 2 · The checklist
SP2.K2

What does life need?

Every living thing on Earth needs four things. Astrobiologists use these needs as a checklist when they judge another world.

  • Liquid water, where the chemistry of life happens.
  • An energy source, such as sunlight or chemical energy from hot rocks.
  • Chemical building blocks, mainly carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulfur.
  • Stable conditions that last long enough for life to start and survive.

Extremophiles are living things that thrive in extreme places, such as boiling hot springs, acid lakes or rock deep underground. They show that life can survive in places once thought impossible. This gives scientists hope that life could survive on harsher worlds too.

Oxygen in the air and sunlight are not on the list. Many microbes on Earth live without either one.

Lesson 3 · Not too hot, not too cold
SP2.K2SP2.S2

The habitable zone

The habitable zone is the range of distances from a star where a planet with enough air could have liquid water on its surface. Closer in, water boils away. Farther out, it freezes.

A brighter star has a habitable zone farther out. A dim red dwarf has one tucked in close. Scientists estimate the middle of the zone with a simple rule, using the star's brightness compared with the Sun (its luminosity, L):

zone edges ≈ 0.95 × √L to 1.67 × √L   (in AU)

One astronomical unit (AU) is the distance from Earth to the Sun, about 150 million km.

Being in the zone is a starting point, not a promise. Mars sits near the outer edge, but its air is too thin to hold liquid water at the surface today. Moons such as Europa are far outside the zone but may hide oceans warmed from inside.

Lesson 4 · Close to home
SP2.K2SP2.S2

Places in our solar system that might hold life

Conditions on some celestial bodies (planets, moons and other natural objects in space) suggest that life may exist, or once existed, beyond Earth.

Mars once had rivers and lakes. Ice is still there today, under the ground and at the poles. Europa, a moon of Jupiter, and Enceladus, a moon of Saturn, have salty liquid oceans under their icy shells. Enceladus sprays water from its ocean into space. Titan, Saturn's largest moon, has lakes of liquid methane and likely a water ocean deep below.

No one has found life on any of them yet. Each one has some, but not all, of the checklist items scientists can confirm.

Model: tap a world

Lesson 5 · Other stars
SP2.K2SP2.S2

Exoplanets and the transit method

An exoplanet is a planet that orbits a star other than the Sun. Scientists had confirmed more than 6000 of them by 2025.

Most are found with the transit method. When a planet passes in front of its star, the star's light dims a tiny bit. A bigger planet blocks more light:

dip in brightness ≈ (planet radius ÷ star radius)²

When starlight passes through a planet's air, gases leave fingerprints in the light. Telescopes such as Webb read these fingerprints, a method called spectroscopy. A gas that could point to life is called a biosignature. Oxygen and methane found together would be a strong hint, because on Earth living things keep both in the air.

Canada built NIRISS, one of the instruments on the James Webb Space Telescope. It was designed partly to study the air of exoplanets.

Lesson 6 · How the search works
SP2.K1SP2.S1

Missions that look for life

Astrobiology moves forward one mission at a time. Rovers read rocks on Mars. Orbiters fly through plumes and map icy moons. Telescopes study the air of distant planets. Radio searches listen for signals from other civilizations.

Scientists are careful. A claim of life needs strong evidence, ruled-out explanations and checks by other teams. Several exciting claims, such as the 2020 report of the gas phosphine at Venus, are still debated.

Big idea: the search for life is slow, careful work. "We have not found it yet" is an honest scientific answer.

Lesson 7 · Natural shelters
SP2.K3

Lava tubes on the Moon and Mars

A lava tube is a tunnel made by flowing lava. The top of a lava river cools and hardens into a crust. Hot lava keeps flowing underneath, then drains away and leaves a hollow tube.

Spacecraft have photographed pits (holes) on the Moon and Mars where tube roofs have collapsed. In 2024, scientists used radar data to show that a pit on the Moon opens into a cave passage.

Conditions inside a lava tube could make it a good place for people and labs:

  • Several metres of rock overhead would block most harmful radiation from the Sun and space.
  • The rock roof would stop tiny micrometeorites.
  • The temperature would stay much steadier than on the surface.

Lava tubes on Earth, such as those in Hawaii and Iceland, are used to train astronauts and test equipment.

Lesson 8 · Why build out there?
SP2.K4SP2.S3

Labs and homes on other worlds

Research labs and places to live on the Moon, Mars or other bodies could help space exploration in three ways:

  • Reducing costs. Launching anything from Earth is expensive. Making water, oxygen and fuel from local ice, and building with local rock, means less has to be launched.
  • More technology beyond Earth. Workshops, 3D printers, spare parts and fuel depots would be close to where they are needed, instead of days or months away.
  • Longer research missions. People could stay for months, run long experiments, and use places such as the quiet far side of the Moon for telescopes.

The Moon's gravity is about one sixth of Earth's. A rocket leaving the Moon needs much less energy than one leaving Earth.

Bases also bring costs, risks to crews, and the duty not to spread Earth microbes to places that might have life of their own.

Teacher notes

  • SP2.K1: the draft defines astrobiology by detecting "the possible existence and distribution" of life. The lab also names the origin of life as one of the field's questions, since NASA and most sources include it. The fields named in Lesson 1 are examples, not a fixed list.
  • SP2.K2: "conditions indicate that life may exist" is taught carefully: conditions can make life possible, but no life has been found beyond Earth. The needs checklist (liquid water, energy, chemical building blocks, stable conditions) is a common simplification. The habitable zone rule (0.95√L to 1.67√L AU) is a rounded conservative estimate; published edges vary by model. Mission facts need a currency check: more than 6000 confirmed exoplanets (NASA Exoplanet Archive, 2025); phosphates in Enceladus plume grains (2023); methane and carbon dioxide at K2-18 b (Webb, 2023) with the 2025 dimethyl sulfide claim still disputed; Venus phosphine (2020) disputed; the 2025 report of a possible biosignature in a Jezero rock sample (Cheyava Falls, sample Sapphire Canyon) taught as possible, not proven; Europa Clipper (launched 2024, arrival 2030), JUICE (launched 2023, arrival 2031) and Dragonfly (planned launch 2028, arrival 2034).
  • SP2.K3: lava tubes on the Moon and Mars are inferred from pits and collapse chains; the 2024 radar study of the Mare Tranquillitatis pit is the first evidence of an accessible cave passage on the Moon. The tube temperature (about 17 °C in shaded parts of that pit, from a 2022 study) and surface range (about −170 °C to 120 °C on the Moon) are rounded estimates. Radiation protection is described in words, without dose numbers.
  • SP2.K4: the three draft benefits are taught as listed. Escape velocities (Earth about 11.2 km/s, Mars about 5.0 km/s, Moon about 2.4 km/s) are standard values. The shipping cost and water use in Investigation 5 are made-up practice values and are labelled. MOXIE (oxygen made from Mars air, 2021 to 2023) is named as a real test of making supplies on site. Planetary protection is mentioned as brief context.
  • Alberta example: the warm springs at Banff (home of the endangered Banff Springs snail and of microbial mats) are used as a local example of life in unusual water. They are warm rather than extreme, and the lab says so.
  • Practice data: the habitable zone and transit practice stars, the water budget, and two biosignature reports marked "practice" are made up or rounded.
