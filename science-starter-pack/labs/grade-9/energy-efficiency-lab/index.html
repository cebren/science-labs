Energy Efficiency Lab

Understanding: The conservation of energy is evident in the energy transfers of electrical devices. Measure power, count joules and kilowatt hours, follow every joule through a device, compare efficiency labels, work out what a device costs to run, and explore where Alberta’s electricity comes from.

Learn

Eight short lessons in order. Start with power and energy, then follow the law of conservation of energy through real devices. Finish with efficiency labels, the cost of running a device, and the sources of Alberta’s electricity.

Lesson 1 · Power
E2.K1

Power is how fast energy is transferred

Every electrical device transfers energy. Some do it slowly and some do it quickly. Power is the rate at which a device transfers energy. In other words, power tells you how many joules the device transfers each second.

Power is measured in watts (W). One watt means one joule of energy is transferred each second. A kilowatt (kW) is 1000 watts.

power = energy ÷ time    P = E ÷ t    1 W = 1 J/s

The power rating printed on a device, such as 1500 W on a kettle, tells you how fast it transfers energy when it runs normally.

Remember: a higher power rating does not mean a device uses more energy in total. It means the device transfers energy faster. Total energy also depends on time.

Lesson 2 · Energy from power and time
E2.K2E2.K3

Use the power rating to find the energy input

If you know how fast a device transfers energy and how long it runs, you can find the total energy input it needs. Multiply the power by the time.

energy = power × time    E = P × t

The units must match. Use one of these two pairs:

  • power in watts and time in seconds gives energy in joules (J)
  • power in kilowatts and time in hours gives energy in kilowatt hours (kWh)

A kilowatt hour is the energy a 1 kW device transfers in one hour. It is a unit of energy, not power. One kilowatt hour equals 3 600 000 J, or 3.6 MJ.

Worked example A 1500 W kettle runs for 3 min. t = 3 × 60 = 180 s E = 1500 W × 180 s = 270 000 J In kWh: 1.5 kW × 0.05 h = 0.075 kWh

Lesson 3 · Conservation of energy
E2.K4

Energy is never made or lost

The law of conservation of energy says that energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can only be transferred from one energy store to another.

You met energy stores in Science 8: chemical, kinetic, thermal, gravitational, elastic and nuclear. An electric current is not a store. It is a way of transferring energy from one store to another.

Think about a battery fan. The battery’s chemical store goes down. The kinetic store of the spinning blades goes up, and the thermal store of the motor and the air around it goes up. Sound is a transfer too, and it ends up warming the air.

Remember: add up every store at the start and every store at the end. The totals are always the same.

Model: the energy ledger

Slide the run time of a battery fan. The battery’s store shrinks while the other stores grow. Watch the total.

Made-up numbers for a toy fan that starts with 2000 J in its battery. Once the blades are spinning steadily, their kinetic store stays the same and the rest of the transfer heats the room.

Lesson 4 · Dissipation
E2.K5

Some energy always spreads out as heat

No device transfers all of its energy to the store we want. Some energy always dissipates. This means it spreads out into the surroundings by heating them, often through friction or the resistance of wires.

The dissipated energy is not destroyed. It is still there, in the thermal store of the room. It is just spread so thinly that it is very hard to use again. So the amount of useful energy goes down.

A Sankey diagram shows this. The width of each arrow stands for an amount of energy. The input arrow splits into a useful arrow and a wasted arrow. The arrows out always add up to the arrow in.

Remember: useful energy + wasted energy = total energy input.

Lesson 5 · Efficiency
E2.K6

Efficiency compares useful output to input

Energy efficiency tells you how much of the energy input a device turns into useful output. It is a ratio, usually written as a percentage.

efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input × 100%

You can use power in place of energy, as long as both numbers are in the same units: useful power out ÷ power in × 100%.

Because energy cannot be created, the useful output can never be more than the input. Because some energy always dissipates, real devices are always below 100% efficient.

Worked example An LED bulb takes in 900 J. It gives out 270 J of light. efficiency = 270 J ÷ 900 J × 100% = 30% wasted = 900 J − 270 J = 630 J (heats the room)

Lesson 6 · Energy ratings
E2.K7

EnerGuide and ENERGY STAR

When you shop for an appliance in Canada, two programs help you compare energy use.

The EnerGuide label is found on major appliances such as fridges, freezers, washers, dryers, dishwashers and stoves. It shows how much energy the model is expected to use in one year, in kilowatt hours, under standard test conditions. A scale shows where that model sits compared with similar models. The program is run by Natural Resources Canada.

The ENERGY STAR symbol marks models that meet strict efficiency standards. They are among the most efficient models in their category. The symbol does not show a number. It tells you the model passed the test.

EnerGuide ratings also exist for whole houses, which helps when people renovate or buy a home.

Remember: a lower EnerGuide number means less energy used each year, which means a lower bill and less demand on power plants.

Lesson 7 · Cost of operating
E2.K8

What a device costs to run

Electricity companies charge for energy, counted in kilowatt hours. To find the cost of running a device, multiply the energy it uses by the price of each kilowatt hour.

cost = energy used (kWh) × price per kWh

If you only know the power rating, find the energy first: E = P × t, with power in kW and time in hours.

Worked example A 1.2 kW space heater runs 5 h a day for 30 days. E = 1.2 kW × 150 h = 180 kWh cost = 180 kWh × $0.15/kWh = $27.00

In this lab the price is a made-up practice price of 15¢ per kWh. Real Alberta prices change from month to month, and real bills also add delivery and other charges.

Lesson 8 · Alberta’s electricity
E2.K9

Where Alberta’s electricity comes from

Electricity is not found in nature ready to use. Power plants transfer energy from a store, such as the chemical store of a fuel or the kinetic store of moving air, into an electric current.

In Alberta, most electricity comes from power plants that burn natural gas. Wind and solar generation have grown quickly, mostly in southern Alberta where it is windy and sunny. Biomass plants burn wood waste, often at pulp and sawmills. A small amount comes from hydro dams on mountain rivers.

Coal was Alberta’s main source for decades. The last coal units stopped burning coal in 2024, and several were converted to burn natural gas. Very little electricity comes from oil. Diesel generators still power a few remote places that are not connected to the grid.

Several First Nations and Métis communities in Alberta, including some in Treaty 6 territory, own or share in solar and wind projects.

Remember: every source follows the conservation of energy. A gas plant transfers chemical energy to thermal, then to the kinetic store of a turbine, then to the grid. A lot of energy dissipates along the way.

    Teacher notes

    • E2.K1 / E2.K2: power is taught as the rate of energy transfer (1 W = 1 J/s). The power rating is taught as the rate at normal operation; real devices may draw less (a fridge cycles on and off, a phone charger tapers), so E = P × t gives the energy input only while the device runs at its rated power.
    • E2.K4: energy stores follow the Science 8 list (chemical, kinetic, thermal, gravitational, elastic, nuclear). Electric current, light and sound are taught as ways of transferring energy rather than stores, so light and sound are shown ending up in the thermal store of the surroundings.
    • E2.K5: dissipation is taught as energy spreading into the thermal store of the surroundings; the energy is conserved but less useful. Sankey splits are rounded typical values for practice (incandescent about 5% light, LED about 30% light, kettle about 90%, fan motor about 70%, phone charger about 80%); real values vary by model.
    • E2.K6: the draft says efficiency compares “the input from the energy store to the output of the device”. The lab teaches the precise ratio: useful energy (or power) output ÷ total energy (or power) input × 100%, and stresses that efficiency is always below 100%.
    • E2.K7: EnerGuide is described as the Natural Resources Canada label showing estimated annual kWh with a comparison scale, and ENERGY STAR as a symbol for top-performing models. Labels in the lab are simplified practice examples, not copies of the official designs. Please check the current list of labelled appliances.
    • E2.K8: the draft says “energy rating in kilowatt hours and the price of power per kilowatt hour”. The lab teaches cost = energy used (kWh) × price per kWh, since the kWh is a unit of energy, not power. The 15¢/kWh practice price is made up; real bills add delivery, transmission and administration charges.
    • E2.K9: the draft lists oil, natural gas, coal, biomass, solar and wind. The lab teaches all six with up-to-date context: coal-fired generation ended in Alberta in 2024 (units retired or converted to gas), oil (diesel) plays only a very small role in remote off-grid places, and hydro is added as a small source. Source shares (gas about 70%, wind about 16%, solar about 7%, hydro about 2%, biomass and other about 5%) are rounded approximations that need a currency check against AESO data.
    • E2.K9: First Nations and Métis ownership of solar and wind projects is described generally, with no community named. Please review the wording and consider a local example or community partner.
    • Data: appliance power ratings are typical rounded values. The household audit, fridge labels, fan ledger, grid day profiles and all practice problems are made up for practice.
