Chemical Change Lab

Understanding: Chemical reactions of pure substances can be determined by observable and quantifiable changes. In a reaction, atoms regroup into new substances. You can see the evidence, measure the mass, write the equation and explain it with electrons.

Learn

Nine short lessons in order. Watch atoms regroup, spot the evidence of a reaction, and tell chemical change from physical change. Weigh a reaction to see that mass is conserved. Then use electrons to explain why some substances react, write equations, speed reactions up, and work safely.

Lesson 1 · What a reaction is
M2.K1M2.S6

Atoms regroup into new substances

A chemical reaction is a process where the atoms in the starting substances break apart from their partners and join up in new ways. The starting substances are the reactants. The new substances are the products.

No atoms appear or disappear. The same atoms are simply rearranged. That is why the products have different properties. Hydrogen and oxygen are both gases that can feed a fire. When they react, their atoms regroup into water, which puts fires out.

When natural gas burns in an Alberta furnace, methane and oxygen regroup into carbon dioxide and water vapour, and energy is released to heat the house.

Remember: reactants → products. Same atoms, new partners, new substances.

Lesson 2 · Evidence of chemical change
M2.K3M2.S1

Clues that a reaction happened

You cannot see atoms regroup. Instead, chemists look for evidence that new substances formed:

  • Colour or odour change. A new colour or a new smell can mean a new substance.
  • Gas produced. Bubbles or fizzing when no heating is happening.
  • Precipitate formed. A precipitate is a solid that forms when two solutions are mixed. The liquid turns cloudy or a solid settles out.
  • Energy change. Light is given off, or the temperature goes up or down.

One clue is not proof. Boiling water makes bubbles, but the gas is still water. The more clues you see, the stronger your case for a chemical change.

Safety: never smell a chemical by sniffing it directly. Your teacher may show you how to waft a little of the air toward your nose.

Lesson 3 · Physical or chemical?
M2.K4M2.S2

Physical change makes no new substances

In a physical change, a substance changes its form, size or state, but its particles stay the same. Melting an ice cube is a physical change: the solid water becomes liquid water. Dissolving sugar in water is also physical: the sugar particles spread out among the water particles, but they are still sugar. Boil the water away and the sugar is left behind.

In a chemical change, the atoms regroup and new substances form. Toasting bread, rusting a nail and burning wood are chemical changes. They are hard or impossible to reverse.

The test question is always the same: were new substances made?

Remember: physical change = same particles, new form. Chemical change = new particles.

Lesson 4 · Conservation of mass
M2.K2M2.S3

Mass in equals mass out

Matter is not created or destroyed in a chemical reaction. Every atom in the reactants ends up in the products. So the mass of the reactants equals the mass of the products. This is the law of conservation of mass. Chemists such as Antoine Lavoisier in France showed it in the late 1700s by weighing reactions in sealed containers.

The catch is gases. If a gas escapes from an open container, the scale reading drops. The mass did not vanish. It floated away as gas. If a gas from the air joins the reaction, such as oxygen when steel wool burns, the scale reading goes up.

To test the law, use a closed system: a container that nothing can enter or leave.

mass of reactants = mass of products
mass of gas escaped = mass before − mass after (open container)

Lesson 5 · Valence electrons and stability
M2.K6M2.S7

Full outer shells are stable

Electrons sit in shells around the nucleus. For the first 20 elements, the first shell holds up to 2 electrons, and the next shells fill up to 8.

The electrons in the outermost shell are the valence electrons. They are the ones that take part in reactions. For main-group elements, the group number tells you how many there are: group 1 has 1, group 2 has 2, group 13 has 3, up to group 17 with 7.

Atoms are most stable when their valence shell is full, like the noble gases in group 18. Helium has 2 and the others have 8. Noble gases almost never react. Other atoms react by losing, gaining or sharing electrons until they have a full shell like a noble gas.

Remember: valence electrons = outer shell electrons. Full valence shell = stable.

Lesson 6 · Electrons and reactivity
M2.K5M2.S7

Why some elements react so easily

Reactivity is how easily a substance takes part in chemical reactions. It depends on the number of valence electrons.

Sodium has 1 valence electron. Losing that one electron gives it a full shell, so sodium reacts very easily. It is stored under oil. Chlorine has 7 valence electrons. Gaining one more gives it a full shell, so chlorine is also very reactive. When they meet, sodium gives its electron to chlorine. Both end up with full shells as the ions Na+ and Cl−.

Atoms that are one electron away from a full shell (groups 1 and 17) are the most reactive. Atoms in the middle of a row, such as carbon with 4, tend to share electrons instead. Noble gases already have a full shell, so they are the least reactive.

Size matters too. Down group 1, the outer electron is farther from the nucleus and easier to lose, so potassium reacts with water more violently than sodium.

Lesson 7 · Representing reactions
M2.K7M2.S5M2.S6

Word equations, chemical equations and models

A word equation names the reactants and products: hydrogen + oxygen → water. The arrow means “react to make”.

A chemical equation uses formulas: H2 + O2 → H2O. As written, this shows 2 oxygen atoms on the left and only 1 on the right. That breaks conservation of mass.

To fix it, we balance the equation by putting numbers called coefficients in front of formulas: 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O. Now each side has 4 hydrogen atoms and 2 oxygen atoms. Never change a subscript, because that would change the substance.

A model, such as balls and sticks or the particle pictures in this lab, shows the same thing as a picture you can count.

Lesson 8 · Reaction rate
M2.K8

Collisions make reactions happen

Particles can only react when they collide. Not every collision works. The particles must hit hard enough to break their old bonds.

The rate of a reaction is how fast reactants turn into products. Anything that makes useful collisions happen more often speeds it up:

  • Higher temperature. Particles move faster, so they collide more often and with more energy.
  • Higher concentration. More reactant particles crowded into the same space bump into each other more often.

That is why food lasts longer in a fridge, and why a fizzing tablet works faster in hot water. Breaking a solid into smaller pieces (more surface area) also speeds reactions up.

Lesson 9 · Working safely with chemicals
M2.K9M2.S4

WHMIS and lab safety

WHMIS, the Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System, is Canada’s system for labelling hazardous products at work and at school. A WHMIS label shows pictograms in red diamonds, a signal word (“Danger” or “Warning”) and safety steps. The safety data sheet (SDS) gives full details, including first aid.

Other safety protocols matter just as much:

  • Read the procedure and the labels before you start.
  • Wear safety goggles. Tie back long hair and loose clothing.
  • Never taste anything, and waft instead of sniffing.
  • Use small amounts. Never return extra chemical to the stock bottle.
  • Report spills, breaks and injuries to your teacher right away.
  • Dispose of chemicals the way your teacher says, and wash your hands.

Investigation 2 · Conservation of mass
M2.S3M2.K2

Weigh before and after

Run the baking soda and vinegar reaction in an open flask and in a plastic bottle sealed with a cap. Record the masses. Then solve the practice problems.

Worked example

An open flask with vinegar and baking soda has a mass of 182.40 g before mixing and 180.75 g after the fizzing stops. How much gas escaped?

gas escaped = mass before − mass after

= 182.40 g − 180.75 g = 1.65 g

In a sealed flask the scale would still read 182.40 g, because the gas stays inside.

Investigation 3 · Equation balancer

Balance the atoms

Change the coefficients with the + and − buttons until every element has the same number of atoms on both sides. Use the smallest whole numbers.

Worked example: CH4 + O2 → CO2 + H2O

1. Carbon: 1 on each side. Leave it.

2. Hydrogen: 4 on the left, 2 on the right. Put a 2 in front of H2O.

3. Oxygen: now 2 + 2 = 4 on the right. Put a 2 in front of O2.

Investigation 4 · Electron predictor
M2.S7M2.K5M2.K6

Predict what an atom will do

Use the group number to find the valence electrons. Then predict whether the atom will lose, gain or share electrons, or stay unreactive.

Worked example: magnesium

Magnesium is in group 2, so it has 2 valence electrons (shells 2, 8, 2).

Losing 2 electrons is easier than gaining 6. It loses them and becomes Mg2+, with the full shells of neon.

Prediction: loses electrons. Magnesium is a reactive metal. A ribbon burns with a very bright white light.

Hydrogen (1 valence electron) can lose or share. In this lab it counts as sharing when it bonds with non-metals.

Teacher notes

  • M2.K2: conservation of mass is taught for a closed system. The lab shows that an open container seems to lose mass when gas escapes (baking soda and vinegar) and to gain mass when oxygen from the air joins (burning steel wool). Lavoisier is named once as context, without dates beyond “late 1700s”.
  • M2.K3: evidence is taught as suggesting, not proving, a chemical change. Boiling water is used as the trap case (gas, but no new substance). Odour is paired with a wafting reminder.
  • M2.K4: dissolving sugar is taught as physical because the sugar can be recovered by evaporation. Students are not asked about borderline cases such as dissolving salts that warm or cool the water.
  • M2.K5: the draft says reactivity “depends on the number of electrons in the valence shell”. The lab teaches this as the main factor for elements, and adds once that atom size also matters (potassium is more reactive than sodium with the same 1 valence electron). Reactivity of compounds is not taught.
  • M2.K6: “full” valence shell is taught as the noble gas arrangement: 2 in the first shell and 8 in the next shells, using a 2, 8, 8, 2 model for elements 1 to 20. Hydrogen is treated as sharing with non-metals; beryllium and boron are left out of the predictor because they usually share.
  • M2.K7 / M2.S5: the draft does not say whether balancing is expected. The lab teaches word equations, chemical equations and simple balancing with coefficients by atom counting, since balancing shows conservation of mass. State symbols are not used. Sodium chloride is drawn as Na and Cl pairs for counting only; it is really a lattice of ions (see M1).
  • M2.K8: temperature is taught as raising both how often and how hard particles collide. Surface area is mentioned once as context; catalysts are not taught. The collision box is a simplified model with made-up energy thresholds.
  • M2.K9 / M2.S4: safety rules are general. Pictograms are simplified drawings for learning. Example hazards (hydrochloric acid stock solution, magnesium ribbon) should be checked against the SDSs and procedures in your own lab.
  • M2.S1 / M2.S3: the Lab Bench trials and mass readings are simulated practice data. They support, but do not replace, the hands-on reactions the skills call for. Reactions shown (copper(II) sulfate with steel wool, magnesium with dilute hydrochloric acid, calcium chloride with baking soda solution) are common school demonstrations; confirm concentrations and disposal with your division’s guidelines.
  • Context: Alberta examples (natural gas furnaces, road salt and rusting) are general. The statements do not name First Nations, Métis, Inuit or Francophone contributions, so none are added.
