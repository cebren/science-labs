When two explanations both fit

SL1.K1

A phenomenon is something in nature we can observe, like a rainbow or a mass extinction. Scientists try to explain it. Sometimes two different explanations both fit the evidence we have so far.

About 66 million years ago, the non-bird dinosaurs died out. Two explanations were debated for decades: a giant asteroid impact, or huge volcanic eruptions in India called the Deccan Traps. Both could, in theory, change the climate enough to cause mass extinction.

When more than one explanation seems valid, arguing harder does not settle it. Scientists need new evidence, ideally evidence that one explanation predicts and the other does not.

The boundary layer of clay from that moment in Earth's history can be seen in Alberta's badlands, in the Red Deer River valley near Drumheller. The Royal Tyrrell Museum studies the dinosaurs found below it.

What makes a theory accepted

SL1.K2

A scientific theory is a well-tested explanation of why or how a phenomenon happens. It is much more than a guess. When scientists compare explanations, the accepted theory is the one that is:

  • Most supported: it agrees with the most evidence from many tests, repeated by different people.
  • Most comprehensive: it explains the widest range of observations, and it makes predictions that turn out true.
  • Simplest: it needs the fewest extra assumptions or special exceptions.

Evidence comes first. Simplicity is used to choose between explanations that fit the evidence equally well.

Peer review: experts check the work

SL1.K3

Peer review means other experts in the same field check a scientist's work before it is published in a scientific journal. Reviewers look closely at two things:

  • Investigation techniques: Was the experiment controlled? Was only one variable changed? Were there enough trials and a big enough sample? Were measurements careful?
  • Analysis and interpretation of data: Are the calculations correct? Do the graphs show the data fairly? Does the conclusion match what the data actually shows?

Peer review is how scientists check whether work is valid, and the evidence behind every accepted theory has been through it many times. It is a strong filter, but it is not perfect. Reviewers can miss mistakes or reject a good idea. That is why other scientists also repeat important experiments.

In 1963, Canadian geophysicist Lawrence Morley sent a paper explaining magnetic stripes on the seafloor to two journals. Both rejected it. Similar work by Fred Vine and Drummond Matthews was published that year, and the idea became key evidence for plate tectonics.

New tools reveal new evidence

SL1.K4

Our senses can only detect so much. New technologies (like telescopes and DNA sequencers) and new methods (like a better way to date rocks) let scientists observe things no one could see before.

New evidence can support a theory, or it can contradict it. When it contradicts a theory, the theory or model may need to change.

Many of these tools came from people around the world. Canadians played a part too: Lawrence Morley and J. Tuzo Wilson helped turn seafloor measurements into the theory of plate tectonics.

Restricted, revised or replaced

SL1.K5

One odd result does not overturn a theory. It could be a mistake. But if substantial contradictory evidence builds up over time, from many tests by many people, scientists change the theory in one of three ways:

  • Restricted The theory still works, but only within a certain range of conditions. Outside that range, a different theory is needed.
  • Revised The theory keeps its main idea but is changed to include the new evidence.
  • Replaced The theory is dropped, and a new theory that explains the evidence better takes its place.

    Reviewer form

    Reviewer math: checking an average

    Reviewers recalculate key numbers. The mean (average) of repeated trials and the range (spread) are the most common.

    x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙ) ÷ n
    range = largest value − smallest value

    Worked example: trials of 14.2 s, 15.0 s and 14.5 s. Mean = (14.2 + 15.0 + 14.5) ÷ 3 = 43.7 ÷ 3 = 14.6 s (rounded). Range = 15.0 − 14.2 = 0.8 s. A small range means the trials agree well.
