Science 9 · Waves: Sound and Light · W1

Sound Waves Lab

Understanding: Sound waves transfer energy from the source of the sound wave and behave predictably. A drumbeat in the gym, thunder rolling over the prairie and a fish finder pinging the bottom of Lac La Biche all start with a vibration, need a medium to travel through, and follow the same rules.

Learn

Eight short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. First see how sound starts and travels. Then learn to read a sound-wave diagram, and finish with what changes the speed of sound.

Lesson 1 · Every sound starts with a vibration
W1.K1W1.S1

A vibrating source pushes on its neighbours

Touch your throat and hum. You can feel a buzz. Every sound starts with a vibrating source: something that moves quickly back and forth. Vocal cords, a guitar string, a drum skin and a speaker cone are all sources.

As the source moves forward, it squeezes the air particles beside it together. This crowded region is a compression. As the source moves back, it leaves a region where particles are spread out. This is a rarefaction. Each particle bumps its neighbour, so the pattern of compressions and rarefactions moves outward.

Sound is a mechanical wave: a wave that moves by making the particles of a material vibrate. The particles only move back and forth around their resting places. The energy moves on.

Remember: no vibration, no sound. Stop the source and the sound stops.

Model: a speaker and the air in front of it

Start the speaker. Watch the red particle. Does it travel away from the speaker, or does it just move back and forth?

Lesson 2 · Sound needs a medium
W1.K1W1.S2

No particles, no sound

A medium is the material a wave travels through. Sound can travel through gases like air, liquids like water, and solids like wood, steel, rock and even the bones of your skull.

In a classic experiment, a ringing bell sits inside a sealed glass jar. A pump removes the air. As the air leaves, the ringing gets quieter and quieter, even though you can still see the bell shaking. With almost no air left, almost no sound gets out.

Space is almost a vacuum: a place with almost no particles. That is why sound cannot travel between the Sun and Earth, and why astronauts on a spacewalk talk by radio.

Remember: sound needs particles to pass the vibration along. It can travel through solids, liquids and gases, but not through a vacuum.

Model: the bell jar

Drag the slider to pump air out of the jar. Then tap a medium below to learn how sound travels through it.

Lesson 3 · Out in every direction
W1.K1W1.S1

Sound spreads out in straight lines from the source

Picture a campfire with people sitting all around it. When one person tells a story, everyone hears it. Sound travels in all directions from the source, like growing rings around a pebble dropped in a pond, except the rings are really spheres.

In one material that is the same all the way through, sound travels outward from the source in straight lines. We can draw these paths as arrows called rays. That is why you can usually tell which direction a sound is coming from.

As the sound spreads, its energy is shared over a bigger and bigger area. That is why a sound gets quieter the farther away you are. Some energy is also absorbed by the air and by soft things like snow.

Sound can also bend around corners and through doorways, and it bends when air temperature changes with height. That is how you can hear someone in the hallway you cannot see. The straight-line rule describes how sound travels through one even material.

Model: sound rings around a source

Tap a listener (or focus one and press Enter) to see what they hear.

Lesson 4 · Reading a sound-wave diagram
W1.K2W1.S3

Turning squeezed air into a wavy line

Air particles are too small to see, so scientists draw sound as a wavy line called a sound-wave diagram. A microphone measures how squeezed the air is at each moment, and a computer draws it.

  • A crest (high point) shows a compression, where the air is squeezed most.
  • A trough (low point) shows a rarefaction, where the air is most spread out.
  • The middle line is normal air, with no sound passing.

The diagram is a graph. It is not a picture of the air moving up and down. In a sound wave, the particles move back and forth in the same direction the sound travels.

Two things to read: how many waves fit in the same time (frequency), and how tall the waves are (amplitude).

Model: particles above, diagram below

The top row shows air particles. The graph below shows the same sound. Play it to watch the crests line up with the compressions.

Lesson 5 · Frequency and pitch
W1.K2W1.S3

More vibrations each second, higher pitch

Frequency is the number of vibrations (or waves) each second. It is measured in hertz (Hz). 1 Hz means one vibration each second.

Pitch is how high or low a sound seems to us. The frequency of a sound wave determines its pitch. A high frequency sounds high, like a whistle. A low frequency sounds low, like a bass drum.

On a sound-wave diagram, a higher frequency means more waves squeezed into the same time, so the waves look closer together.

Most people can hear from about 20 Hz to about 20 000 Hz. Sounds above that are ultrasound, used by bats and by medical scanners. Sounds below that are infrasound, made by elephants and by some storms.

wavelength = speed ÷ frequency    λ = v ÷ f

You do not need to memorize this. It shows why high-pitched sounds have short waves in air.

Model: the pitch dial

Change the frequency. Count the waves in the window. Tap Play to hear a short tone (turn your volume down first).

Lesson 6 · Amplitude and volume
W1.K2W1.S3

Bigger squeezes, louder sound

Amplitude is how far the wave goes from the middle line to a crest. For sound, it shows how strongly the air is squeezed and stretched.

Volume, or loudness, is how loud or soft a sound seems. The amplitude of a sound wave determines its volume. Pluck a guitar string gently and you get a soft sound with a small amplitude. Pluck it hard and the string swings farther, making a louder sound with a bigger amplitude. The pitch stays the same.

A bigger amplitude also means the wave carries more energy. Loudness is often measured in decibels (dB). Hearing sounds louder than about 85 dB for a long time can damage your ears, so protect your hearing at concerts and with power tools.

Remember: frequency sets pitch (how close the waves are). Amplitude sets volume (how tall the waves are). You can change one without the other.

Model: the volume dial

Change the amplitude, or tap an example sound. The frequency stays fixed, so only the height of the waves changes.

Lesson 7 · The medium changes the speed
W1.K3W1.K4W1.S2W1.S4

Sound races through solids

The speed of sound depends on the type of medium. In air at room temperature, sound travels about 343 metres each second. In water it is about 1480 m/s, more than four times faster. In steel it is about 5900 m/s.

In most cases, sound travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids and slowest in gases. In solids and liquids, the particles are packed close together and pushed back hard when squeezed, so they pass the vibration on quickly. Materials like these are called stiff.

This is why the draft curriculum says sound travels faster through denser materials, such as water compared with air. That pattern holds for most everyday materials. But density alone is not the reason. Lead is denser than steel, yet sound is slower in lead. Helium is less dense than air, yet sound is faster in helium. What matters most is how stiff the material is.

speed = distance ÷ time    v = d ÷ t

Lesson 8 · Temperature changes the speed
W1.K3W1.K4W1.S4

Warm air carries sound faster

Temperature measures how fast particles are moving. In warm air, the particles move faster, so they bump into their neighbours sooner and pass the sound along more quickly.

In air, the speed of sound goes up by about 0.6 m/s for every 1 °C. At 0 °C it is about 331 m/s. On a hot +30 °C July day in Edmonton it is about 349 m/s. On a −30 °C January morning it drops to about 313 m/s.

v ≈ 331 + 0.6 × T    (v in m/s, T in °C, for air)

This formula is a tool for air only. You do not need to memorize it. In water, sound also travels a little faster when the water is warmer, in most conditions.

Model: the Alberta thermometer

Change the air temperature or tap a day. Watch the particles and the speed.

Lab Bench

Four investigations. Compare sound-wave diagrams, then use the speed of sound to solve real scenarios. Every investigation has a worked example and new practice each time.

Investigation 1 · Compare two sounds
W1.S3W1.K2

Which is higher? Which is louder?

Two sound-wave diagrams are drawn on the same time scale. Decide which sound has the higher pitch and which is louder.

Worked example Sound A shows 6 waves in the window. Sound B shows 3 waves. A has the higher frequency, so A has the higher pitch. Sound A's crests are only half as tall as Sound B's. B has the bigger amplitude, so B is louder.

Investigation 2 · Thunder timer
W1.S4W1.K3

How far away is the lightning?

Light from a lightning flash reaches you almost instantly. The thunder takes longer. Count the seconds between flash and thunder, then use the speed of sound in air to find the distance.

v ≈ 331 + 0.6 × T    d = v × t
Worked example A storm near Red Deer on a 20 °C evening. Thunder arrives 6 s after the flash. v ≈ 331 + 0.6 × 20 = 343 m/s d = 343 m/s × 6 s = 2058 m, about 2.1 km Quick rule: about 3 seconds for every kilometre.

Practice values are made up. If you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck: go indoors.

Investigation 3 · Echo finder
W1.S4W1.S2

Use an echo to measure distance

An echo is sound that bounces off a surface and comes back. The sound travels there and back, so divide the total distance by 2.

d = v × t ÷ 2
Worked example A fish finder on an Alberta lake sends a click down through the water (about 1480 m/s). The echo returns after 0.02 s. Total path = 1480 m/s × 0.02 s = 29.6 m Depth = 29.6 m ÷ 2 = 14.8 m

Practice values are made up. Notice that the same echo time means a much bigger distance in water than in air.

Investigation 4 · Two-path race
W1.S4W1.S2W1.K4

Which path wins?

Sometimes one sound reaches you by two paths at once. Choose two media, a temperature for any air path, and a distance. The calculator finds each travel time and the difference.

Worked example A worker taps a steel pipe 500 m away on a 20 °C day. Through steel: t = 500 m ÷ 5900 m/s ≈ 0.085 s Through air: t = 500 m ÷ 343 m/s ≈ 1.46 s You hear the tap through the pipe about 1.4 s before you hear it through the air.

Never put your ear on a railway or LRT track to listen for a train. Tracks are dangerous places.

Teacher notes

  • W1.K1: “travel in straight lines” is taught as straight outward paths (rays) through one even medium. A short note says sound also bends around corners and through openings, and bends when air temperature changes with height, so students are not confused by hearing around a corner. “Requires a medium” is taught with the bell jar; solids, liquids and gases all carry sound and a vacuum does not.
  • W1.K2: pitch and volume are taught as what we hear, set by frequency (Hz) and amplitude. Loudness is also noted to fall with distance as energy spreads (Lesson 3). Decibel examples (about 30, 60, 90 and 110 dB) and the 85 dB hearing-safety guide are rounded context values. Hearing range is given as about 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz.
  • W1.K2/S3: the sound-wave diagram is taught as a graph of air pressure over time (crest = compression, trough = rarefaction), not a picture of air moving up and down. λ = v ÷ f is shown as context only.
  • W1.K4: the draft says sound travels faster “through denser materials”. The lab keeps the draft example (water faster than air) and the general pattern (solids, then liquids, then gases), but explains it with particles being close together and stiff. It adds two counterexamples: lead is denser than steel but slower, and helium is less dense than air but faster. Please review whether this precision suits the final wording.
  • W1.K3/K4: v ≈ 331 + 0.6 × T is a rounded tool for air, not for memorizing. Reference speeds are rounded: air 343 m/s at 20 °C, helium about 1000, water about 1480, lead about 2200 and steel about 5900 m/s (steel values range from about 5000 to 6000 depending on the form). Densities are rounded. Water is noted as faster when warmer “in most conditions”.
  • Context: Prairie thunderstorms, Lac La Biche fish finder and Edmonton temperatures are general examples. Thunder, echo and pipe problems use made-up practice data and are labelled that way. Safety notes cover lightning, hearing and train tracks.
