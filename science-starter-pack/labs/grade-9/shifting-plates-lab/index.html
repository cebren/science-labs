Science 9 · Earth Science: Plate Tectonics · ES1

Shifting Plates Lab

Understanding: The layers of Earth behave differently and contribute to geological processes that continually reshape Earth’s surface. In this lab you will cut Earth open, watch the mantle churn, put the continents back together, and see how plates pushing on each other built the Rocky Mountains west of Edmonton.

Learn

Eight short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start deep inside Earth, work up to the plates, then follow the evidence from Wegener’s idea to the theory of plate tectonics.

Lesson 1 · Inside Earth
ES1.K1ES1.S1

Crust, mantle and core

Nobody has ever drilled through the crust. Scientists learned what is inside Earth by studying earthquake waves. The waves speed up, slow down and bend as they pass through different materials, a bit like an X-ray of the planet.

Earth has three main layers:

  • The crust is the thin, rocky outer layer we live on. It is only about 7 km thick under the oceans and about 35 to 40 km thick under most continents.
  • The mantle is the thickest layer, about 2900 km deep. It is hot rock that is mostly solid, but it can flow very slowly over millions of years, like very stiff putty.
  • The core is the centre, made mostly of iron and nickel. The outer core is liquid metal. The inner core is solid, even though it is hotter, because the pressure there is enormous.

If Earth were an apple, the crust would be thinner than the skin.

Remember: crust (thin, solid rock), mantle (thick, mostly solid rock that flows slowly), outer core (liquid metal), inner core (solid metal).

Lesson 2 · Two ways to slice the top
ES1.K2ES1.K1

The lithosphere is broken into plates

You can describe the outer part of Earth by what it is made of or by how it behaves.

By what it is made of, there is crust on top of mantle rock. By how it behaves, there are two different zones:

  • The lithosphere is the stiff, solid outer shell. It includes all of the crust and the uppermost solid part of the mantle. It is about 100 km thick on average.
  • Under it is the asthenosphere, the upper part of the mantle that is hot enough to be soft. It is still mostly solid, but it can flow slowly.

The lithosphere is cracked into large pieces called tectonic plates. The plates fit together like a cracked eggshell and ride on the soft asthenosphere below.

Remember: a tectonic plate is a piece of lithosphere: crust plus the top layer of solid mantle.

Lesson 3 · What moves the plates
ES1.K3

Convection currents and gravity

Heat from deep inside Earth warms the bottom of the mantle. Warm rock is a little less dense, so it slowly rises. Near the top it cools, becomes denser and sinks again. This loop is a convection current. It is the same pattern you see in a pot of soup on a stove, but mantle rock moves only a few centimetres a year.

The plates sit on top of this moving mantle. Gravity also pulls on them in two ways:

  • Slab pull: where an old, cold, dense plate sinks into the mantle, its sinking edge pulls the rest of the plate along behind it. Scientists think this is the biggest force on most plates.
  • Ridge push: new plate is hot and sits high at an ocean ridge. As it cools it slides down and away from the ridge.
Remember: plates move on the upper mantle because of convection currents and gravity (slab pull and ridge push).

Lesson 4 · A puzzle of continents
ES1.K4ES1.K5

Wegener and continental drift

In 1912 the German scientist Alfred Wegener proposed that all the continents were once joined in one huge land mass. He called it Pangaea, which means “all lands”. About 200 million years ago Pangaea began to break apart, and the pieces slowly drifted to where they are today. This idea is called continental drift.

Wegener gathered evidence:

  • Shapes of the continents. The east coast of South America fits into the west coast of Africa like puzzle pieces. The fit is even better along the edges of the continental shelves under the sea.
  • Matching fossils. Fossils of the same land plants and animals are found on continents now separated by oceans. Mesosaurus, a small freshwater reptile, could not have swum across an ocean, yet its fossils are in both Brazil and southern Africa.
  • He also found matching rock layers, matching mountain belts, and signs of ancient glaciers in places that are warm today.

Most scientists rejected the idea for about 40 years. Wegener could not explain what force could push whole continents through solid ocean floor.

Remember: continental drift says the continents were once one land mass that broke and drifted apart over millions of years. The evidence: continent shapes and matching fossils.

Lesson 5 · From an idea to a theory
ES1.K6ES1.S2

The theory of plate tectonics

In the 1950s and 1960s, new tools let scientists map the ocean floor and measure its rocks. Researchers from many countries found evidence Wegener never had. Their work built on continental drift and became the theory of plate tectonics.

Plate tectonics says Earth’s lithosphere is broken into seven major plates and many smaller ones. The plates move over the upper mantle, driven by heat-related convection and by gravity. Continents do not plough through the ocean floor. They ride along as passengers on moving plates.

A scientific theory is a well-tested explanation backed by many lines of evidence. Plate tectonics explains far more than continental drift: it explains why the continents moved and where earthquakes, volcanoes and mountains form.

Remember: continental drift described that continents moved. Plate tectonics explains how and why.

    Lesson 6 · Two kinds of plate
    ES1.K7

    Oceanic and continental

    Plates are made of two kinds of lithosphere.

    • Oceanic lithosphere lies under the oceans. Its crust is dark basalt rock about 7 km thick. It is thinner and denser.
    • Continental lithosphere carries the continents. Its crust is lighter rock like granite, about 35 to 40 km thick, and up to 70 km under big mountains. It is thicker and less dense.

    Both kinds float on the denser mantle below, a bit like wood on water. The less dense continental crust floats higher, which is why continents stand above the sea.

    When two plates push together, the denser oceanic one sinks under the other. This sinking is called subduction. Because oceanic plates keep getting recycled, the oldest ocean floor is only about 200 million years old. Continental rock does not sink, so it can be very old. Some of the oldest known rock on Earth, about 4 billion years old, is in the Northwest Territories.

    Many large plates carry both kinds. The North American Plate holds the continent and also the western half of the Atlantic Ocean floor. We often call a plate “oceanic” or “continental” by the kind of lithosphere at its leading edge.

    Lesson 7 · The evidence
    ES1.K8ES1.S3

    Four clues that plates move

    Seafloor spreading. A chain of underwater mountains runs down the middle of the Atlantic. Molten rock rises there, cools and forms new ocean floor that moves away on both sides. Rock near the ridge is young. Rock gets older the farther it is from the ridge.

    Earth’s magnetic field flips direction every so often. New rock records the direction of the field as it cools. That makes matching magnetic stripes on both sides of the ridge, like a barcode printed twice.

    Mountain formation. Where plates push together, rock crumples and stacks into mountain ranges. Fossils of sea creatures are found near the tops of the Himalaya and the Rockies.

    Seismic activity. Earthquakes are not scattered at random. Most happen in narrow belts along plate edges, where plates grind past, pull apart or push together.

    Volcanic activity. Most volcanoes also line up along plate edges: at spreading ridges and where one plate sinks under another.

    Lesson 8 · Close to home
    ES1.K9ES1.S3

    How the Rocky Mountains formed

    For hundreds of millions of years, western Alberta lay under shallow seas. Layer after layer of sand, mud and shells built up and hardened into sedimentary rock: limestone, shale and sandstone.

    Then the edge of North America became a collision zone. An ocean plate to the west was sinking under the continent, and chains of volcanic islands rode in on it and crashed into the coast. This compression squeezed the land from the west, mostly between about 150 and 55 million years ago.

    The squeezing pushed the old sea-floor layers east. They broke along low-angle cracks called thrust faults and stacked up like a pile of rugs shoved across a floor. At Mount Yamnuska near Canmore, much older limestone sits right on top of younger rock along the McConnell Thrust.

    Since then, rivers and glaciers have carved the stacked slabs into the peaks and valleys you see from Highway 1. That is why you can find fossil sea shells high in Banff and Jasper.

    Remember: the Rockies formed when tectonic plates collided and compressed the edge of North America, folding and stacking rock layers.

    Lab Bench

    Investigation 1 · ES1.S1

    Build Earth to scale

    Choose the radius of your model. The calculator works out how thick each layer must be. To scale a layer, divide its real thickness by Earth’s radius (6371 km) and multiply by your model’s radius.

    model thickness = real thickness ÷ 6371 km × model radius
    LayerReal thicknessShare of radiusModel thickness
    Worked example A model Earth has a radius of 50 cm. How thick is the mantle? 2900 km ÷ 6371 km = 0.455 0.455 × 50 cm = 22.8 cm The continental crust would be 35 ÷ 6371 × 50 = 0.27 cm, less than 3 mm.

    Investigation 2 · ES1.S3

    How far do plates travel?

    Plates move about 2 to 10 cm a year, roughly as fast as your fingernails grow. That sounds tiny, but over millions of years it adds up to whole oceans.

    speed = distance ÷ time    v = d ÷ t    so   d = v × t
    Worked example The Atlantic Ocean widens about 2.5 cm a year. How far would it spread in 1 million years? d = v × t = 2.5 cm/yr × 1 000 000 yr = 2 500 000 cm 2 500 000 cm ÷ 100 000 cm per km = 25 km Over about 180 million years, that is roughly 4500 km: the width of an ocean.
