1 · CLAIMS AND EVIDENCE · SL2.K2

What makes a claim scientific?

A claim is a statement that something is true. Anyone can make one. In science, a claim is accepted only when it fits the available evidence, and only when that evidence was collected and treated fairly.

A scientific theory is a well-tested explanation of how part of the natural world works. It is supported by many lines of evidence from many independent investigations. Cell theory, germ theory and plate tectonics are examples. In everyday talk "theory" can mean a guess, but in science a theory is one of the strongest kinds of understanding we have.

An unscientific claim may sound scientific, with lab coats, big words or graphs, but it does not match the evidence, or it depends on biased treatment of data such as picking only the results that agree. The scientific community does not accept these claims.

Scientific theories can still change. When strong new evidence appears, scientists test it and revise the explanation. Unscientific claims usually stay the same no matter what the evidence shows.

2 · SURVIVORSHIP BIAS · SL2.K1

Counting only the winners

Survivorship bias happens when we pay attention to successful outcomes because they are easy to see, and overlook the failures because they have disappeared from view.

During World War II, mathematician Abraham Wald studied damage on aircraft that came back from missions. Many returning planes had bullet holes in the wings and body. The obvious idea was to add armour there. Wald pointed out the problem: the planes that were hit in the engines mostly did not come back, so their damage was never counted. The places with few holes on returning planes were the most dangerous places to be hit.

Everyday examples: "Old houses were built better," when the weak old houses were torn down long ago. "That famous player quit school and got rich," while thousands who did the same are never interviewed.

3 · SELECTION BIAS · SL2.K4

Who got picked?

Scientists usually cannot measure a whole population, so they study a sample. The sample is useful only if it is representative, meaning it is like the whole population in the ways that matter.

Selection bias happens when the way participants or data are chosen makes the sample different from the population. A common cause is choosing people or data that are not random: asking only your friends, only volunteers, or only people standing in one place.

In a random sample, every member of the population has an equal chance of being picked. Random samples still vary a little by chance, but they do not lean one way on purpose or by accident.

4 · MISLEADING GRAPHS · SL2.K3

Data dressed up to fool you

Real data can be shown in ways that seem to support an unscientific claim. Two common tricks:

  • An inappropriate scale. A bar graph whose axis starts high, not at zero, makes small differences look huge. This is called a truncated axis.
  • Selective reporting. Showing only the data that agrees, such as a short time window, a few chosen sites, or only the trials that worked. This is selection bias in how results are reported, often called cherry-picking.

To compare bars fairly, compare the actual values. For bar heights the honest comparison is the ratio of the values:

actual ratio = B ÷ A

On a graph whose axis starts at s, the bars you see only show the part above s:

apparent ratio = (B − s) ÷ (A − s)

Worked example: A = 62 %, B = 66 %, s = 60. Actual ratio = 66 ÷ 62 ≈ 1.06. Apparent ratio = (66 − 60) ÷ (62 − 60) = 6 ÷ 2 = 3. The graph makes B look 3 times bigger when it is only about 6 % bigger.

5 · CRITICAL THINKING · SL2.K5

Your claim-checking toolkit

Critical thinking means questioning a claim carefully and fairly before you accept or reject it. It is not the same as rejecting everything. It means asking for evidence and checking how the evidence was gathered.
