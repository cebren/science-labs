Science 9 · Waves: Sound and Light · W3

Mirrors and Lenses Lab

Understanding: Optical technology uses the behaviour and interaction of light waves. A camera, a pair of glasses, the curved safety mirror at a store corner and the reflective signs along an Alberta highway all work because light reflects off mirrors and bends through lenses in ways you can predict.

Learn

Eight short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with the ray model of light. Then see how mirrors reflect and lenses refract, how shape and distance change the image, and how real technologies put these ideas to work.

Safety: never look at the Sun through a lens, mirror, telescope or binoculars. Never use a magnifying glass to focus sunlight near skin, paper or dry grass. Never shine a laser at anyone's eyes.

Lesson 1 · The ray model
W3.K2W3.S3

Light travels in straight lines, so we draw rays

In one even material, such as air or glass, light travels in a straight line. We show the path of light with a ray: a straight line with an arrow that points the way the light is going.

A ray is a model. Real light spreads out as a wave in all directions, but a few rays are enough to predict where light will go. That makes rays the main tool for designing mirrors, lenses, cameras and glasses.

You can see light's straight path when a sunbeam shines through dusty air, or when a laser beam crosses a foggy room.

Remember: a ray shows the direction light travels. It stays straight until the light reaches a new surface or a new material.

Model: rays from different sources

Pick a source. Count how the rays spread.

Lesson 2 · Mirrors reflect
W3.K3W3.S3

The law of reflection

Reflection is light bouncing off a surface. A mirror is a very smooth, shiny surface that reflects almost all the light that hits it.

To describe reflection, draw the normal: a dashed line at 90° to the mirror where the ray hits. The angle of incidence is between the incoming ray and the normal. The angle of reflection is between the reflected ray and the normal.

Law of reflection: angle of incidence = angle of reflection
θi = θr

A rough surface, such as paper, also follows this law at every tiny bump. The bumps face many ways, so the light scatters. This is diffuse reflection, and it is why paper shows no image.

Model: a ray on a mirror

Lesson 3 · Images in a flat mirror
W3.K5W3.S4

Where is the image in a plane mirror?

A plane mirror is a flat mirror. Reflected rays reach your eye, and your brain traces them back in straight lines. They seem to come from a point behind the mirror. That point is the image.

No light really reaches the space behind the mirror, so this is a virtual image. You cannot catch it on a screen.

A plane mirror image is always upright, the same size as the object, and the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front. It is also reversed front to back, which is why writing looks backwards.

Try it: move the object. The image moves too, but its size and orientation never change in a flat mirror.

Model: object and image

Lesson 4 · Curved mirrors
W3.K4W3.S2W3.S3

The shape of a mirror changes where light goes

A concave mirror curves inward, like the inside of a spoon. Parallel rays reflect and meet at one point, the focal point (F). It is a converging mirror. Flashlights, car headlights and big telescopes use concave mirrors.

A convex mirror bulges outward, like the back of a spoon. Parallel rays reflect and spread apart, as if they came from a focal point behind the mirror. It is a diverging mirror. It shows a wide view, so it is used for car side mirrors and safety mirrors at store corners and parkade exits.

The focal length (f) is the distance from the mirror to F. A more strongly curved mirror has a shorter focal length.

Model: parallel rays meet a mirror

Lesson 5 · Lenses refract
W3.K3W3.K2W3.S3

Light bends when it changes material

Refraction is the bending of light as it passes at an angle from one material into another. It happens because light changes speed. Light is slower in water and slower still in glass.

When light slows down, it bends toward the normal. When it speeds up again, it bends away from the normal. A ray that hits straight on, along the normal, slows down but does not bend.

Refraction is why a pencil looks broken in a glass of water, and why a fish in Pigeon Lake looks closer to the surface than it really is.

Context only: n₁ × sin θ₁ = n₂ × sin θ₂ (Snell's law)
n tells how much a material slows light: air 1.00, water 1.33, glass about 1.5

You do not need to memorize these formulas. The model uses them for you.

Model: a ray crosses a boundary

Lesson 6 · Lens shapes
W3.K4W3.K3W3.S2

Convex and concave lenses

A lens is a curved piece of clear glass or plastic. Light refracts as it enters the lens and again as it leaves, so a lens can steer many rays at once.

A convex lens is thicker in the middle. It bends parallel rays inward to meet at a focal point on the far side. It is a converging lens. Magnifying glasses, cameras and your eye use convex lenses.

A concave lens is thinner in the middle. It spreads parallel rays apart, as if they came from a focal point on the near side. It is a diverging lens. Glasses for nearsighted people use concave lenses.

Watch the names: a concave mirror converges, but a concave lens diverges. A convex mirror diverges, but a convex lens converges.

Model: parallel rays through a lens

Lesson 7 · Distance changes the image
W3.K5W3.S4W3.S3

Move the object, change the image

For a convex lens or a concave mirror, where you put the object decides what image you get.

Far away (more than 2f): the image is real, upside down and smaller. A real image can be caught on a screen. This is how a camera and your eye work.

Between f and 2f: the image is real, upside down and larger. A movie projector works this way.

Closer than f: the image is virtual, upright and larger. This is a magnifying glass or a makeup mirror.

Three rays find the image: one parallel to the axis, one through the centre, and one through F. The image forms where the rays meet.

Model: ray diagram

Lesson 8 · Optical technology
W3.K1W3.S1

Technologies that steer light

Engineers and scientists use reflection and refraction to solve problems. The curriculum groups these technologies into three families.

Optics and imaging: tools that make images, such as cameras, telescopes, microscopes, glasses and projectors.

Medical applications: tools that help doctors see inside the body or treat it, such as endoscopes and laser eye surgery.

Security and sensors: tools that detect, measure or protect, such as barcode scanners, safety mirrors and reflective road signs.

People around the world have designed optical tools for thousands of years. Inuit snow goggles, carved with narrow slits, cut glare from sun on snow and ice. Telescopes at the TELUS World of Science observatory in Edmonton and the dark skies of Jasper let Albertans look deep into space.

Explorer: how does it use light?

Pick a technology. See its family and which behaviour of light it uses.

Lab Bench

Four investigations. Test five mirrors and lenses on an optical bench, practise the law of reflection, predict images with the lens equation and build a periscope. Each one has a worked example or a clear goal.

Investigation 1 · The optical bench
W3.S2W3.S4W3.K4W3.K5

How do shape and distance change the image?

Question: how do the shape of a mirror or lens and the object's distance change the image?

Every mirror and lens here has a focal length of 10 cm. Choose one, set the object distance and press Record. Test at least four distances for each shape: 5, 15, 20 and 30 cm work well.

Worked example Concave mirror, f = 10 cm, object at 30 cm. The object is beyond 2f (20 cm), so expect a real, inverted, smaller image. The bench shows the image at 15 cm in front of the mirror, half the object's height, upside down.

Look for patterns: which shapes can make a real image? Which shapes always make an upright, smaller image no matter where the object is?

Investigation 2 · Law of reflection
W3.S3W3.K3

Find the angles

Angles are always measured from the normal, not from the mirror. If you know the angle between the ray and the mirror surface, subtract it from 90°.

Worked example A ray hits a mirror at 25° to the mirror surface. Angle of incidence = 90° − 25° = 65° Angle of reflection = angle of incidence = 65°

Investigation 3 · Predict the image
W3.S4W3.K5

Use the lens equation

For a convex lens or a concave mirror, one equation links the focal length (f), the object distance (do) and the image distance (di). A second one gives the magnification (m): how many times taller the image is.

1/f = 1/do + 1/di
m = −di ÷ do

A positive di means a real image. A negative di means a virtual image. A negative m means the image is upside down.

Worked example A convex lens has f = 10 cm. The object is 15 cm away. 1/di = 1/10 − 1/15 = 3/30 − 2/30 = 1/30, so di = 30 cm (real) m = −30 ÷ 15 = −2, so the image is upside down and twice as tall

You do not need to memorize these equations. Use them as tools.

Investigation 4 · Build a periscope
W3.S1W3.S3W3.K1

See over a wall with two mirrors

A periscope uses two plane mirrors to let you see over a wall or around a corner. Submarine crews, people at parades and engineers checking inside machines use them.

Goal: tilt both mirrors so light from the flag reaches the eye.

Investigate this technology. When you have it working, answer these questions in your notes:

  1. What problem does a periscope solve, and who uses it?
  2. Which behaviour of light does it use: reflection, refraction or both?
  3. What shape are the mirrors, and what angle works?
  4. Is the image upright? Is it virtual or real?
  5. How could you improve it, for example to see farther or more brightly?

Teacher notes

  • W3.K2: “light travels in a straight line” is taught as straight travel through one even material. Lessons 2 and 5 show the path changing at a mirror or at a boundary between materials. The ray is named as a model of a wave.
  • W3.K3: “mirrors reflect light, whereas lenses refract light” is taught as each device's main job. Strictly, lenses also reflect a few percent of light at each surface (the reason for anti-glare coatings), and most household mirrors have a glass front that refracts light before the metal layer reflects it. Snell's law and refractive indices (water 1.33, glass about 1.5, diamond 2.42) appear as context only.
  • W3.K4: taught with concave and convex shapes for both mirrors and lenses, and the terms converging and diverging. The note that a concave mirror converges but a concave lens diverges is stressed because students often mix them up.
  • W3.K5: the statement says the image “can change in size or orientation” with object location. This is true for concave mirrors and convex lenses. For a plane mirror, size and orientation never change. For convex mirrors and concave lenses, the image is always virtual, upright and smaller, and only its size and position change. The lab teaches this distinction.
  • W3.S4: 1/f = 1/do + 1/di and m = −di ÷ do are given as tools, not for memorizing, using the real-is-positive sign convention for an ideal thin lens or small curved mirror. Ray diagrams in the models are drawn to this ideal; real lenses and mirrors blur slightly at the edges.
  • W3.K1: technology summaries (camera, eye, glasses, telescope, microscope, projector, endoscope, laser eye surgery, pulse oximeter, barcode scanner, fibre optics, safety mirror, retroreflective signs, LiDAR) are general. Endoscopes and fibre optics are described as guiding light by repeated reflection inside a glass fibre (total internal reflection), named as context.
  • Context and review: Inuit snow goggles are described only generally as a slit design that cuts glare; please review wording and consider a community partner. Alberta examples (TELUS World of Science observatory, Jasper dark sky preserve, highway signs, Pigeon Lake) are general. Bench values use an ideal focal length of 10 cm; practice problems are made up.
