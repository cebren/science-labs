Hazard Watch Lab

Understanding: Various geological hazards are associated with geological processes within Earth. In this lab you will tour the Ring of Fire, read the clues left by past disasters on Canada’s west coast and in the Alberta Rockies, try the tools scientists use to watch for danger, and learn how two different scales describe an earthquake.

Learn

Eight short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with the big picture around the Pacific Ocean, zoom in on Canada, look back at three famous events, then learn how scientists watch for hazards and measure earthquakes.

Lesson 1 · The big picture
ES2.K1ES2.S1

The Ring of Fire

A geological hazard is a natural event caused by processes in Earth that can harm people or property. Earthquakes, volcanoes, landslides and tsunamis are all geological hazards.

The Ring of Fire is a horseshoe-shaped belt around the edge of the Pacific Ocean. Most of the world’s earthquakes and many of its active volcanoes happen here. Scientists often say about 90 percent of earthquakes and about three quarters of active volcanoes are in this belt.

Why here? The Pacific Ocean floor is made of several plates. Along most of the ring, an ocean plate slides under another plate. This is called subduction. The sinking plate can get stuck and then slip, which makes earthquakes. Rock melts deep below and rises to feed volcanoes.

Big idea: hazards cluster where plates meet. The edges of the Pacific are busy plate boundaries.

Lesson 2 · Canada’s west coast
ES2.K1ES2.S1

Four hazards on one coast

British Columbia sits on the Ring of Fire. Off Vancouver Island, the small Juan de Fuca Plate slides under the North American Plate. This boundary is called the Cascadia Subduction Zone. Farther north, near Haida Gwaii, the Pacific Plate grinds sideways past North America.

These plate movements give the west coast four main hazards:

  • Earthquakes when stuck plates suddenly slip.
  • Tsunamis, which are huge ocean waves made when the sea floor moves in a big earthquake.
  • Volcanic activity from melted rock rising above the sinking plate. BC has volcanoes such as Mount Garibaldi and Mount Meager.
  • Landslides on steep mountain slopes, often set off by shaking or heavy rain.

Edmonton is far from a plate boundary, so our earthquake and volcano hazard is low. Alberta still has landslides, and small earthquakes do happen.

Lesson 3 · Reading the past
ES2.K2

Historical accounts as evidence

Big geological events can be hundreds of years apart. To understand them, scientists combine different kinds of evidence:

  • Oral histories passed down by First Nations, who have lived on this land for thousands of years.
  • Written records, such as old diaries, newspapers and government reports.
  • Physical evidence in rocks, soil, lava and tree rings.

When the stories and the rocks agree, scientists can be much more confident about what happened, when, and how big it was. Historical accounts help scientists estimate how often an event happens, which helps communities plan for the next one.

Oral histories belong to the communities that keep them. This lab describes them only in general terms. Learn more from the Nations themselves when you can.

    Lesson 4 · Alberta’s deadliest landslide
    ES2.K2ES2.K3

    The Frank Slide

    Early on April 29, 1903, a huge piece of Turtle Mountain broke away above the coal-mining town of Frank, in the Crowsnest Pass of southwestern Alberta. Millions of tonnes of limestone crashed down in less than two minutes and buried part of the town. Somewhere between about 70 and 90 people died. The exact number is not known, because records from the time are incomplete.

    Records from the time, and studies of the mountain since, point to several causes working together. No single cause explains it.

    Turtle Mountain is not finished moving. Today the Alberta Geological Survey watches it with sensors that measure tiny movements of the cracks near the top. If the movement speeds up, people below can be warned.

    Connect: a landslide hazard is not only on the coast. The Frank Slide is a historical account from our own province.

    Lesson 5 · Watching for warning signs
    ES2.K3ES2.S2

    Monitoring hazards

    To monitor a hazard means to measure it again and again so changes can be spotted early. Scientists use many tools:

    • A seismograph records ground shaking. A network of them can find where an earthquake started and how big it was.
    • Gas emission monitoring measures gases such as sulfur dioxide and carbon dioxide coming out of a volcano. A sudden change can mean magma is rising.
    • GPS stations and tiltmeters measure tiny changes in the shape of the ground, such as a volcano swelling.
    • Crack meters measure how fast cracks open on an unstable slope, like at Turtle Mountain.

    Monitoring cannot stop a hazard. It gives people information and, sometimes, time.

    Lesson 6 · Reducing the harm
    ES2.K3ES2.S2

    Warning, building and planning

    We cannot stop earthquakes, but we can minimize their impacts. Ways to do this include:

    • Early warning systems detect the first, faster earthquake waves and send an alert before the stronger shaking arrives. Canada began an earthquake early warning system in British Columbia in 2024. Tsunami warnings tell coastal people to move to high ground.
    • Emergency preparedness means knowing what to do: Drop, Cover and Hold On during shaking, keep an emergency kit, and practise drills.
    • Construction standards are rules in building codes. They require strong connections, cross bracing and other designs so buildings bend without collapsing.
    • Land-use planning keeps homes, schools and hospitals away from the most dangerous places, such as unstable slopes, tsunami zones and soft ground.

    Lesson 7 · How big was it?
    ES2.K4ES2.S3

    The Richter scale measures magnitude

    In 1935, Charles Richter and Beno Gutenberg in California created a scale to compare earthquakes using seismograph records. It gives one number, the magnitude, for the size of the earthquake at its source.

    The Richter scale is logarithmic. Each step up of one whole number means the waves on the seismograph are 10 times taller. It also means about 32 times more energy is released.

    amplitude ratio = 10(M₂ − M₁)
    energy ratio ≈ 32(M₂ − M₁)

    Today, scientists usually report large earthquakes with the moment magnitude scale. It works like the Richter scale but is more accurate for very big earthquakes. News reports often still say “Richter.”

    Lesson 8 · How strong did it feel?
    ES2.K4ES2.S3

    The Modified Mercalli Intensity scale

    The Modified Mercalli Intensity (MMI) scale describes the intensity of an earthquake: what people felt and how much damage happened at a certain place. It uses Roman numerals from I (not felt) to XII (total destruction). It began with Italian scientist Giuseppe Mercalli and was later modified by American scientists.

    One earthquake has one magnitude but many intensities. Intensity is usually highest near the epicentre and drops farther away. It also depends on the ground and the buildings.

    Compare: Richter magnitude = size of the earthquake at its source, from instruments. Mercalli intensity = effects at one place, from observations.

    Investigation 1 · ES2.S1

    Risk analyzer

    A hazard is not the same as a risk. Scientists and planners often think of risk as three things multiplied together:

    risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability
    • Hazard: how likely and how strong the event is in that place.
    • Exposure: how many people, homes and roads are in the way.
    • Vulnerability: how easily they are harmed. Good buildings, warnings and practice lower it.

    Each hazard score is rated 0 to 3 (made-up classroom ratings). Exposure is rated 1 to 3. Vulnerability goes from 3 (little preparation) to 1 (well prepared). Highest possible risk score is 27.

    Worked example Metro Vancouver earthquakes: hazard 3 × exposure 3 × vulnerability 2 = 18 out of 27. Edmonton earthquakes: hazard 1 × exposure 3 × vulnerability 2 = 6. Lots of people, but a low hazard, so a lower risk.

    Investigation 2 · ES2.K3 · ES2.S2

    Early warning timer

    An earthquake sends out two main kinds of waves. P waves are faster but cause little damage. S waves are slower and cause the strong shaking. Sensors near the epicentre detect the P waves, and computers send an alert. If the alert reaches you before the S waves, you get a few seconds to Drop, Cover and Hold On.

    arrival time = distance ÷ wave speed
    warning time = S wave arrival − alert time

    This simplified model uses a P wave speed of 6 km/s, an S wave speed of 3.5 km/s, and an alert sent 10 seconds after the earthquake starts. Real systems vary.

    Investigation 3 · ES2.K4 · ES2.S3

    Magnitude math

    Compare two earthquakes. Because the Richter scale is logarithmic, small differences in magnitude mean big differences in size.

    Worked example Magnitude 7 compared to magnitude 5. Difference = 7 − 5 = 2. Amplitude ratio = 10² = 100. The seismograph waves are 100 times taller. Energy ratio ≈ 32² ≈ 1000. About 1000 times more energy is released.

    Investigation 4 · ES2.K4 · ES2.S3

    Intensity survey

    After a made-up magnitude 6.2 earthquake, people in six places sent in felt reports. Use the MMI ladder in Learn, Lesson 8, to rate each report. Then look at the pattern.

    What the survey shows

    All six reports come from the same earthquake, so the Richter magnitude is the same number, 6.2, for everyone. The intensity changes from place to place. It is highest close to the epicentre and on soft ground, and lowest far away. That is why the two scales answer different questions.
