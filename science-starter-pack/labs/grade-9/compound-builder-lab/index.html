Compound Builder Lab

Understanding: Pure substances can be classified by their composition. Every pure substance is an element or a compound. Elements come as single atoms or as molecules. Compounds are ionic or molecular, and IUPAC rules give each one a name and a formula.

Learn

Ten short lessons in order. Sort pure substances into elements and compounds. Meet elements made of single atoms and of molecules. Build ions, then see how ions and shared electrons make compounds. Finish with IUPAC names, formulas and WHMIS safety symbols.

Lesson 1 · Two kinds of pure substance
M1.K1

Elements and compounds

In Science 7 you learned that a pure substance has only one kind of particle all the way through. Pure substances come in two groups.

An element is made of only one kind of atom. It cannot be broken down into simpler substances, even by a chemical change. Each element has a symbol on the periodic table, such as O for oxygen and Cu for copper.

A compound is made of two or more different elements that are chemically joined in a fixed ratio. Water is always two hydrogen atoms for every one oxygen atom. A compound can be broken apart only by a chemical change. For example, passing electricity through water splits it into hydrogen gas and oxygen gas.

A compound behaves very differently from its elements. Sodium is a soft metal that reacts with water. Chlorine is a poisonous green gas. Joined together, they make sodium chloride: table salt.

Remember: element = one kind of atom. Compound = two or more different elements joined in a fixed ratio.

Lesson 2 · How element particles look
M1.K2M1.S1M1.S2

Monatomic, diatomic and polyatomic elements

Every particle of an element contains only one kind of atom. But the particles do not all look the same.

Monatomic elements are made of single atoms that stay apart. The noble gases in the last column, such as helium, neon and radon (Rn), are monatomic. The draft curriculum spells this word monoatomic. Both spellings mean “one atom”.

Diatomic elements are made of molecules with two atoms of the same kind. Seven elements are diatomic: hydrogen (H2), nitrogen (N2), oxygen (O2), fluorine (F2), chlorine (Cl2), bromine (Br2) and iodine (I2). A memory trick: HOFBrINCl, said “hof-brinkle”.

Polyatomic elements are made of molecules with more than two atoms of the same kind. Sulfur forms rings of eight atoms (S8). White phosphorus forms P4. Ozone, high in the atmosphere, is O3.

Alberta link: gas plants that clean “sour” natural gas recover huge amounts of sulfur. It is stored in bright yellow blocks. Each tiny grain is made of S8 rings.

Metals and carbon are written with the symbol alone, like Cu or C. Their atoms join into one big connected network instead of small separate molecules.

Lesson 3 · Atoms acting as one unit
M1.K3M1.S3

Molecules, from tiny to huge

A molecule is a group of atoms joined together that acts as a single unit. The atoms in a molecule stay together as the substance moves, melts or boils.

A molecule can be an element or a compound. O2 is a molecule of the element oxygen, because both atoms are oxygen. CO2 is a molecule of a compound, because it has carbon and oxygen.

Molecules range hugely in size. An oxygen molecule has 2 atoms. A sugar molecule has 24. One DNA molecule in a human cell holds billions of atoms, yet it still acts as one unit.

Careful: not every substance is made of molecules. Ionic compounds like table salt are built from ions packed in a repeating pattern called a crystal lattice. Metals are networks of atoms.

Lesson 4 · Gaining and losing electrons
M1.K7M1.S2M1.S3

Ions: cations and anions

An atom has positive protons in its nucleus and negative electrons in shells around it. A neutral atom has the same number of each, so the charges cancel.

An ion is an atom that has gained or lost electrons, so it has a charge.

  • An atom that loses one or more electrons has more protons than electrons. It becomes a positive ion, called a cation. Metals form cations.
  • An atom that gains one or more electrons has more electrons than protons. It becomes a negative ion, called an anion. Non-metals form anions.

The number of protons never changes. It is what makes sodium sodium. Only electrons move.

Atoms tend to gain or lose just enough electrons to end up with a full outer shell, like the nearest noble gas. Sodium loses 1 electron to become Na+. Chlorine gains 1 to become Cl−.

Naming: a cation keeps the element name (sodium ion). An anion's name ends in -ide (chloride ion).

Memory trick: a cation has a “t” that looks like a plus sign (+). An anion is a negative ion.

Lesson 5 · Two kinds of compound
M1.K4M1.K5M1.K8M1.S2

Ionic or molecular?

Compounds come in two kinds. You can usually tell which kind from the elements inside.

An ionic compound forms when a metal joins a non-metal. The metal atoms lose electrons and become cations. The non-metal atoms gain those electrons and become anions. Opposite charges attract, so the ions pack together in a crystal lattice.

The ions always combine in a specific ratio that makes the total charge zero. The compound as a whole is neutral. In sodium chloride there is one Na+ for every Cl−.

A molecular compound forms when two or more different non-metals join. No atom gives up its electrons. Instead, the atoms share pairs of electrons, which holds them together as molecules. Water, carbon dioxide and methane (natural gas) are molecular compounds.

Hydrogen sits above the metals in the periodic table, but it is a non-metal. When two atoms of the same non-metal share electrons, as in H2, the result is a molecule of an element, not a compound.

Lesson 6 · Reading charges from the table
M1.K6M1.K5M1.S5

Using the periodic table to find the ratio

The periodic table tells you what charge each main-group ion has. Count the columns that matter:

  • Group 1 metals lose 1 electron: +1. Group 2 lose 2: +2. Aluminum in group 13 loses 3: +3.
  • Group 17 non-metals gain 1 electron: −1. Group 16 gain 2: −2. Group 15 gain 3: −3.

Then find the smallest number of each ion that makes the total charge zero.

Worked example: aluminum and oxygen 1. Charges from the table: Al3+ and O2−. 2. Smallest total both charges reach: 6. 3. Two Al3+ give +6. Three O2− give −6. Total: 0. 4. Formula: Al2O3, aluminum oxide.

Many transition metals, like iron and copper, can form more than one ion. Their names include a Roman numeral, such as iron(III) oxide for Fe2O3. This lab sticks to main-group metals with one charge.

Lesson 7 · The rules chemists share
M1.K9M1.S4

IUPAC names

Chemists around the world use the same naming rules, set by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC). One name means one substance, in any country.

Ionic compounds (metal + non-metal):

  1. Name the metal first, unchanged: magnesium.
  2. Name the non-metal second, with its ending changed to -ide: chlorine → chloride.
  3. Do not add prefixes. The charges already fix the ratio. MgCl2 is magnesium chloride.

Molecular compounds (two non-metals):

  1. Use a prefix to show how many atoms of each element.
  2. Leave off mono- on the first element: CO is carbon monoxide.
  3. The second element ends in -ide: N2O4 is dinitrogen tetraoxide.

IUPAC drops the extra vowel only in monoxide. Many books also write tetroxide and pentoxide, and you will see both. A few compounds keep everyday names that IUPAC accepts: water (H2O), ammonia (NH3) and methane (CH4).

Lesson 8 · Reading a chemical formula
M1.K10M1.S6

Symbols and subscripts

A chemical formula shows which elements are in a compound and how many atoms of each.

  • Each element is shown by its symbol. A symbol starts with a capital letter, sometimes followed by one lowercase letter: Co is cobalt, but CO is carbon and oxygen.
  • A small number written low after a symbol is a subscript. It tells how many atoms of that element there are.
  • If there is no subscript, there is one atom. IUPAC never writes a subscript of 1.

For an ionic compound, the formula shows the ratio of ions. For a molecular compound, it shows the atoms in one molecule.

Worked example: glucose, C6H12O6Carbon 6, hydrogen 12, oxygen 6. Total: 6 + 12 + 6 = 24 atoms in one molecule.

Lesson 9 · Reading hazard symbols
M1.K11M1.S7

WHMIS pictograms

Many pure substances are hazardous. Hydrochloric acid burns skin. Methanol is poisonous and catches fire easily. In Canadian workplaces, including school labs, hazards are shown with the Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System, or WHMIS.

A WHMIS label uses pictograms: black symbols on a white background inside a red diamond. Each pictogram stands for a group of hazards. A label also has a signal word (“Danger” for more severe hazards, “Warning” for less severe), hazard statements and safety steps. A safety data sheet (SDS) gives the full details.

WHMIS matches the Globally Harmonized System (GHS), so the same pictograms are used in many countries.

Products sold for home use, like bleach, carry consumer hazard symbols with a different border shape instead. Always read the label and follow your teacher's safety rules.

Lesson 10 · Putting it together
M1.S3M1.K1M1.K3

How atoms, ions, elements, molecules and compounds connect

These five words are easy to mix up. Each one names a different thing.

  • Atom: the smallest particle of an element.
  • Element: a pure substance made of one kind of atom.
  • Ion: an atom that has gained or lost electrons.
  • Molecule: atoms sharing electrons, acting as one unit.
  • Compound: two or more different elements chemically joined. Ionic compounds are made of ions. Molecular compounds are made of molecules.
Test yourself: O2 is a molecule but not a compound. NaCl is a compound but not a molecule. H2O is both.

Teacher notes

  • M1.K2: the draft spells “monoatomic”; the lab uses the more common “monatomic” and says both mean one atom. Metals and carbon are noted as networks written with the symbol alone, so students do not call copper “monatomic” by default. Students meet “polyatomic element” here; polyatomic ions (such as hydroxide) are not taught.
  • M1.K3: the draft lists O2, CO2 and DNA. The lab adds that ionic compounds and metals are not made of molecules (NaCl is a lattice, described by a formula unit). Ladder counts: glucose 24 atoms, human insulin 788 atoms (C257H383N65O77S6), DNA in one human chromosome “billions” (no exact figure given).
  • M1.K6: ratios are found from main-group ion charges by group (1, 2, 13 positive; 15, 16, 17 negative). Transition metals with more than one charge and Roman numeral names are mentioned once (iron(III) oxide) but not practised. Please confirm whether the final curriculum expects multivalent metals.
  • M1.K8: the draft says “two or more non-metal atoms”. The lab teaches two or more different non-metal elements, since H2 or O2 are molecules of elements, not compounds. Hydrogen is treated as a non-metal. Metalloids are shown on the table but not used in naming.
  • M1.K9: molecular names follow the 2005 IUPAC recommendation (no vowel dropped except “monoxide”, so “tetraoxide” and “pentaoxide”). The lab notes that “tetroxide” and “pentoxide” are common and are accepted in practice. Water, ammonia and methane are taught as accepted everyday names. Canadian spellings “aluminum” and “sulfur” are used; IUPAC writes “aluminium”.
  • M1.K10: formula order taught as metal first for ionic compounds and in the order of the name for molecular ones. Atom counts for ionic compounds are per formula unit.
  • M1.K11: the lab teaches the WHMIS 2015 (GHS-aligned) pictograms, signal words and the SDS. Pictograms are simplified drawings for learning and are labelled as such. The environment pictogram is noted as optional in Canada. Consumer products use different hazard symbols, mentioned once. WHMIS covers mixtures as well as pure substances. Examples (hydrochloric acid, methanol, propane, hydrogen peroxide) describe typical label hazards; check against the SDS for the products in your lab.
  • Context: Alberta examples (sulfur blocks at sour gas plants, natural gas as methane, ammonia for fertilizer, road salt) are general. The statements do not name First Nations, Métis, Inuit or Francophone contributions, so none are added. All Lab Bench practice is generated from real compounds.
