Science 9 · Computer Science · CS2

Innovation Workshop Lab

Understanding: Innovation stems from creativity when developing and implementing solutions. In this lab you will follow design processes from a need to a solution people use, look at what makes an idea innovative, improve a real design through testing, and weigh the good and bad impacts of new solutions.

Learn

Six short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Lessons 1 and 2 are about design processes, Lessons 3 and 4 are about the innovation mindset, and Lessons 5 and 6 are about responsible innovation.

Lesson 1 · Design processes

A design process is a loop, not a straight line

CS2.K1CS2.S1

A design process is a set of steps people use to turn a need, want or problem into a working solution. There are many versions. Most include the same big moves: define the problem, research it, come up with ideas, build a prototype, test it, and put the best version into use.

A prototype is an early model of a solution, built so you can test it. Testing shows what works and what does not, so designers go back and improve the design. That is why the steps form a loop.

Example in this lesson: a bus stop on a windy Edmonton street in January. Riders wait in the cold. How could the shelter be better?

Try it: tap each step on the loop to see what it looks like for the bus shelter.

The design loop

Lesson 2 · From idea to innovation

An idea becomes an innovation when people use it

CS2.K1

An innovation is a new or improved solution that has been implemented. Implemented means it is put into real use by the people it was made for.

A great idea in a notebook is not an innovation yet. A prototype that works in a lab is not one yet either. It becomes an innovation when it leaves the design process and starts solving the problem in the real world.

Some innovations are new: nothing like them existed before. Others are improved: they make an existing solution work better. Both count.

Try it: choose a Canadian innovation, then move the slider along its path. Watch when it becomes an innovation.

Lesson 3 · Exploring and adding value

Innovators look for new ways and add value

CS2.K2

Innovation is not luck. It comes from approaching a design process with clear intent, meaning you set out on purpose to do three things:

  • Explore new or improved ways to address a need, want or problem.
  • Add value, so the solution is clearly better for someone.
  • Take risks by trying ideas that might not work (Lesson 4).

Value is the benefit people get from a solution. It can save time or money, make people safer or healthier, help the environment, or let more people take part. A solution that is new but adds no value is not much use.

Try it: pick a solution and look through the value lens. The bars are rough class judgments, not measurements.

Lesson 4 · Taking risks

Bold ideas carry risk, so test them small

CS2.K2

In design, taking a risk means trying something that might fail. The newer an idea is, the less anyone knows about whether it will work. New ideas can also bring the biggest gains.

Innovators take smart risks, not careless ones. They never risk anyone’s safety. They test early with cheap prototypes, so a failure costs little and teaches a lot. A failed test is data that shows what to change.

Safe, familiar ideas are less likely to fail, but they usually only make small improvements.

Try it: move the newness dial and switch on early prototyping. The model is made up to show the pattern, not real data.

Lesson 5 · Responsible innovation

Every solution has impacts, good and bad

CS2.K3CS2.S2

An impact is an effect a solution has on people, communities, the economy or the environment. Impacts can be positive (helpful) or negative (harmful).

Some impacts are intended: the designers planned them. Others are unintended: nobody planned them, and they may only show up years later.

Responsible innovation means thinking about both positive and negative potential impacts before and after a solution is used, and changing the design to reduce harm.

Try it: choose an innovation and see its impacts sorted by area. Green edges are positive and red edges are negative.

Lesson 6 · Weighing impacts

Which negative impacts need attention first?

CS2.K3CS2.S2

Not every negative impact is equally serious. Designers often rate each one on two scales from 1 to 5:

  • Likelihood: how likely is it to happen?
  • Severity: how bad would it be if it did?
risk score = likelihood × severity

A high score means the impact needs a plan to reduce it, called mitigation. You do not need to memorize this formula. It is one tool for comparing impacts. Values and the voices of the people affected matter too.

Try it: set the two sliders for an impact of shared e-scooters and see where it lands on the matrix.

Lab Bench

Four investigations. Use a design process to improve a solution, evaluate the impacts of new solutions, and practise the calculations designers use. All data here are made up for practice.

Investigation 1 · CS2.S1

Design sprint: a better winter bus shelter

The old shelter at a stop near your school has a roof and three glass walls. Riders say it is cold, dark after school and hard to use with a wheelchair. Your budget is $12 000 (a made-up number). Choose features, test the design with riders, then save it as a version. Keep improving until it meets all the criteria, then implement it.

Investigation 3 · CS2.S1

How much better is the new version?

Worked example
percent change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100%
A school’s old recycling bins collected 40 kg of recyclables a week. After a redesign with clearer labels, they collect 52 kg. (52 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100% = 12 ÷ 40 × 100% = 30% increase If smaller is better (like wait time or cost), a decrease is the improvement. A wait that drops from 10 min to 8 min is a 20% decrease.

Investigation 4 · CS2.S2

Rank the risks

Worked example
risk score = likelihood × severity
A school app reminds students about homework. One risk: it sends too many alerts at night. Likelihood 4, severity 2: 4 × 2 = 8. Another risk: student data is shared without permission. Likelihood 2, severity 5: 2 × 5 = 10. The data risk scores higher, so the team deals with it first, for example by storing less data.
