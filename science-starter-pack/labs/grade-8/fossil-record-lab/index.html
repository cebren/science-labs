Fossil Record Lab

Understanding: Evidence from geological records provides information about Earth’s history. In this lab you will walk the geological time scale, read rock layers like pages in a book, follow a living thing as it becomes a fossil, compare fossils across millions of years and explore the dinosaur country of the Alberta badlands.

Learn

Seven short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Lessons 1 and 2 are about time and rock layers. Lessons 3 and 4 are about how fossils form and why most living things never become one. Lessons 5 and 6 show what fossils tell us. Lesson 7 visits the Alberta badlands.

Lesson 1 · Deep time

The geological time scale

ES1.K1ES1.S1

Earth is about 4.6 billion years old. That is so long that scientists call it deep time. To keep track of it, they use the geological time scale. It is a chart that splits Earth’s history into time intervals.

The biggest intervals are eons. Eons are split into eras, and eras are split into periods. For example, the dinosaurs of Alberta lived in the Cretaceous period, in the Mesozoic era, in the Phanerozoic eon.

Many boundaries on the scale mark big changes. Some mark major geological events, such as mountains rising or seas flooding a continent. Others mark the appearance and disappearance of life forms. The end of the Cretaceous, about 66 million years ago, is when the non‑bird dinosaurs disappeared.

Scientists write Ma for “millions of years ago.” So 66 Ma means 66 million years ago.

Lesson 2 · Reading rock layers

Relative dating: which layer is older?

ES1.K2ES1.S2

Sedimentary rock forms in layers. Sand, mud or shells settle on the bottom of a lake, river or sea. Over time, new layers pile on top and press the lower ones into rock.

Relative dating is a way to put rock layers in order from older to younger. It does not tell you an age in years. It tells you which came first. Geologists use a few rules.

Superposition: in layers that have not been flipped or disturbed, the oldest layer is at the bottom and the youngest is at the top.

Original horizontality: layers are laid down flat. If they are tilted or folded, something happened to them later.

Cross‑cutting: anything that cuts through a layer, such as a crack called a fault or a sheet of cooled magma called a dike, is younger than the layers it cuts.

Scientists can also find an age in years by measuring natural radioactive elements in some rocks, such as volcanic ash. That is called absolute dating. In this lab you only need relative dating.

Lesson 3 · Making a fossil

From living thing to stone

ES1.K5ES1.K4ES1.S3

A fossil is the preserved remains or traces of a living thing from long ago. Fossils form in two main ways.

Mineralized: after burial, water seeping through the sediment carries dissolved minerals. The minerals fill the tiny spaces in bone, shell or wood. Sometimes they slowly replace the original material. The result is a stony copy, such as a dinosaur bone or petrified wood.

Compressed: a leaf, fern or soft animal is squashed flat under the weight of layers above. Heat and pressure drive off most of the liquids and gases. A thin dark film of carbon, called a carbon film, is left in the rock.

Other fossils are impressions. A shell pressed into mud can dissolve away and leave a hollow called a mold. If minerals fill the mold, they form a cast. Footprints, burrows and nests are trace fossils. They show what an animal did, not its body.

Fossils usually take thousands to millions of years to form.

Lesson 4 · Where fossils are found

Why fossils are rare, and why they are in sedimentary rock

ES1.K4ES1.K7

Most living things never become fossils. A fossil needs specific conditions:

  • Fast burial in mud, sand or volcanic ash, before scavengers and decay destroy the remains.
  • Hard parts such as bones, shells, teeth or wood. Soft bodies usually rot first.
  • Little oxygen, which slows decay.
  • Time for minerals to do their work.

That is why fossils are found in sedimentary rocks. Those rocks form from the same layers of sediment that bury living things. Igneous rock forms from melted rock, which would destroy remains. Metamorphic rock is changed by great heat and pressure, which usually crushes or erases fossils.

Even after a fossil forms, geological processes can destroy it. Weathering and erosion can break it apart at the surface. Rock can be heated, squeezed or melted deep underground. This is why the fossil record has gaps.

Lesson 5 · Reading the fossil record

What fossils tell us about the past

ES1.K3

The fossil record is all the fossils ever found, together with the rock layers they came from. Like a library with missing pages, it is incomplete, but it still tells us a lot.

Living and extinct organisms: fossils show us living things that are gone today, such as dinosaurs and ammonites, and the ancestors of living things, such as early horses. An organism is extinct when no members of its species are left alive.

Past climates and environments: coral fossils point to a warm, shallow sea. Coal comes from swamp plants. Fossils of turtles and crocodile relatives point to a warm climate.

Geological events: sea fossils high in the Rocky Mountains show that the rock formed under water and was later pushed up. A layer where many kinds of fossils suddenly stop can mark a mass extinction.

Some fossils are index fossils. They were common, spread widely, and lived for a short time. Finding one helps geologists match layers of the same age in different places.

Lesson 6 · Evidence of change

Comparing organisms and fossils

ES1.K6ES1.S4

When scientists compare fossils from older and younger layers, and compare fossils with living organisms, they see that living things have changed over time. Scientists call this change in living things over many generations evolution.

Horses are a well studied example. The oldest horse relatives, from about 55 million years ago, were about the size of a fox and had several toes on each foot. Younger fossils are larger, with fewer toes and taller teeth for chewing tough grass. Today’s horses stand on a single toe on each foot.

Some fossils show a mix of features. Tiktaalik, found in 2004 on Ellesmere Island in Nunavut, lived about 375 million years ago. It had gills and scales like a fish, but also a neck and sturdy fins with wrist‑like bones.

Comparing body parts of living animals adds more evidence. The arm of a human, the wing of a bat and the flipper of a whale have the same set of bones arranged in different ways.

Lesson 7 · Alberta’s badlands

“Bad lands to cross”

ES1.K8ES1.S5ES1.K7

Early French explorers called the dry, steep, gullied land of what is now Alberta “les mauvaises terres à traverser,” which means bad lands to cross. The name badlands stuck.

Badlands have bare slopes, hoodoos and deep valleys, with very few plants. Along the Red Deer River, near Drumheller and in Dinosaur Provincial Park, meltwater at the end of the last Ice Age carved deep valleys. Rain and runoff keep wearing the soft rock away.

That erosion is why the badlands are renowned for extensive fossil beds. Each spring, rain and melting snow uncover new bones in rock layers laid down about 77 to 66 million years ago, when Alberta was a warm coastal plain beside an inland sea. The same erosion will destroy fossils that are not found in time.

The badlands lie in Treaty 7 territory. First Nations peoples knew these lands and their large bones long before scientists arrived.

    Investigation 1 · Represent the time scale

    Squeeze 4.6 billion years

    ES1.S1ES1.K1

    A scale model makes deep time easier to picture. You can turn any age into a distance on a strip, or into a date on a one‑year calendar. You do not need to memorize these formulas.

    fraction of history = age ÷ 4600 Ma
    distance from “today” end = fraction × length of strip
    days before midnight on Dec 31 = fraction × 365
    Worked example: the end of the dinosaurs (66 Ma) fraction = 66 ÷ 4600 = 0.0143 On a 100 m strip: 0.0143 × 100 m = 1.43 m from the “today” end. On the calendar: 0.0143 × 365 = 5.2 days before midnight, so about December 26.
