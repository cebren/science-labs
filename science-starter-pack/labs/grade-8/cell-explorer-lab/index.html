Cell Explorer Lab

Understanding: Cells support life in organisms of varying complexity. A single bacterium in a canola field, a yeast cell in bread dough and the trillions of cells in your body all carry out the same basic jobs: they take in what they need, release energy, build what they use and keep their insides steady.

Learn

Nine short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with what a cell is, learn how microscopes revealed cells, then look inside plant, animal and bacterial cells and see how a cell keeps itself alive.

Lesson 1 · The basic unit of life
LS1.K1LS1.K4

Every living thing is made of cells

A cell is the smallest unit that can carry out all the processes of life. It takes in materials, releases energy, grows, responds and makes more cells. That is why we call the cell the basic unit of life.

Some organisms are unicellular: the whole organism is one cell. A bacterium or a yeast cell is a complete living thing. Other organisms are multicellular: they are made of many cells that share the work. Your body has trillions of cells.

Cells come in many sizes and shapes, and the shape usually fits the job. A red blood cell is a small flexible disc that squeezes through tiny blood vessels. A nerve cell has long thin branches that carry signals over long distances.

Remember: most cells are measured in micrometres (µm). One micrometre is one thousandth of a millimetre.

Lesson 2 · Seeing the invisible
LS1.K2

Microscopes opened up the world of cells

Most cells are far too small to see with your eyes alone. Your eye can just make out something about one tenth of a millimetre wide. Before microscopes, nobody knew cells existed.

Each better microscope let scientists see smaller things. Early lenses showed that living things are built of cells. Later, scientists used microscopes to show that new cells come only from cells that already exist. These ideas became the cell theory:

  • All living things are made of one or more cells.
  • The cell is the basic unit of life.
  • New cells come from existing cells.

Today, electron microscopes and glowing dyes let scientists see the tiny structures inside cells and even watch them work.

Lesson 3 · The compound light microscope
LS1.K3LS1.S1LS1.S2

Two lenses multiply the view

A compound light microscope uses light and two sets of lenses. The eyepiece (ocular lens) is the lens you look through. The objective lens is the lens just above the slide. Most school microscopes have three or four objectives on a turning nosepiece.

Each lens magnifies the image. The total magnification is the product of the two lenses: you multiply them.

total magnification = eyepiece magnification × objective magnification
Worked exampleEyepiece 10×, objective 40×.Total = 10 × 40 = 400×. The cell looks 400 times wider than it really is.

You do not need to memorize this. It is a tool you can look up and use.

Lesson 4 · Organelles
LS1.K4LS1.K5LS1.S4LS1.S5

Each organelle has a job

Inside a cell are organelles: tiny specialized structures that each do a particular job. They work a bit like the rooms and machines in a factory. Organelles are found in the cells of both unicellular and multicellular organisms.

These parts are found in both plant and animal cells:

  • Cell membrane: the thin outer layer that controls what enters and leaves.
  • Nucleus: holds the cell’s genetic instructions and controls its activities.
  • Cytoplasm: the jelly-like fluid that fills the cell. Organelles sit in it and many reactions happen in it.
  • Mitochondria: release energy from food by cellular respiration.
  • Ribosomes: tiny structures that build proteins.
  • Vacuoles: sacs that store water, food and wastes.

Lesson 5 · Plant and animal cells
LS1.K6LS1.S4

Two extra parts make a plant cell

Plant cells have every organelle an animal cell has. They also have two parts animal cells do not have:

  • Cell wall: a stiff layer outside the membrane. It supports the cell and gives it a box-like shape.
  • Chloroplasts: green organelles that capture light energy and use it to make sugar by photosynthesis.

A plant cell also usually has one large central vacuole full of water. When it is full, it pushes out against the wall and keeps the plant firm. Animal cells have only small vacuoles.

Not every plant cell has chloroplasts. A root cell or an onion bulb cell gets no light, so it has none. Cell walls are also found outside the plant world, in fungi and bacteria.

Lesson 6 · Cells without a nucleus
LS1.K7

Bacteria keep their DNA loose

Some unicellular organisms, including all bacteria, have no nucleus. Their genetic material (DNA) sits in a loop in the cytoplasm instead of inside a nucleus. Bacteria also have no mitochondria or chloroplasts.

Bacteria still have a cell membrane, cytoplasm and ribosomes. Most also have a cell wall. Some have a tail-like flagellum they spin to swim.

Bacteria are much smaller than plant and animal cells. A typical bacterium is about 1 to 2 µm long. About 30 of them would fit across one cheek cell.

Yeasts, amoebas and other protists are unicellular too, but their cells do have a nucleus.

Lesson 7 · Microbes and health
LS1.K8LS1.S6

Tiny organisms, big effects

A microbe is a living thing too small to see without a microscope. Many are unicellular: bacteria, some fungi such as yeasts, and many protists such as amoebas.

Most microbes are harmless, and many are helpful. Bacteria in your gut help digest food. Bacteria in the roots of peas and lentils help those crops grow.

Some microbes cause disease. They can damage cells, use up the host’s nutrients or release harmful substances. This can affect humans and other multicellular organisms, including farm animals and crops.

Remember: the same kind of microbe can be harmless in one place and harmful in another. Hand washing, safe water and cooking food fully all lower the risk.

Lesson 8 · Keeping the inside steady
LS1.K9LS1.S8

Four processes keep a cell alive

A cell must keep its inside environment steady even when things outside change. Four processes help:

  • Cellular respiration provides energy. In the mitochondria, sugar (glucose) reacts with oxygen. Energy is released, and carbon dioxide and water are made.
  • Diffusion moves vital substances such as oxygen into the cell and wastes such as carbon dioxide out.
  • Osmosis moves water into or out of the cell.
  • Protein formation happens at the ribosomes. Proteins build cell parts and do specific jobs, such as speeding up reactions.
glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy

Lesson 9 · The semi-permeable membrane
LS1.K10LS1.K9LS1.S7

A gatekeeper with tiny gaps

The cell membrane is semi-permeable. It lets some particles through and blocks others. Small particles such as water, oxygen and carbon dioxide pass through easily. Large particles such as starch cannot.

Diffusion is the movement of particles from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated. Particles keep moving at random until they are spread evenly.

Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a semi-permeable membrane. Water moves toward the side with more dissolved particles, where there is less free water.

The gaps in this model are a simple picture. Real membranes let particles through in several ways, including through special proteins.

Investigation 1 · Virtual microscope
LS1.S1LS1.S2LS1.S3

Find, focus and observe

Choose a slide. Always start on the lowest power objective. Use the coarse focus to find the image, then the fine focus to sharpen it. Only use the fine focus on high power.

  1. Carry a real microscope with two hands: one on the arm, one under the base.
  2. Center what you want to see before you switch to a higher power.
  3. Add a stain to make the nucleus and cell edges easier to see.
  4. Count how many cells fit across the field of view to estimate their size.
cell size ≈ field of view diameter ÷ number of cells across

    Investigation 4 · Cells in different solutions
    LS1.S7LS1.S8LS1.K10

    What happens when the water outside changes?

    Place a cell in a solution. Predict which way water will move by osmosis, then run the test.

    • Fresh water has fewer dissolved particles than the cell. Water moves in.
    • A matching solution has the same amount. Water moves both ways equally.
    • Salty water has more dissolved particles than the cell. Water moves out.

    Elodea, a pond plant found in Alberta, is often used for this test. In salty water you can see the membrane pull away from the wall.

    Investigation 5 · Potato core osmosis
    LS1.S7

    Measure osmosis with mass

    Cut equal potato cores, find each mass, then soak them in salt solutions for 24 hours. Pat them dry and find the mass again.

    Manipulated variable: salt concentration. Responding variable: percent change in mass. Controlled: core size, potato, time, temperature.

    % change = (final mass − starting mass) ÷ starting mass × 100
    Worked exampleStart 2.50 g, final 2.80 g.(2.80 − 2.50) ÷ 2.50 × 100 = +12%. The core gained water.

    Teacher notes

    • LS1.K2: the cell theory is added as context. Timeline dates (Hooke 1665, van Leeuwenhoek 1670s, Schleiden 1838, Schwann 1839, Virchow 1855, Ruska and Knoll 1931, the University of Toronto electron microscope 1938) are well known but should be checked against your resource. Remak’s earlier work on cell division is mentioned so Virchow is not credited alone.
    • LS1.K3: lens values (10× eyepiece; 4×, 10×, 40× objectives) and field of view diameters (about 4.5, 1.8 and 0.45 mm) are typical values that vary between microscopes. Estimating cell size from the field of view is added to support LS1.S1 and S3.
    • LS1.K5: cytoplasm is taught as the jelly-like fluid that fills the cell and holds the organelles, since it is not an organelle in the strict sense. Ribosomes are taught as tiny structures without a membrane. Animal cells are taught as having small vacuoles; plant cells usually have one large central vacuole.
    • LS1.K6: “only in plant cells” is taught as “in plant cells but not animal cells”. The lab notes that fungi and bacteria also have cell walls, algae also have chloroplasts, and plant cells that get no light (roots, onion bulb) have no chloroplasts.
    • LS1.K7: “some unicellular organisms, including bacteria” is taught as all bacteria having no nucleus, with DNA in a loop in the cytoplasm (this links to LS2.K2). Yeasts and protists are noted as unicellular organisms that do have a nucleus. Mature human red blood cells, which lose their nucleus, appear once in Sort It as a challenge card.
    • LS1.K8: fungi are taught through unicellular yeasts, since most fungi (mushrooms, moulds) are multicellular. Most microbes are described as harmless or helpful. The clubroot organism is described as a protist-like soil parasite. Alberta details (clubroot found in Alberta canola in the early 2000s, Giardia in untreated surface water) are described generally and should be checked. Disease content is general science, not medical advice; be aware some students may have personal experience with these illnesses.
    • LS1.K9: “regulate the internal environment” is taught as keeping conditions inside the cell steady. Cellular respiration is given as a word equation in the mitochondria. Protein formation is taught at the ribosomes using instructions from the nucleus; DNA itself is left for LS2.
    • LS1.K10: semi-permeable is noted as also called selectively permeable. Osmosis is defined as the diffusion of water across a semi-permeable membrane toward the side with more dissolved particles. The particle model uses simple gaps and makes water less likely to cross from a side crowded with sugar; real membranes also use transport proteins.
    • Numbers: cell sizes are rounded typical values (bacterium about 2 µm, red blood cell about 8 µm, cheek cell about 60 µm, human egg about 0.1 mm, onion skin cell about 250 µm, a long nerve cell about 1 m). Microscope images are drawings, not photographs. Potato core results come from a made-up model and are labelled as practice data.
