Fluids and Pressure Lab

Understanding: The motion and arrangement of particles can be used to predict the behaviour of fluids. Liquids and gases flow because their particles can slide past each other. Moving particles hit surfaces and create pressure. The space between particles decides how much a fluid can be squeezed.

Learn

Six short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with what makes a fluid, then see where pressure comes from, how thick fluids resist flowing, how much fluids squeeze, and how a trapped liquid can lift a car.

Lesson 1 · What is a fluid?
F2.K1

Fluids have no fixed shape and can flow

A fluid is any substance that can flow and has no fixed shape. Liquids and gases are both fluids.

The particle model explains why. In a solid, particles are packed tightly and held in place. They can only vibrate. In a liquid, particles are still close together, but they can slide past each other. In a gas, particles are far apart and move freely in every direction.

Because liquid and gas particles can move past each other, a fluid takes the shape of its container. A liquid keeps its volume. A gas spreads out to fill the whole container.

Remember: "fluid" does not mean "liquid". The air in your classroom is a fluid too.

Lesson 2 · Where pressure comes from
F2.K2F2.K3

Pressure is particles hitting a surface

Fluid particles are always moving. In a gas, they constantly crash into the walls of their container. Each hit is a tiny push on the wall.

The wall pushes back on each particle with an equal but opposite force. That is why a particle bounces off. Millions of these hits every second add up to a steady push spread over the wall. That push spread over an area is pressure.

Pressure goes up when there are more hits or harder hits. You can get more hits by adding particles or by squeezing the same particles into less space. You get harder hits by warming the gas, because the particles move faster.

Liquids exert pressure too. In a liquid, pressure also grows with depth, because of the weight of the liquid above. Your ears feel this at the bottom of a pool.

Lesson 3 · Pressure, force and area
F2.K2F2.K5

P = F / A

Pressure is the force pushing perpendicular (straight into) a surface, divided by the area of that surface.

pressure = force ÷ area    P = F / A

Force is in newtons (N). Area is in square metres (m²). Pressure is in pascals (Pa). One pascal is one newton on each square metre. That is small, so we often use kilopascals (kPa). 1 kPa = 1000 Pa.

The same force on a smaller area makes a bigger pressure. That is why skate blades cut into ice, and why wide snowshoes keep you on top of deep snow. Snowshoes were developed by Indigenous peoples of North America for winter travel.

The unit is named after Blaise Pascal, a French scientist and mathematician of the 1600s who studied fluids and air pressure.

Worked example: a student pushes down with 600 N on boots with a total area of 0.06 m². P = 600 N ÷ 0.06 m² = 10 000 Pa = 10 kPa.

Model: standing on snow

Lesson 4 · Viscosity
F2.K2

Some fluids resist flowing more than others

Viscosity is a fluid's resistance to flowing. Honey has a high viscosity. It pours slowly. Water has a low viscosity. It pours quickly.

A viscous fluid also resists objects moving through it. A marble sinks slowly through honey but quickly through water. Timing a falling marble is one way to compare viscosity.

In a thick liquid, the particles tug on each other more strongly, or are tangled, so they do not slide past each other easily. Warming most liquids lowers their viscosity, because the particles move faster and slide apart more easily. Cold syrup pours slowly. Warm syrup runs.

Alberta example: bitumen from the oil sands is so viscous that it is mixed with a thinner liquid, called diluent, before it can flow through a pipeline.

Lesson 5 · Compressibility
F2.K2F2.K4

Space between particles decides how much a fluid squeezes

Compressibility is how much a fluid's volume changes when pressure pushes on it.

Gas particles have lots of empty space between them. Pushing on a gas moves the particles closer together, so the volume shrinks a lot. Gases are highly compressible. That is how a scuba tank or a bike tire holds so much air.

Liquid particles are already touching or nearly touching. There is almost no space to squeeze out. Liquids are nearly incompressible. Water's volume changes by only about 0.005% for each extra 100 kPa of pressure.

Try it: seal the tip of a syringe and push the plunger. With air inside, it moves in. With water inside, it hardly moves at all.

Lesson 6 · Pascal's law and hydraulics
F2.K5F2.K6

Push on a trapped fluid and the push spreads everywhere

Pascal's law says that when you add pressure to a fluid trapped in a closed container, that pressure is passed on equally to every part of the fluid and the container walls.

A hydraulic system uses a trapped liquid, usually oil, to do this. Push a small piston with a small force. The pressure is the same at a big piston, so the big piston pushes with a big force.

P = F₁ / A₁ = F₂ / A₂    so   F₂ = F₁ × A₂ / A₁

There is a trade-off. The big piston moves a shorter distance than the small one. The work in and the work out stay the same.

A system that uses a trapped gas, usually compressed air, is called pneumatic. Liquids work better for lifting, because they do not squeeze. Gases are useful when a springy, quick action is wanted.

Worked example: 50 N on a 5 cm² piston gives P = 10 N/cm². On a 100 cm² piston, F₂ = 10 N/cm² × 100 cm² = 1000 N.

Teacher notes

  • F2.K2: the draft defines viscosity as "a fluid's resistance to motion". The lab teaches it as resistance to flowing, and adds that a viscous fluid also resists objects moving through it (the marble drop test). The warming effect is taught for liquids only.
  • F2.K3: the collision model is taught with a gas in a box. The wall's equal but opposite push is shown on each bounce. A short note adds that liquid pressure also grows with depth, because of the weight of liquid above. Temperature is included as "harder hits" so students can predict pressure changes.
  • F2.K4: liquids are taught as nearly incompressible, not perfectly incompressible. The figure of about 0.005% per extra 100 kPa for water is a rounded reference value.
  • F2.K5: the draft calls P = F/A "Pascal's law". In physics, P = F/A is the definition of pressure, and Pascal's law (Pascal's principle) says that pressure added to an enclosed fluid is passed on equally throughout the fluid. The lab teaches both, keeps the formula symbolic, and names the pascal unit after Blaise Pascal (France, 1600s).
  • F2.K6: automotive brakes are taught as hydraulic. Hospital beds are taught as one example that can use a hydraulic lift (many modern beds are electric). Jackhammers are taught as usually pneumatic (compressed air), so they show a trapped gas moving a piston rather than a liquid.
  • Examples: snowshoes are described in general terms as developed by Indigenous peoples of North America; please review this wording. The bitumen and diluent example and the Edmonton air pressure value (about 94 kPa) are rounded. Viscosity times, syringe volumes and plunger areas are simulated or approximate practice data.
