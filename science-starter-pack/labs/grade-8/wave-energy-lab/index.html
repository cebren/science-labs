Science 8 · Energy: Stores, Static and Waves · EN3

Wave Energy Lab

Understanding: Energy can be transferred as a wave. A ripple crossing Sylvan Lake, a rumble from a distant earthquake and the sunlight warming a January sidewalk all carry energy from one place to another, and none of them carries the water, rock or air along with it.

Learn

Eight short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with what a wave is, measure its parts, see how it carries energy, then follow waves as they cross from one material into another.

Lesson 1 · Energy on the move
EN3.K1EN3.S1

A wave carries energy, not matter

Tie one end of a rope to a post and flick the other end. A bump races along the rope. When it reaches the post, the post shakes. Energy travelled from your hand to the post.

Did the rope travel to the post? No. Each piece of rope moved up, then back down to where it started. A wave is a disturbance or oscillation (a back and forth or up and down motion) that transfers energy from one point to another without carrying matter along with it.

The same is true on a lake. A duck sitting on the water bobs up and down as waves pass. The waves move toward shore, but the duck stays in about the same place.

Remember: the energy travels along the wave. The material only moves back and forth around its resting place.

Waves that break on a beach are different: near shore the wave topples over and water does rush forward. Out in open water, the water mostly just bobs.

Model: a rope of beads

Watch the red bead. Send a single pulse or make a steady wave. Does the red bead ever reach the post?

Lesson 2 · Measuring a wave
EN3.K2EN3.S2

Waves repeat, so we can measure them

A steady wave has a repeating pattern. The high points are crests. The low points are troughs. The middle line is the rest position, where the rope sits when no wave passes.

  • Wavelength (λ): the distance from one crest to the next crest, in metres.
  • Amplitude (A): the distance from the rest position to a crest (or to a trough).
  • Frequency (f): how many waves pass a point each second, in hertz (Hz). 1 Hz means 1 wave per second.
  • Period (T): the time for one whole wave to pass, in seconds.
  • Speed (v): how far a crest travels each second, in metres per second (m/s).
T = 1 ÷ f     v = f × λ

You do not need to memorize these. They are tools you can look up and use.

Model: parts of a wave

The scale is 1 m = 100 px across the box.
Lesson 3 · Energy in a wave
EN3.K3EN3.S1

Bigger and faster waves carry more energy

In Science 8 you track energy in stores, like the kinetic store of a moving object or the gravitational store of a raised object. A wave is a way to transfer energy from one store to another.

To make a wave with a bigger amplitude, you have to swing your hand farther. To make a wave with a higher frequency, you have to swing it more often. Both take more energy from you, so the wave carries more energy to the far end.

For the same kind of wave in the same material, doubling the amplitude makes the wave carry about 4 times as much energy each second. Doubling the frequency also gives about 4 times as much.

energy carried each second ∝ A² × f²  (same wave type, same material)

The ∝ sign means “goes up with”. Light works a little differently. For light, frequency sets how much energy each tiny packet carries, and brightness depends on how many packets arrive.

Lesson 4 · Does it need a medium?
EN3.K4EN3.S3

Mechanical waves and electromagnetic waves

The material a wave travels through is called its medium. Water is the medium for a lake wave. Air is the medium for most sounds you hear.

Mechanical waves need a medium. They travel by making the particles of the medium move. Sound, water waves, waves on a string and seismic waves (waves through rock from an earthquake) are all mechanical.

Electromagnetic waves (EM waves) do not need a medium. They can cross empty space, where there are almost no particles at all. Radio waves, microwaves, visible light and X-rays are all EM waves. They can also travel through some materials, like light through glass.

Proof from space: sunlight crosses about 150 million km of nearly empty space to reach Edmonton in about 8 minutes. Huge explosions on the Sun make no sound we can hear on Earth, because sound cannot cross empty space.

Model: the bell jar

A ringing bell with a light sits in a sealed jar. Pump the air out and compare what still gets through.

Lesson 5 · Particles in a mechanical wave
EN3.K5EN3.S3

Particles oscillate, energy travels on

In a mechanical wave, each particle of the medium is pushed by its neighbour, moves, and passes the push along. The particles oscillate around their resting places, while the energy moves in the direction the wave travels.

Particles can oscillate in two main ways:

  • Across the direction of travel, like a rope wave. This is called a transverse wave.
  • Along the direction of travel, squeezing together and spreading apart, like a push on a slinky. This is called a longitudinal wave. Sound in air works this way.

Earthquakes send out both kinds. The faster P waves squeeze and stretch rock (longitudinal). The slower S waves shake rock side to side (transverse). Seismometers in Alberta record both.

Model: watch the marked particles

Lesson 6 · Meeting a new medium
EN3.K6EN3.S4

Five things a wave can do at a boundary

When a wave reaches a new medium, such as light hitting water or sound hitting a wall, its energy can go several ways.

  • Transmitted: the wave passes through into the new medium.
  • Reflected: the wave bounces back. An echo is reflected sound.
  • Refracted: the wave changes direction as it enters the new medium at an angle.
  • Dispersed: a mix of waves spreads apart because each frequency travels at a slightly different speed in the new medium. A prism splits white light into colours this way.
  • Absorbed: the medium takes in the wave’s energy, usually warming up.
Most of the time, several happen at once. A window lets most light through, reflects a little (you can see your reflection at night) and absorbs a little.

Model: boundary explorer

Lesson 7 · The law of reflection
EN3.K7EN3.S5

The angle in equals the angle out

To describe a reflection, draw a normal: a dashed line at 90° to the surface, at the point where the wave hits.

  • The angle of incidence (i) is between the incoming ray and the normal.
  • The angle of reflection (r) is between the reflected ray and the normal.

The law of reflection says these two angles are always equal.

angle of incidence = angle of reflection    i = r

Measure both angles from the normal, not from the mirror. A ray that hits the mirror 30° from its surface is 60° from the normal. Rough surfaces, like paper, scatter light in many directions, but each tiny piece of the surface still follows the law.

Model: a flat mirror

Drag the slider, or focus the red light source and use the arrow keys.

Lesson 8 · Refraction
EN3.K8EN3.S4

A change in speed bends the wave

Refraction is the bending of a wave because its speed changes when it passes into a new medium. Light travels about 300 000 km each second in air, but only about 225 000 km/s in water and about 200 000 km/s in glass.

Think of a wagon rolling at an angle from a sidewalk onto grass. The wheel that reaches the grass first slows down first, so the wagon turns.

  • Slowing down: the wave bends toward the normal.
  • Speeding up: the wave bends away from the normal.
  • Hitting straight on (along the normal): the speed changes, but the wave does not bend.

Refraction is why a straw looks broken in a glass of water, and why lake waves turn to come in almost straight toward shore: waves slow down in shallow water.

Model: light into a new medium

Lab Bench

Four investigations. Use the wave equation, test how amplitude and frequency change the energy a wave delivers, measure reflections, and use echoes to find the depth of a lake. All numbers here are made up for practice.

Investigation 1 · The wave equation
EN3.S2EN3.K2

Wave calculator

Enter any frequency and wavelength. The calculator finds the speed and the period.

Worked example (made-up numbers)

Waves on Sylvan Lake have crests 4 m apart. A float bobs up and down once every 2 s. How fast are the waves moving?

Step 1. The period is T = 2 s, so the frequency is f = 1 ÷ T = 1 ÷ 2 s = 0.5 Hz.

Step 2. v = f × λ = 0.5 Hz × 4 m = 2 m/s.

Practice: randomized problems

Investigation 2 · Ripple tank
EN3.S1EN3.K3

What makes a wave deliver more energy?

Question: how do amplitude and frequency affect the energy a water wave delivers to a float?

A paddle at one end of a model ripple tank makes waves. A sensor in a float at the far end measures the energy that arrives in 10 seconds, in made-up energy units.

  1. Predict: what will happen to the energy if you double the amplitude?
  2. Change one variable at a time. Keep the other one the same.
  3. Run each setting and record it. Real measurements wobble a little, so run each one twice.

Investigation 3 · Mirror protractor
EN3.S5EN3.K7

Represent the law of reflection

A light ray hits a flat mirror. Find the angle of reflection. Watch out: some problems give the angle from the mirror surface, not from the normal. Change it first.

Worked example

A ray hits a mirror 25° from the surface. What is the angle of reflection?

Step 1. The normal is at 90° to the surface, so i = 90° − 25° = 65°.

Step 2. The law of reflection says r = i, so r = 65°.

How to draw it on paper: draw the mirror, draw the normal with a protractor, measure i from the normal, then measure the same angle on the other side of the normal.

Practice: randomized rays

Investigation 4 · Echo sounder
EN3.S4EN3.K6

Using reflected sound to measure depth

A boat’s echo sounder sends a short pulse of sound down into the lake. The sound is reflected by the lake bottom and returns to the boat. The sounder times the round trip.

depth = speed × time ÷ 2

Divide by 2 because the sound travels down and back up. Sound travels about 1480 m/s in water and about 343 m/s in air at 20 °C.

Worked example (made-up numbers)

An echo returns after 0.05 s. How deep is the lake?

Step 1. Distance there and back = 1480 m/s × 0.05 s = 74 m.

Step 2. Depth = 74 m ÷ 2 = 37 m.

A soft, muddy bottom absorbs more of the sound, so the echo is weaker. A rocky bottom reflects more, so the echo is stronger.

Practice: randomized echoes

Teacher notes

  • EN3.K1: “without transporting matter” is taught as no overall transport: particles oscillate around a rest position and return. Breaking waves near a beach are noted as the exception students will raise.
  • EN3.K2: period and speed are linked with T = 1 ÷ f and v = f × λ, shown as tools, not for memorizing. Amplitude is taught from rest position to crest (not crest to trough).
  • EN3.K3: the draft says higher frequency and amplitude “transfer a greater amount of energy”. The lab teaches this as energy carried each second, for the same wave type in the same medium, using the proportionality energy ∝ A² × f² as context. For light, a brief note says frequency sets the energy of each packet and brightness depends on how many arrive (photons are not named). Energy stores link to EN1.
  • EN3.K4: “do not require a medium” is taught as can travel through empty space; EM waves can also pass through some materials. Seismic waves are classed as mechanical. Sun distance and light travel time are rounded (about 150 million km, about 8 minutes).
  • EN3.K5: transverse and longitudinal are added as brief context to show the two directions particles can oscillate. P and S waves are described qualitatively (P faster); no speeds are given.
  • EN3.K6: “dispersed” is ambiguous. The lab teaches dispersion as a mix of frequencies spreading apart because each travels at a slightly different speed in the new medium (prism, rainbow). Scattering from rough surfaces is mentioned under reflection only. Students learn that several behaviours usually happen at once.
  • EN3.K7: angles are taught as measured from the normal, with practice that converts angles given from the mirror surface. Diffuse reflection is mentioned briefly; each small piece of a rough surface still obeys the law.
  • EN3.K8: refraction is taught as bending when a wave enters a new medium at an angle; a wave entering along the normal changes speed without bending. The model uses indices of refraction (air 1.00, water 1.33, glass 1.50) to draw rays, but students only see the speeds (about 300 000, 225 000 and 200 000 km/s) and the toward or away rule. Shallow water slowing lake waves is given as context.
  • Numbers: sound in water about 1480 m/s and in air about 343 m/s at 20 °C are rounded reference values. Sylvan Lake wave values, ripple tank energies and echo times are made up for practice and labelled that way.
