Science 8 · Sustainability Capstone · SL3

Sustainable Futures Lab

Understanding: Sustainability supports present needs while addressing long-term impacts. People meet today's needs for food, energy and homes in ways that keep Earth's systems working for the people who come after us.

Learn

Ten short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with what sustainability means, then look at the actions and technologies that support it, the way we farm and mine, Alberta's energy choices, and the impacts every choice has on people, nature and money.

Lesson 1 · Lasting systems
SL3.K2SL3.K1SL3.S1

What is sustainability?

Sustainability is the ability of systems to function effectively into the future. A system is a set of parts that work together, like a lake and its fish, a farm, a city or an electricity grid.

A sustainable system meets present needs without wearing itself out. A lake can feed people for hundreds of years if we take fish no faster than new fish grow. If we take too many, the catch is big for a few years and then the system stops working.

In Grade 7 you learned about environmental stewardship: the shared job of protecting, restoring and wisely using the environment. Stewardship supports sustainability. When people care for land, water and air, they meet today's needs in ways that also consider the needs of future generations.

Remember: stewardship is what people do. Sustainability is the result we want: systems that keep working for people today and in the future.

Lesson 2 · What we can do
SL3.K3SL3.S2

Four actions that support sustainability

Communities, companies and governments use four big kinds of action:

  • Using green technology that lowers harm to the environment.
  • Managing resources such as oil, gas, forests and water while protecting air, land and water quality.
  • Planning urban or rural expansion (new neighbourhoods, roads and farms) while preserving natural areas.
  • Setting targets for reducing pollution, waste and greenhouse gas emissions.

A greenhouse gas is a gas, such as carbon dioxide or methane, that traps energy in the atmosphere and warms Earth. A target is a clear goal with a starting amount (the baseline), an amount to reach and a date.

Lesson 3 · Green technology
SL3.K4SL3.S3

Technology that lowers our impact

Green technology is technology designed to reduce the impacts of human activity on the environment. The curriculum names five examples:

  • Electric vehicles and hydrogen-powered vehicles, which give off no exhaust from a tailpipe.
  • Solar power, which turns sunlight into electricity.
  • Carbon capture and storage (CCS), which traps carbon dioxide from a factory or plant and stores it deep underground.
  • Vertical farming, which grows crops indoors in stacked layers.

Every technology has trade-offs. How much a technology helps often depends on where its energy comes from. An electric car charged from a grid that burns a lot of fossil fuel still causes some emissions at the power plant.

Lesson 4 · Smart monitoring
SL3.K5SL3.S3

AI and drones: monitor, predict, improve

Artificial intelligence (AI) is computer software that finds patterns in large amounts of data and uses them to make predictions or decisions. A drone is an aircraft with no pilot on board. It can carry cameras and sensors.

Together, they can reduce environmental impacts in three ways:

  • Monitoring: watching what is happening now, like counting wildlife or spotting smoke.
  • Predicting: forecasting what will happen, like where a wildfire may spread.
  • Improving how resources are used: using only the water, fertilizer or energy that is needed.

AI also has a cost. The computers that run it use electricity and many data centres use water for cooling.

Lesson 5 · Sustainable farms
SL3.K6

Sustainable agriculture

Sustainable agriculture is farming that protects biodiversity, supports soil and ecosystem health and keeps yields strong over the long term. A yield is the amount of crop harvested from an area of land.

Practices on Alberta farms include:

  • Crop rotation: growing different crops in a field in different years, such as canola, then wheat, then peas.
  • Zero tillage: planting seeds without ploughing, so soil stays covered and holds water.
  • Cover crops: plants grown to cover and feed the soil between main crops.
  • Keeping shelterbelts and wetlands: rows of trees and sloughs that block wind and give wildlife a home.
  • Rotational grazing: moving cattle between pastures so grass can regrow.

"Maximizes long-term yield" means the best harvest year after year for decades. It does not mean the biggest possible harvest this year.

Lesson 6 · Innovation
SL3.K7SL3.S3

New ideas from sustainability goals

Goals to use fewer resources and cut pollution push people to innovate, which means create new and better ways of doing things. Two examples:

  • Precision agriculture: farmers use GPS, drones, sensors and AI to treat each part of a field differently. Seed, fertilizer and sprays go only where they are needed.
  • Autonomous zero-emission mining equipment: autonomous means it runs itself without a driver. Zero emission means it gives off no exhaust, because it runs on batteries or hydrogen instead of diesel.

Driverless haul trucks already work in some Alberta oil sands mines. Most still burn diesel. Battery-electric and hydrogen trucks are being tested so that mines can have both features at once.

Lesson 7 · Cleaner energy
SL3.K8SL3.S4

Balancing energy needs and emissions

Albertans need energy to heat homes through cold winters, run businesses and travel long distances. Cleaner energy resources help balance those needs with sustainability goals because they release fewer greenhouse gases for each unit of energy.

  • Natural gas is a fossil fuel. Making electricity from it releases much less carbon dioxide than coal (about 40% less over a plant's whole life), so it is cleaner than coal, but it is not emission free. Methane that leaks from wells and pipes is also a greenhouse gas.
  • Hydrogen gives off only water when used in a fuel cell. It is an energy carrier: it must be made using another energy source.
  • Nuclear power plants give off almost no greenhouse gases while running.
  • Renewables such as wind, solar and hydro use sources that are naturally replaced.

Alberta stopped making electricity from coal in 2024. Most of its power now comes from natural gas, with a growing share from wind and solar.

Lesson 8 · Hydrogen
SL3.K9SL3.K8

Hydrogen: one gas, many jobs

Hydrogen (H₂) is the lightest gas. It stores energy that can be released by burning it or by combining it with oxygen in a fuel cell, which makes electricity and water.

Hydrogen can be used in:

  • Gas utility systems: mixed into the natural gas that heats homes.
  • Transportation: fuel cell trucks, buses and trains.
  • Industrial processes: making fertilizer and refining fuels.
  • Power generation: burned in turbines or used in fuel cells to make electricity.
  • Power storage: extra electricity makes hydrogen, which is stored and turned back into electricity later.

How clean hydrogen is depends on how it is made. People use colour names: grey hydrogen is made from natural gas and releases carbon dioxide; blue hydrogen is made the same way but most of the carbon dioxide is captured and stored; green hydrogen splits water using renewable electricity.

Lesson 9 · Uranium and nuclear
SL3.K10SL3.S4

Alberta's uranium and nuclear energy

Uranium is a heavy metal element found in some rocks. In a nuclear power plant, nuclear fission splits uranium atoms. Each split releases a large amount of energy, which heats water into steam that spins a turbine to make electricity.

Alberta has uranium resources in its northeast, where the uranium-rich Athabasca Basin crosses from Saskatchewan. These deposits are not mined today. Alberta has no nuclear power plant yet, but it is studying nuclear energy, including small modular reactors, as part of a clean energy strategy.

A nuclear plant can run day and night in any weather with very low emissions. It takes many years and a lot of money to build, and its used fuel stays radioactive for a very long time, so it must be stored safely.

Northeastern Alberta is within Treaty 8 territory. Decisions about mining and energy projects involve consultation with First Nations and Métis communities.

    Lesson 10 · Three kinds of impact
    SL3.K11SL3.S5

    Social, environmental and economic impacts

    Every sustainability effort has impacts, which are effects on the world. Scientists and planners look through three lenses:

    • Social: effects on people and communities, such as health, safety, jobs, culture and fairness.
    • Environmental: effects on air, land, water, living things and the climate.
    • Economic: effects on money, such as costs, savings, prices and income.

    Impacts can be positive or negative, and different groups can feel them differently. A wind farm can bring lease payments to a farmer but change the view for a neighbour. Good decisions weigh all three lenses and listen to the people affected, including First Nations and Métis communities.
