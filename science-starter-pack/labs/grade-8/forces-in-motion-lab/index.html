Forces in Motion Lab

Understanding: The motion of an object changes because of forces acting on it. Add up the pushes and pulls on an object to find the net force. Balanced forces leave motion unchanged. Unbalanced forces speed objects up, slow them down or turn them.

Learn

Six short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start by adding forces together, then see what balanced and unbalanced forces do to motion, and finish by measuring speed.

Lesson 1 · Adding up forces
F1.K1

Net force is the sum of all forces

A force is a push or a pull. Forces are measured in newtons (N). A small apple pushes down on your hand with a force of about 1 N.

Every force has a size, called its magnitude, and a direction. That is why scientists draw forces as arrows. A longer arrow means a bigger force.

Most objects have several forces acting on them at once. The net force is the sum of all of them. Forces in the same direction add together. Forces in opposite directions work against each other, so you subtract.

net force = (forces to the right) − (forces to the left)
Worked example: two students pull a sled right with 60 N and 40 N. One student pulls left with 70 N. Net force = (60 + 40) − 70 = 30 N to the right.

Model: tug of war

Lesson 2 · Balanced forces
F1.K2

Zero net force: no change in motion

When the forces on an object cancel out, the net force is zero. We say the forces are balanced.

Balanced forces do not change an object's motion. That has two meanings:

  • An object at rest stays at rest.
  • An object that is already moving keeps the same speed and the same direction.

Many people think balanced forces mean an object must be still. That is a common mistake. A car cruising at a steady speed on a straight highway has balanced forces too.

A force diagram shows one object as a box, with an arrow for each force on it. Pick an example to see its diagram.

Lesson 3 · Unbalanced forces
F1.K3

Net force changes speed and direction

When the net force is not zero, the forces are unbalanced. Unbalanced forces change motion. What happens depends on the direction of the net force compared with the direction the object is moving:

  • Net force in the same direction as the motion: the object speeds up.
  • Net force in the opposite direction: the object slows down. It may stop, and if the force keeps acting it starts moving the other way.
  • Net force at an angle to the motion: the object changes direction.

A bigger net force makes a bigger change in the same amount of time.

The model shows a motion map: the puck's position every quarter second, like a strobe photo. Even spacing means steady speed. Growing gaps mean speeding up.

Model: hockey puck motion map

Lesson 4 · Newton's first law
F1.K4

The law of inertia

Isaac Newton described motion in three laws in 1687. His first law of motion, also called the law of inertia, says:

An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion keeps moving in a straight line at a constant speed, unless an unbalanced force acts on it.

Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes in its motion. Inertia is a property of the object. It is not a force.

On Earth, a sliding object usually slows down and stops. That is not because it "runs out" of motion. Friction and air resistance are unbalanced forces acting against the motion. The less friction there is, the farther the object slides. With no friction at all, it would never stop.

Model: same shove, different surfaces

A puck leaves your stick at 5 m/s. Choose a surface to see how far it slides before friction stops it.

Distances are estimates for a smooth puck. Real values depend on the surface.

Lesson 5 · Mass and inertia
F1.K5

More mass, more inertia

Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms (kg). The greater an object's mass, the greater its inertia.

More inertia means you need a bigger force to change the object's motion. An empty toboggan is easy to start and easy to stop. A toboggan loaded with three friends takes a much bigger push to get going, and it is harder to stop at the bottom of the hill.

For the same push over the same time, doubling the mass gives only half the change in speed.

Mass is not the same as weight. Weight is the pull of gravity on an object. An astronaut's mass, and so their inertia, is the same on Earth and in space.

Toboggans were developed by First Nations peoples for hauling loads over snow, long before they were used for fun on winter hills.

Model: push a toboggan on smooth snow

Each toboggan gets the same push for 1 second. Friction is ignored to keep the comparison fair.

Lesson 6 · Speed
F1.K6

Speed links distance and time

Speed tells you how far an object travels in a certain amount of time. It is distance divided by time.

v = d ÷ t   ·   speed = distance ÷ time

Common units are metres per second (m/s) and kilometres per hour (km/h). To change m/s into km/h, multiply by 3.6.

The formula gives the average speed over a trip. Your speed may change during the trip, but the average tells you the overall rate.

Worked example: a cyclist on a river valley trail rides 1200 m in 240 s. v = d ÷ t = 1200 m ÷ 240 s = 5 m/s. That is 5 × 3.6 = 18 km/h.

The same formula can be rearranged: d = v × t and t = d ÷ v. You do not need to memorize these. You can always look them up.

Model: distance and time graph

A steeper line means a greater speed. Example speeds are typical values for practice.

Teacher notes

  • F1.K2: "the object's motion will not change" is taught as both cases: an object at rest stays at rest, and a moving object keeps the same speed and direction. The lab names the common misconception that balanced forces mean an object must be still.
  • F1.K3 and F1.S2: net force is found along one horizontal line (right positive, left negative), as S2 limits. Lesson 3 shows a sideways push only to illustrate a change in direction, without adding forces at angles. The lab notes that a backward net force slows an object, which may stop and then reverse if the force continues.
  • F1.K4: inertia is taught as a property of matter, not a force. Friction and air resistance are named as the unbalanced forces that stop sliding objects on Earth. Newton's Principia date (1687) is given once as context.
  • F1.K5: mass is separated from weight. "The greater the force required" is shown with equal pushes giving smaller speed changes for larger masses (same force, double mass, half the change). The formula F = ma is not taught; the halving pattern is shown as data only.
  • F1.K6: the draft writes v = d/t with "v", the usual symbol for velocity. The lab teaches it as average speed with no direction, and gives the m/s to km/h factor of 3.6.
  • Examples: toboggans are described as developed by First Nations peoples for hauling loads over snow, in general terms only; please review this wording. Surface sliding distances, mover speeds and all Lab Bench data are estimates or made-up practice data.
