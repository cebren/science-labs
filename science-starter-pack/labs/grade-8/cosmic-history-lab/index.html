Cosmic History Lab

Understanding: Understandings of the universe have changed over time. In this lab you will find our address in the universe, follow how ideas about the sky changed, trace the universe from the Big Bang to the solar system, compare how bright stars look and follow a star from birth to death.

Learn

Seven short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Lessons 1 and 2 find our place in the universe. Lesson 3 follows how ideas changed. Lessons 4 and 5 explain the Big Bang theory. Lessons 6 and 7 are about stars.

Lesson 1 · Our cosmic address

Planet, solar system, galaxy, universe

SP1.K1SP1.K2SP1.S1

Astronomy is the scientific study of the universe. Astronomers study planets, moons, stars, galaxies and the universe as a whole. The universe is everything that exists: all of space, matter and energy.

Each thing in space sits inside something bigger. Earth is a planet. It orbits the Sun with seven other planets, so it is part of our solar system. Our solar system is one small part of the Milky Way, a galaxy. A galaxy is a huge group of stars, gas and dust held together by gravity.

The Milky Way is only one galaxy. The universe holds billions of galaxies. Many of the stars in them have their own planets, so there are other solar systems too.

A light-year is the distance light travels in one year, about 9.5 trillion km. It is a distance, not a time.

Lesson 2 · Stars we can see

Every star you can see is in our galaxy

SP1.K3SP1.S4

On a dark night far from the city, you can see a few thousand stars with your naked eye, which means with no telescope or binoculars. Almost all of them are in the Milky Way. Most are close neighbours of the Sun, within a few thousand light-years.

The faint band of light called the Milky Way is the glow of millions of stars too far away to see one by one. We are inside the galaxy, so we see it edge on, like looking across a plate from its rim.

The main exception is the Andromeda Galaxy. From a dark place you can see it as a faint smudge. Its light left about 2.5 million years ago. You are not seeing single stars, only their blended glow.

Lesson 3 · Changing ideas

How our picture of the universe changed

SP1.K4SP1.S2

People have studied the sky for thousands of years. A theory in science is a well-tested explanation. A model is a simpler picture that helps explain or predict. Over time, new tools and new observations changed both.

In Babylonia, sky watchers kept careful records of the Moon and planets on clay tablets and found repeating patterns. In ancient Greece, thinkers built models with Earth at the centre. Ptolemy made the Earth-centred model good enough to predict planet positions. Galileo used a telescope and found evidence that did not fit it.

Later, astronomers measured that some fuzzy patches were whole galaxies far beyond the Milky Way, and that galaxies are moving apart. That led to the Big Bang theory.

Try it: tap each point on the timeline. For each one, notice the tool or evidence that made the idea change.

Many cultures around the world built deep knowledge of the sky. First Nations, Métis and Inuit peoples have long traditions of reading the sky for seasons, travel and teachings. Learn these from Knowledge Keepers and community sources.

    Lesson 4 · The Big Bang theory

    A universe that is stretching

    SP1.K5SP1.S3

    The Big Bang theory explains the origin of the universe. About 13.8 billion years ago, everything in the universe we can observe was packed into an extremely small, hot and dense state. The temperature and pressure were enormous. Since then, the universe has been expanding and cooling.

    The Big Bang was not an explosion at one spot in empty space. Space itself has been stretching everywhere at once. That means there is no centre. Every galaxy sees the others moving away.

    Three main kinds of evidence support the theory. Distant galaxies are moving away from us, and farther ones move away faster. A faint glow of microwaves, left over from the early universe, comes from every direction. The universe is mostly hydrogen and helium, in the amounts the theory predicts.

    Try it: tap any galaxy to make it home. Then drag the slider to stretch space. Compare how far the nearby and faraway galaxies moved.

    Lesson 5 · From the Big Bang to us

    Hydrogen, helium, stars and the solar system

    SP1.K6SP1.S3SP1.S1

    As the universe expanded, it cooled. In the first few minutes, the simplest atom centres formed: hydrogen and helium. They became full atoms about 380 000 years later.

    Gravity pulled clouds of hydrogen and helium together. In a few hundred million years, the first stars lit up. Inside stars, small atoms fuse into bigger ones. Stars made heavier elements such as carbon, oxygen and iron, and spread them into space when they died.

    About 4.6 billion years ago, a cloud of gas and dust that held those heavier elements collapsed. It formed our Sun and solar system. The iron in your blood and the calcium in your bones were made inside stars.

    Lesson 6 · How bright a star looks

    Luminosity and distance

    SP1.K7SP1.S4

    How bright a star looks from Earth is its apparent brightness. It depends on two things. Luminosity is the total amount of light energy a star gives off each second. Distance is how far the star is from us.

    A star with more luminosity looks brighter. A star that is farther away looks dimmer, and the dimming is fast. Light spreads out over a bigger and bigger area as it travels. If a star were twice as far away, it would look one quarter as bright. Three times as far, one ninth as bright.

    apparent brightness ∝ L ÷ d²
    (full form: b = L ÷ (4π d²))

    So a dim star that is close can look brighter than a very luminous star that is far away. Sirius, the brightest star at night, is bright mainly because it is close.

    Try it: pick two real stars or set your own. Change the luminosity and distance and see which one looks brighter.

    Two-star test

    Luminosities and distances of real stars are rounded estimates, and some are uncertain. The model ignores dust between the stars.

    Lesson 7 · The life of a star

    Stars are born, change and die

    SP1.K8SP1.S5

    Stars have a life cycle. A star forms when gravity pulls a cloud of gas and dust, called a nebula, into a hot ball. When its core gets hot enough, it starts to fuse hydrogen into helium and shines.

    Over its life a star changes in mass, size and brightness. It slowly loses mass in a stream of gas called a stellar wind. Near the end it swells into a giant, becomes much brighter and can throw off its outer layers.

    How a star lives and dies depends mostly on its mass when it forms. Small stars live a very long time and fade quietly. A star like the Sun becomes a red giant, then a white dwarf. A very massive star lives fast and ends in a supernova explosion, leaving a neutron star or a black hole.

    Try it: choose a star mass, then step through its life. Watch the size, brightness and mass readouts.

    Investigation 3 · Cosmic calendar

    Squeeze 13.8 billion years into one year

    SP1.S3SP1.K6

    The history of the universe is hard to picture. Imagine it as one calendar year. The Big Bang is the first moment of January 1. Today is the last moment of December 31.

    day of the year = (time after the Big Bang ÷ 13.8 billion years) × 365
    Worked exampleThe solar system formed 4.6 billion years ago, which is 13.8 − 4.6 = 9.2 billion years after the Big Bang.9.2 ÷ 13.8 = 0.667. Then 0.667 × 365 = about 243.3 days.After 243 full days, the date is about September 1. The solar system is new on this calendar.

    Investigation 4 · Brightness calculator

    Which star looks brighter, and by how much?

    SP1.S4SP1.K7
    brightness ratio A ÷ B = (LA ÷ LB) × (dB ÷ dA)²
    Worked exampleStar A has 100 times the luminosity of star B. Star A is 5 times as far away.Luminosity part: 100. Distance part: (1 ÷ 5)² = 1 ÷ 25.100 ÷ 25 = 4, so star A looks 4 times as bright as star B.

    You do not need to memorize the formula. Remember the idea: more luminous means brighter, and twice as far means one quarter as bright.
