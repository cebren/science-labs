Resource Extraction Lab

Understanding: Geological resource extraction methods change Earth’s surface. Alberta sits on coal, oil sands, gravel, salt and more. Finding these resources, digging them up and caring for the land afterward all leave a mark that people must plan for.

Learn

Seven short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with what counts as a geological resource, find out why Alberta has the resources it has, then follow them from the ground into products, through exploration and mining, and on to caring for the land.

Lesson 1 · What counts as a resource?
ES2.K1

Geological resources

A geological resource is a material that formed naturally on or below Earth’s surface, has economic value, and can be extracted (taken out) for people to use. Economic value means people will pay for it.

Geological resources include fossil fuels such as coal, oil and natural gas, metal ores such as copper and iron ore, and industrial minerals and rock such as gravel, limestone and salt.

A material has to pass all four tests. Gravel buried under a city park is still gravel, but it is not a resource you can extract there. Iron in Earth’s core is real, but nobody can reach it.

Remember: natural, on or below the surface, worth money, and possible to extract.

Whether a deposit is worth extracting can change. If prices rise or new technology lowers costs, a deposit that was not worth mining can become one that is.

    Lesson 2 · Why here and not there?
    ES2.K2

    Resources are unevenly spread

    Geological resources are found only where past geological processes made them. That is why some places have plenty of a resource and other places have none.

    • Ancient seas covered Alberta several times. Tiny sea creatures were buried in mud and slowly changed into oil and natural gas. Reefs and shells became limestone. When shallow seas dried up, they left thick beds of salt.
    • Swamps along old coastlines buried thick layers of plants. Heat and pressure turned them into coal.
    • Glaciers and rivers dropped sand and gravel across the province during and after the last Ice Age.
    • Most metal ores, like copper, form from hot fluids moving through cracks in rock, often where mountains or volcanoes formed. Alberta’s plains have few of these, so most copper and iron used in Canada comes from places like British Columbia, Quebec and Labrador.

    Ages are rounded. Each layer holds clues, including fossils, that geologists use to predict where a resource might be.

    Lesson 3 · From rock to product
    ES2.K3

    Alberta resources become raw materials

    A raw material is a natural material used to make something else. Alberta’s fossil fuels and minerals supply raw materials for many products.

    Natural gas contains ethane and propane. Petrochemical plants near Red Deer and Fort Saskatchewan turn these into plastics such as polyethylene and polypropylene. Plastics end up in IV bags, tubing, syringes, gloves, food packaging and phone parts.

    A cell phone needs more than plastic. It uses copper wires, a lithium battery, gold on its contacts, and glass made from sand. Most of these metals are mined outside Alberta, so a phone is built from resources from many countries.

    Big idea: almost every manufactured product starts as something that was grown or something that was extracted from the ground.

    Lesson 4 · Changing the surface
    ES2.K4

    Surface mines and underground mines

    A surface mine removes the soil and rock on top, called overburden, to reach a deposit near the surface. Gravel pits, rock quarries, Alberta coal mines and oil sands mines are surface mines. They change the land over a wide area: forests, wetlands and farmland are cleared and big pits and waste piles are left until the land is reclaimed.

    An underground mine reaches a deep deposit through shafts and tunnels. It disturbs less land at the surface, but it costs more per tonne, needs strong supports and ventilation, and can leave waste rock piles and cause the ground above to sink.

    The choice depends mostly on how deep the deposit is and its shape, not only on what the material is. Coal and gravel are usually near the surface. Copper and iron ore come from both kinds of mine. Many of the world’s largest copper and iron mines are open pits, and deeper deposits are mined underground.

    Some resources are not dug at all. Oil and gas flow up wells. Deep oil sands are heated with steam underground so the thick oil can be pumped out (in situ means “in place”). Salt can be dissolved with water and pumped up.

    Lesson 5 · Finding resources
    ES2.K5

    Exploration: where is it, and how much?

    Exploration is the search for a resource before anyone mines it. Geologists use many methods, usually starting wide and cheap and ending narrow and expensive.

    • Surveying Earth’s surface: mapping rock outcrops, collecting rock and soil samples, and looking for fossils and minerals that hint at what lies below.
    • Geophysical surveys: instruments on the ground or in aircraft measure magnetism or gravity, or send sound waves down and record the echoes (seismic surveys).
    • Core sample drilling: a hollow drill bit cuts a long cylinder of rock called a core. Geologists log every layer and send samples to a lab to measure how much resource the rock holds.

    Only a few places that are explored ever become mines.

    Lesson 6 · Doing it responsibly
    ES2.K6

    Reducing environmental impacts in Alberta

    Extraction always has impacts. Responsible extraction uses methods and rules that make those impacts smaller. In Alberta, examples include:

    • Carbon capture and storage (CCS): catching carbon dioxide from a plant and storing it deep underground.
    • Methane emission reduction: finding and fixing leaks and capturing gas that would escape from wells and pipelines.
    • Water recycling and filtration: cleaning and reusing water so less is taken from rivers.
    • Tailings management: safely storing and treating the leftover water, sand and clay from processing, then reclaiming the land.

    Reclamation means returning disturbed land to a useful, stable landscape such as forest, wetland or farmland. Alberta law requires companies to reclaim land they disturb.

    Lesson 7 · Weighing the impacts
    ES2.S3Understanding

    Economic and environmental impacts

    Economic impacts are effects on money, jobs and businesses. Resource projects create jobs, pay taxes, and pay royalties, which are fees paid to the owner of the resource. In Alberta most mineral rights belong to the province, so royalties help pay for schools, hospitals and roads. Prices for resources rise and fall, so these benefits can rise and fall too.

    Environmental impacts are effects on land, water, air, wildlife and climate. They include cleared land, lost habitat, water use, dust, noise, waste, and greenhouse gas emissions.

    Different people feel different impacts. Good decisions listen to all of them, including nearby First Nations and Métis communities whose Treaty and Aboriginal rights must be respected. Governments have a legal duty to consult them about projects that may affect those rights.

    Investigation 3 · Ore math
    ES2.S3ES2.K4

    Grade, stripping ratio and profit

    Mining companies use three simple calculations to decide whether a deposit is worth mining. You do not need to memorize them.

    metal mass = ore mass × grade ÷ 100
    stripping ratio = waste rock mass ÷ ore mass
    profit = revenue − costs
    Worked example (made-up numbers) A pit produces 2 000 t of ore with a copper grade of 0.5%. Copper sells for $10 000 per tonne in this example. Copper: 2 000 t × 0.5 ÷ 100 = 10 t Revenue: 10 t × $10 000 = $100 000 The miners also removed 6 000 t of waste rock. Stripping ratio: 6 000 ÷ 2 000 = 3 (3 t of waste for every tonne of ore). If mining and processing cost $40 per tonne of ore, costs are 2 000 × $40 = $80 000. Profit: $100 000 − $80 000 = $20 000.
