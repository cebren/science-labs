AI Impact Lab

Understanding: AI impacts human tasks in a variety of ways. In this lab you will train a leaf sorter, watch a robot learn by trial and error, build a tiny text generator, test a chatbot, explore a video feed, and look at how AI is changing science, jobs and fairness.

Learn

Ten short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Artificial intelligence (AI) means computer systems that do tasks that usually need human thinking, like recognizing pictures, understanding speech or making predictions. All numbers in the models are made up for practice.

Lesson 1 · AI and efficiency
CS2.K1

AI can help people work more efficiently

A task is done more efficiently when it takes less time, effort or energy for the same result. AI helps in three main ways.

Information processing: AI can sort, search and summarize huge amounts of data, like thousands of photos, much faster than a person.

Decision making: AI can compare many options and suggest a choice, like the best order to plow streets after a snowfall. People still make the final call.

Task completion: AI can carry out parts of a job, like writing captions for a video or steering a tractor in straight rows.

Remember: AI usually helps people rather than replacing them. A person checks the work, handles the unusual cases and is responsible for the result.

Model: where does AI save time?

Pick a task. See which kind of help AI gives, what the person still does, and a made-up time comparison.

Times are made-up estimates for practice

Lesson 2 · Machine learning with and without labels
CS2.K2

Supervised and unsupervised learning

Machine learning is a kind of AI where a computer learns patterns from data instead of following only rules a person wrote.

In supervised learning, the AI is given labelled examples: data with the right answer attached. Here, each leaf is labelled aspen or willow. The AI uses the examples to label a new leaf it has never seen.

In unsupervised learning, there are no labels. The AI looks for patterns and groups similar data together. It can find two groups of leaves, but it cannot name them. A person decides what each group means.

Remember: labels in, labels out (supervised). No labels, find groups (unsupervised).

Model: leaf sorter

Leaf measurements are made up for practice

Lesson 3 · Learning by trial and error
CS2.K2

Reinforcement learning

In reinforcement learning, nobody gives the AI the right answers. The AI tries actions and gets a reward (points added) for good results and a penalty (points taken away) for bad ones. Over many tries it learns which actions earn the most reward.

This delivery robot must reach its charger. Each move costs 1 point, so short routes are better. Reaching the charger earns 10 points. Rolling onto the stairs costs 10 points and ends the try.

At first the robot moves almost at random. Train it and watch the arrows. Each arrow shows the move the robot now thinks is best from that square.

Remember: reinforcement learning means trial and error with rewards and penalties. Game-playing AI and some robots learn this way.

Model: train a delivery robot

Lesson 4 · Where bias comes from
CS2.K3

Bias can enter an AI system in four ways

Bias in AI means the system gives results that are unfair or less accurate for some people or situations. It can come from:

Developers: the people who build the AI choose what it should do, what data to collect and who to test it with.

Data sets: if the training data has many more examples of some groups than others, the AI learns those groups best.

Algorithms: the steps and goals in the program. An algorithm that aims for the best average score can still do poorly for a small group.

User input: what users type, click and rate can teach the AI. If many users feed it unfair or one-sided input, it can learn that too.

Model: a voice assistant’s training data

A voice assistant learns from recordings of people speaking. Change how many recordings come from speakers with accent A or accent B. Watch how well it understands each group.

Accuracy values come from a made-up model

Lesson 5 · Generative AI
CS2.K4

Generative AI makes new content

Generative AI produces new digital content such as text, images, videos and music. It learns patterns from a huge number of examples. Then it builds something new, one small piece at a time, by predicting what is likely to come next.

A text generator predicts the next word. An image generator predicts pixels. A music generator predicts notes or sounds.

The model here learned from only twelve sentences about Alberta weather. Real text generators learn from billions of sentences, but the idea of predicting the next piece is the same.

Remember: generative AI predicts what is likely, not what is true. Its content can sound right and still be wrong, so check it.

    Lesson 6 · Chatbots
    CS2.K5

    Chatbots hold conversations in real time

    A chatbot is a program that simulates a conversation with online users. It takes a question, works out what the person is asking, and gives an answer in real time, which means right away.

    Simple chatbots look for keywords and pick a stored answer. AI chatbots use a language model trained on huge amounts of text, so they can handle questions worded in many different ways and write new replies.

    A chatbot does not understand the way a person does. It finds patterns in words. It can give a confident answer that is wrong, so important answers should be checked.

    Model: science fair helper bot

    This is a simple keyword chatbot. Ask about the date, place, rules, judging or how to sign up. Then try wording a question in an unusual way.

    Lesson 7 · Recommendation algorithms
    CS2.K6

    Your feed learns from what you do

    A recommendation algorithm is an AI program that predicts content you may like. It uses your previous online activity: what you watched, clicked, liked, searched and how long you stayed.

    Streaming apps, online stores and social media all use them. They can help you find things you enjoy. They can also narrow what you see. When a feed shows more and more of the same thing, people call it a filter bubble.

    Lesson 8 · AI in science
    CS2.K7

    Scientists leverage AI

    Data analysis: AI finds patterns in data sets too big to check by hand, like sorting photos from wildlife cameras or finding signals in medical scans.

    Climate modelling: climate models are computer programs that simulate the atmosphere, oceans and land. AI can speed up parts of these models and help find patterns in weather and climate data.

    Classifying galaxies: telescopes take pictures of millions of galaxies. Volunteers in citizen science projects such as Galaxy Zoo have labelled galaxies by shape, and those labels help train AI to sort the rest.

    Galaxies are often sorted by shape: spiral (arms that wind out from the centre), elliptical (smooth oval) or irregular (no clear shape).

    Model: you and the AI classify galaxies

    Galaxies are drawn models, not real telescope images

    Lesson 9 · AI and jobs
    CS2.K8

    AI is changing work

    AI impacts jobs in three main ways.

    Automating repetitive tasks: jobs with steps that repeat the same way, like sorting, data entry or driving a fixed route, can be done partly by machines.

    New career opportunities: people are needed to build, train, test, repair and supervise AI systems.

    Changing skills: many workers now need to use AI tools, check AI results, and focus on skills AI does poorly, like caring for people, solving new problems and working in teams.

    Model: one job, three changes

    Teacher notes

    • CS2.K5: “understand questions” is taught with care. Chatbots process text and find patterns of meaning; they do not understand the way people do, and AI chatbots can give confident wrong answers. The lab’s model is a simple keyword chatbot (not AI) to show the idea, and the text explains that AI chatbots use a language model. Some chatbots are rule based and do not use AI at all.
    • CS2.K6: recommendation algorithms are taught as using the person’s previous activity, as the statement says. Many also use the activity of similar users and information about the content itself, and not every recommender uses machine learning. The feed model uses only the person’s taps.
    • CS2.K2: a penalty is taught as a negative reward. The leaf sorter uses a nearest neighbour method (supervised) and a k-means style grouping (unsupervised); these names are not taught to students.
    • CS2.K3: algorithm bias is taught as design choices in the steps and goals (for example, aiming for the best average). User input includes feedback loops, where what users click and rate trains the system.
    • CS2.K8: no job loss statistics are given. The mine haul truck example (some Alberta oil sands mines use self-driving haul trucks supervised from control rooms) is described generally; please verify wording.
    • CS2.K7: Galaxy Zoo is described generally as a citizen science project whose volunteer labels help train AI. Galaxy pictures in the lab are drawn models.
    • CS2.K9: one sentence names the OCAP principles of First Nations data governance under privacy. Please review the wording. Deepfakes and privacy can be sensitive topics; scenarios avoid real people.
    • Data: all times, accuracies, training counts, feed results and the science fair details are made up or simplified for practice and labelled as such.
