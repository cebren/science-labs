Patterns and Bias Lab

Big idea: Unbiased data can help accurately predict trends. Find the pattern between two variables, use it to predict, then check whether bias could be steering you to the wrong conclusion.

Five short sections, in order. Each one has a model to try. All data on this page is practice data, built to match patterns scientists really observe.

1. Two variables, one relationship

SL2.K1

A variable is anything you can measure or change. When you measure two variables together, you can plot each pair as a point on a scatter plot. The independent variable (the one you choose or change) goes on the x-axis. The dependent variable (the one that responds) goes on the y-axis.

If the points form a shape, the two variables have a relationship:

  • Positive: as x goes up, y tends to go up.
  • Negative: as x goes up, y tends to go down.
  • No relationship: the points are scattered with no upward or downward direction.

A trend line (line of best fit) runs through the middle of the points to show the general pattern.

2. Patterns help us predict, spot risks and decide

SL2.K1

Once you know how two variables are related, the pattern becomes a tool. It can help you:

  • Predict trends: estimate a value you did not measure.
  • Identify risks: notice when one variable is reaching a level linked to harm.
  • Make decisions: choose an action based on what the pattern says is likely.

A prediction inside the range of your data is called interpolation. A prediction outside that range is extrapolation. Extrapolation is riskier because the pattern might change where you have no data.

Critical thinking means questioning the evidence and the reasoning before you accept a conclusion. A pattern on a graph is a starting point, not the final answer.

4. How bias leads to wrong conclusions

SL2.K3

Bias is a tendency that pushes how data is collected or understood in one direction, away from a fair result. Bias can lead to incorrect conclusions in three main ways.

Route A: errors in reasoning

Thinking mistakes, such as noticing only evidence that agrees with you (confirmation bias), judging a whole group from a few examples, or deciding one thing causes another just because they change together.

Try it. Sam says, "Our class is always rowdy on full moon days." Compare what Sam remembers with the full record of 30 school days.

Route B: misinterpretation and inaccurate analysis

Mistakes in handling the data, such as picking only the part that supports your idea (cherry-picking), using a misleading graph scale, or stretching a trend line too far.

Try it. A Grade 8 step challenge ran for 12 weeks. Choose a window of weeks and see what trend it seems to show.

Route C: flawed decision making

When a biased conclusion is used to make a choice, the decision can waste money or cause harm. Follow the chain.

Biased dataBus riders on Route 8 are counted only on one stormy Friday in February.
Wrong conclusion"Hardly anyone rides Route 8."
Flawed decisionThe route is cut, and students who rely on it lose their way to school.
BetterCount riders on many days across seasons, then decide.

5. Unbiased data predicts better

Understanding · SL2.K2 · SL2.K3

Data is unbiased when it fairly represents the whole situation. Unbiased data does not guarantee a perfect prediction, but it makes the pattern far more likely to show what is really happening. Ways to collect fairer data:

  • Use a random sample, where everyone has an equal chance of being picked.
  • Collect enough data. Bigger samples even out chance differences.
  • Decide how you will measure before you start, and measure every trial the same way.
  • Keep all the data, including results that surprise you, and report outliers.
  • Ask someone else to check your graph and reasoning.

Try it. Your school has 400 students. Blue rings mark students who bike to school. Estimate the percentage who bike using two different survey methods.

No samples yet.

Take several samples with each method. Random samples land near the true value, and bigger samples land closer. Bike rack samples land too high every time, no matter how big they are. More biased data does not fix bias.
