Atom Builder Lab

Understanding: Atoms can be defined by their structure. Every atom has a tiny nucleus of protons and neutrons, with electrons around it. The number of protons tells you which element it is.

Learn

Five short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with how small atoms are, then open one up and see what is inside.

Lesson 1 · Everything is made of atoms

Zoom in far enough and you find atoms

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. Your desk, the air, the water in the North Saskatchewan River and your own body are all matter.

All of this matter is made of atoms. An atom is the smallest piece of an element that still has the properties of that element. An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom, such as carbon, oxygen or gold.

Atoms are very small. A single drop of water holds more than a thousand billion billion atoms. About half a million carbon atoms lined up side by side would just stretch across the width of one human hair.

Lesson 2 · Too small to see

You need technology to detect an atom

An atom is far too small to see with your eyes, and too small even for the microscope in our science room. A light microscope cannot show details smaller than about 200 nanometres. A nanometre (nm) is one billionth of a metre. Most atoms are only about 0.1 to 0.5 nm wide.

To detect single atoms, scientists use technology that does not depend on visible light. A scanning tunnelling microscope moves an extremely sharp metal tip just above a surface and measures a tiny electric current. A computer turns those measurements into a map of the atoms. An atomic force microscope feels the surface with a tiny tip, a bit like reading braille.

Researchers at the University of Alberta in Edmonton use these microscopes to study and even move single atoms on silicon surfaces.

Lesson 3 · Inside an atom

Protons, neutrons and electrons

Atoms are made of even smaller pieces called subatomic particles. There are three kinds.

ParticleChargeWhere it isRelative mass
Protonpositive (+1)nucleus1
Neutronno charge (0)nucleus1
Electronnegative (−1)shells around the nucleusabout 1/1836

The nucleus is the tiny, dense centre of the atom. It holds the protons and neutrons, so it holds almost all of the atom's mass. If the nucleus were the size of a blueberry, the whole atom would be about a kilometre wide. Most of an atom is empty space.

An atom is electrically neutral. It has the same number of electrons as protons, so the positive and negative charges cancel out.

Lesson 4 · Atomic models

A model is our best picture so far

No one has ever looked at an atom the way you look at a ball. So scientists build an atomic model. An atomic model is a theoretical representation of an atom's structure. Theoretical means it is based on evidence and reasoning, not on a direct photo.

When new evidence appears, scientists improve the model. Each model on the timeline explained something the older one could not. Scientists from many countries added pieces. Ernest Rutherford did his first prize-winning work on radioactivity at McGill University in Montréal, where Harriet Brooks, one of Canada's first nuclear physicists, worked with him.

Lesson 5 · The Bohr model

Electrons fill shells in order

In 1913, Niels Bohr suggested that electrons travel around the nucleus in layers called shells. Each shell holds a set number of electrons. Electrons fill the shell closest to the nucleus first.

  • The first shell holds a maximum of 2 electrons.
  • The second shell holds a maximum of 8 electrons.
  • The third shell holds 8 electrons for the atoms you study this year.

We write the number in each shell as an electron arrangement. Sodium has 11 electrons, so its arrangement is 2, 8, 1.

The Bohr model is a useful picture, but it is simplified. Today scientists describe electrons as a cloud where they are likely to be found, not as tiny planets on fixed paths. In bigger atoms, the third shell can hold up to 18 electrons.
