Science 8 · Energy: Stores, Static and Waves · EN2

Static Charge Lab

Understanding: Static electricity accumulates and can be discharged, which results in the transfer of energy. On a dry January day in Edmonton, a snap from a doorknob and a summer thunderstorm over the prairie come from the same idea: electrons pile up in one place, then rush back.

Learn

Seven short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with what charge is, then see how rubbing moves electrons, how charges push and pull, how a spark sends them back, and how the same idea powers machines and lightning.

Lesson 1 · Counting charges
EN2.K1

Static electricity is a charge imbalance

All matter is made of atoms. In the centre of each atom are protons, which have a positive (+) charge. Around the centre move electrons, which have a negative (−) charge. One proton and one electron carry the same amount of charge.

Most objects have exactly as many electrons as protons. The charges cancel, so the object is neutral.

Protons are locked inside the centre of the atom. Electrons on the outside can be moved. When an object gains extra electrons, it becomes negatively charged. When it loses electrons, it has more protons than electrons, so it becomes positively charged.

Static electricity (also called electrostatic charge) is a build-up of charge that stays on the surface of a material. Static means staying still: the extra or missing electrons stay where they are until something lets them move.

Remember: only electrons move. Negative means extra electrons. Positive means missing electrons.

Lesson 2 · Rub, then separate
EN2.K2EN2.S2

Rubbing moves electrons from one surface to another

When two different materials touch and are pulled apart, some electrons move from one surface to the other. Rubbing makes many more points of contact, so more electrons move.

  • The material that loses electrons becomes positively charged.
  • The material that gains electrons becomes negatively charged.

The two charges are equal in size. No charge is made or destroyed. Electrons only move from one place to another.

Which material gains electrons? Scientists list materials in a rubbing series (the triboelectric series). A material lower on the list holds electrons more tightly, so it takes electrons from a material higher on the list.

Real results change with humidity, dirt and how hard you rub. Different books order some materials a little differently. Use the list as a guide, not a rule.

Lesson 3 · Push and pull
EN2.K3EN2.S2

How charged materials interact

Charged objects push or pull on each other without touching. There are three rules.

  • Like charges repel. Two positives push apart. Two negatives push apart.
  • Unlike charges attract. A positive and a negative pull together.
  • A charged object attracts a neutral object. This happens by induction, which you will see in Lesson 4.

The force gets stronger as the objects get closer. Two balloons rubbed on the same hair both become negative, so they push away from each other.

Test for charge: attraction can happen with a neutral object, but only two charged objects with the same charge can repel. Repelling is the sure sign that an object is charged.

Lesson 4 · Charged meets neutral
EN2.K3

Induction: why a balloon sticks to a wall

A neutral object has equal positive and negative charge. So why does a charged balloon pull on it?

Bring a negative balloon close. Its extra electrons push on the electrons in the neutral object.

  • In a conductor, such as a metal can, electrons move easily. Many of them slide to the far side. The near side is left positive.
  • In an insulator, such as paper or a wall, electrons cannot travel far. Inside each atom they shift a tiny bit away from the balloon. The near surface ends up slightly positive.

The object is still neutral overall. The charge has only been rearranged. This is called an induced charge. The near side is closer to the balloon than the far side, so the pull wins over the push. The neutral object is attracted.

Remember: induction rearranges charge. It does not add or remove electrons.

Lesson 5 · Let it go
EN2.K4EN2.S1

Discharge: sparks send electrons back

Charge built up on an insulator stays put. To get rid of it, electrons need a path. A conductor, such as metal or your body, gives them that path.

Shuffle across a carpet in socks and you can pick up extra electrons. Reach for a metal doorknob. Just before your finger touches, the electrons jump across the tiny air gap. That jump is a spark. Losing the extra charge this way is called discharge.

A spark is an energy transfer. The energy stored in the separated charges is transferred as light (the flash), sound (the snap) and heating of the air. You feel it because the moving charge stimulates nerves in your finger.

Charge can also leak away quietly. Damp air carries charge away slowly, which is why shocks are much more common in dry Alberta winters than in humid summers. A wire to the ground, called grounding, lets charge flow away safely.

Safety: at the gas station, touch a metal part of the car away from the nozzle before you pick up the pump. This discharges you safely before you get near fuel vapour.

Lesson 6 · Static at work
EN2.K1EN2.K3

Machines that use static electricity

Static electricity is not only a nuisance. Engineers use the attraction between unlike charges to move tiny particles exactly where they want them.

  • Laser printers place toner powder in the shape of letters.
  • Paint sprayers pull paint droplets onto a car body or a bike frame.
  • Air filters pull dust and smoke out of air, from home furnace filters to factory smokestacks.

Each one follows the same plan: give particles a charge, then use an oppositely charged surface to attract them.

    Lesson 7 · Nature’s biggest spark
    EN2.K5EN2.S3

    Lightning is static discharge

    On a hot summer afternoon over the Alberta prairie, tall thunderclouds (cumulonimbus clouds) build up. Strong rising air carries water droplets and ice up through the cloud.

    Inside, small ice crystals rise while heavier soft hail, called graupel, falls. When they collide, electrons move between them. The light ice crystals usually end up positive and are carried to the top of the cloud. The graupel usually ends up negative and gathers lower in the cloud.

    The negative cloud base repels electrons in the ground below, so the ground surface becomes positive by induction. When the charge difference is big enough, the air can no longer insulate. A giant spark jumps: lightning. Most lightning stays inside a cloud or goes between clouds. Some strikes the ground.

    The flash heats the air to about 30 000 °C, which is hotter than the surface of the Sun. The air expands so fast that it makes the boom we call thunder.

    Safety: when thunder roars, go indoors. Stay inside until 30 minutes after the last thunder you hear.

    Investigation 2 · EN2.S2

    The sticky tape test

    Press one strip of tape onto a table (the bottom tape, B). Press a second strip on top of it (the top tape, T). Pull them apart quickly. One strip pulls electrons from the other, so T and B end up with opposite charges.

    Hang the strips from a ruler. Predict what each pair will do, then test it. A strip of paper (P) is neutral.

    Why this works: repelling only happens between like charges. Attraction happens with unlike charges or with a neutral object.

    Investigation 3 · EN2.S1 · EN2.K2

    Count the charge

    The net charge of an object compares its protons and electrons:

    net charge = number of protons − number of electrons

    A positive answer means missing electrons. A negative answer means extra electrons. Zero means neutral. Real objects have trillions of particles, so the numbers here are tiny made-up counts for practice.

    Worked exampleA balloon has 6000 protons and 6000 electrons. Rubbed on hair, it gains 250 electrons.Electrons: 6000 + 250 = 6250Net charge: 6000 − 6250 = −250, so the balloon is negative.The hair lost 250 electrons, so its net charge is +250.

    Investigation 4 · EN2.S3 · EN2.K5

    Flash to bang: how far is the storm?

    Light from a lightning flash reaches you almost instantly. Thunder travels at the speed of sound, about 340 metres per second. Count the seconds from the flash to the bang and you can find the distance:

    distance = speed of sound × time
    d = v × t  (v ≈ 340 m/s)

    A quick rule: about 3 seconds for every kilometre.

    Worked exampleYou count 9 seconds from flash to thunder.d = 340 m/s × 9 s = 3060 m, about 3.1 km.If you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck. Go indoors.

    Teacher notes

    • EN2.K1: the draft defines static electricity as “the accumulation of electrons on the surface of a material”. The lab teaches it as a build-up of charge imbalance on a surface: extra electrons give a negative charge and missing electrons give a positive charge. Students are told directly that positive charge never comes from gaining protons. The equipment list (laser printers, paint sprayers, air filters) is described in simplified steps in Lesson 6.
    • EN2.K2: rubbing is taught as contact and separation that moves electrons between surfaces, with rubbing increasing contact. Charge is conserved: the two objects get equal and opposite charges. The rubbing series used (dry skin, glass, hair, nylon, wool, silk, paper, cotton, rubber balloon, polyester, plastic wrap, vinyl, Teflon) is a common classroom order; sources disagree on some positions and real results depend on humidity and surface condition. Walking on carpet is modelled as the person gaining electrons, which depends on the shoe and carpet materials.
    • EN2.K3: “charged objects are attracted to neutral objects (induction)” is taught as induced charge separation. In conductors, electrons move across the object. In insulators, charge shifts within atoms and molecules (polarization, named only once). The near side is oppositely charged and closer, so attraction outweighs repulsion. The object stays neutral overall. The attraction strength meter is a qualitative model only.
    • EN2.K4: the draft says discharge happens “when a conductor touches the material”. The lab teaches that the spark jumps across a small air gap just before contact, and that charge can also leak away without a spark (humid air, grounding). The spark is taught as an energy transfer by light, sound and heating, linking to the understanding statement. Gas station advice reflects common Canadian safety guidance; please verify current wording.
    • EN2.K5 and EN2.S3: charge separation is taught with the ice crystal and graupel collision model, which is the leading explanation and is still researched. Cloud tops are usually positive and the lower cloud usually negative; real storms are more complex. Lightning temperature is rounded to about 30 000 °C (sources give roughly 20 000 °C to 30 000 °C). Flash to bang uses 340 m/s and about 3 seconds per kilometre. The safety rule (indoors when thunder roars, wait 30 minutes) matches Environment and Climate Change Canada guidance; please verify.
    • Numbers: electron counts, carpet charge units, spark gaps and tape test outcomes are made-up model values for practice and are labelled that way. Lightning can be a sensitive topic for students who have experienced storms, fires or injuries.
