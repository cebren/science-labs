Lesson 1

Four kinds of scientific knowledge

SL1.K1

Scientific knowledge is more than a list of facts. It includes four kinds of knowledge that work together:

  • Fact an observation that has been confirmed again and again.
  • Law a description of what always happens under certain conditions.
  • Theory an explanation of why or how it happens.
  • Model a representation that helps explain or predict.

Choose a phenomenon (something that happens in nature) to see all four kinds of knowledge about it.

Lesson 2

Facts: confirmed, but open to revision

SL1.K2

When an observation is repeatedly confirmed by many people, with different tools, in different places, scientists may accept it as a scientific fact.

Facts are strong, but they are not locked forever. Better tools or new evidence can lead scientists to revise or reject a fact.

Example: for about 30 years, textbooks stated that human body cells have 48 chromosomes. In 1956, Joe Hin Tjio and Albert Levan used a better technique to spread out and count chromosomes. They counted 46. Other labs confirmed it, and the accepted fact was revised.

Lesson 3

What laws and theories share

SL1.K3

Laws and theories are different jobs, but they have a lot in common. Both are:

  • widely accepted by the scientific community
  • supported by large bodies of evidence from many investigations
  • equally important: neither one ranks above the other
  • used to make predictions about phenomena
  • testable: scientists can design investigations that could show them to be wrong

In everyday talk, "theory" can mean a hunch. In science, a theory is one of the strongest kinds of knowledge there is.

Lesson 4

Scientific laws describe

SL1.K4

A scientific law describes what happens, every time, under stated conditions. It does not explain why.

A law can be written in three ways:

  • Cause and effect: Newton's third law. When object A pushes on object B, object B pushes back on A with an equal force in the opposite direction.
  • Rule: the law of superposition. In undisturbed rock layers, lower layers are older than the layers above them.
  • Equation: the law of reflection. The angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence: θr = θi

Laws are considered universal: the law of reflection works for a mirror in Edmonton, a lake surface in Jasper, and a telescope mirror in space. Each law states its conditions, such as "undisturbed layers".

Lesson 5

Scientific theories explain

SL1.K5

A scientific theory explains why or how phenomena happen. A theory pulls together, or integrates, a wide range of knowledge: many facts, several laws, and evidence from different branches of science.

The theory of plate tectonics says Earth's outer layer is broken into plates that move slowly over the hotter rock beneath. That one explanation connects earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain building, fossils and the shape of continents.

Canadian geophysicist J. Tuzo Wilson helped build the theory in the 1960s by explaining transform faults and hot spots.

Lesson 6

Models explain and predict

SL1.K6SL1.K7

A scientific model is a representation that helps explain or predict a phenomenon. Models can be diagrams, physical objects, equations or computer simulations.

Scientists use models to test hypotheses while building theories. The Big Bang theory explains that the universe began very hot and dense about 13.8 billion years ago and has been expanding ever since.

Belgian physicist and priest Georges Lemaître, who wrote in French, proposed an expanding universe in 1927. Models of an expanding universe predicted that distant galaxies should move away faster than nearby ones. Edwin Hubble's data in 1929 matched. Models also predicted leftover heat from the early universe. It was detected in 1965 as the cosmic microwave background.

Every model has limits. In the balloon model, galaxies sit on a 2D surface, but the real universe has three dimensions of space.

Lesson 7

Theories do not grow up into laws

SL1.K8

A common myth says a hypothesis becomes a theory, and a theory becomes a law once it is proven. That is not how science works.

Laws and theories are different kinds of knowledge that answer different questions. A theory cannot change into a law, and a law cannot change into a theory. No amount of evidence turns an explanation into a description.

Example: Newton's law of universal gravitation describes how strongly objects attract each other. Einstein's general theory of relativity explains gravity as mass curving space and time. Both are still used today. Neither one turned into the other.

Station 3

Predict with a model: gases in an Alberta winter

SL1.S3

The particle model of gases, part of kinetic molecular theory, says gas particles move faster when warmer. At constant pressure, faster particles push outward harder and the gas takes up more space. This matches Charles's law, discovered by French scientist Jacques Charles: at constant pressure, the volume of a gas is proportional to its temperature in kelvin.

V₂ = V₁ × T₂ ÷ T₁   (T in kelvin: K = °C + 273)

Worked example: a 3.0 L balloon at 22 °C (295 K) is taken outside at −30 °C (243 K). V₂ = 3.0 L × 243 ÷ 295 = 2.5 L. The balloon shrinks.

You do not need to memorize this formula. Use it as a model to make predictions.
