Science 8 · Life Science: Cells and Organ Systems · LS3

Body Systems Lab

Understanding: The interdependent functions of organ systems influence the overall health of an individual. Your body is built in levels, from single cells up to whole organ systems. Those systems depend on each other every second. When one of them is not working well, the others feel it, and doctors use tests like X-rays and blood tests to find out what is going on.

Learn

Eight short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with how the body is built in levels, meet specialized cells and stem cells, then explore the seven organ systems, how they depend on each other, and how doctors check on them.

Lesson 1 · Levels of organization
LS3.K1LS3.S1

From one cell to a whole organism

A bacterium or an amoeba is a whole organism made of one cell. You are a complex organism: you are made of trillions of cells, and they are organized in levels. Each level is built from the one before it.

Cells are the basic unit of life. A group of similar cells working together on one job is a tissue, such as muscle tissue. An organ is made of two or more kinds of tissue working together, such as the heart. An organ system is a group of organs that work together on a major job, such as the circulatory system. All your organ systems together make the organism: you.

Remember: cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism. Each level is made of many parts from the level before it.

Lesson 2 · Specialized cells
LS3.K2LS3.S3

Every cell shape has a job

In a complex organism, most cells are specialized cells. A specialized cell has a shape and set of parts that suit one particular job. It does that job very well, but it cannot do the jobs of other cells.

A nerve cell is long and thin with branches, so it can carry signals over long distances and connect with many other cells. A red blood cell is a small, flexible disc with no nucleus. That leaves more room for hemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen, and lets the cell squeeze through tiny blood vessels.

Look for the link: in each specialized cell, the shape and parts fit the job.

Lesson 3 · Stem cells
LS3.K3LS3.S3

Cells that have not chosen a job yet

Stem cells are non-specialized cells. They have no special job yet. When a stem cell divides, it can make more stem cells, or it can make cells that become specialized. This process of becoming specialized is called differentiation.

Stem cells in an early embryo can become almost any type of cell in the body. Stem cells found in a child or adult, such as the blood stem cells in bone marrow, can make only a few related kinds of cells. Blood stem cells make red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets, millions every second.

In 1961, James Till and Ernest McCulloch, two scientists in Toronto, showed that blood stem cells exist. Their work helped make bone marrow transplants possible, which can rebuild the blood of a person with some kinds of cancer.

Compare: a specialized cell does one job well. A stem cell does no special job, but it can divide and become the cells the body needs.

Lesson 4 · Seven organ systems
LS3.K4LS3.S2

A tour of the body’s organ systems

This unit studies seven human organ systems. The digestive system breaks food into nutrients. The respiratory system takes in oxygen and gets rid of carbon dioxide. The circulatory system moves blood around the body. The muscular system moves the body. The skeletal system supports and protects it. The nervous system senses and controls. The excretory system removes wastes from the blood.

Your body has a few more systems as well, such as the immune system that fights germs and the endocrine system that makes hormones. You will meet them in later grades.

Lesson 5 · Systems depend on each other
LS3.K4

No system works alone

Organ systems are interdependent: each one needs the others to do its job. Think about a muscle cell in your leg. It needs oxygen from the respiratory system and nutrients from the digestive system. The circulatory system delivers both. The nervous system tells the muscle when to contract, and the muscle pulls on bones of the skeletal system to move you. The wastes the cell makes are carried away to the lungs and kidneys.

When you exercise, your muscles work harder and need more oxygen. Your nervous system speeds up your breathing and your heart, so more oxygen reaches the muscles and more carbon dioxide is removed.

blood pumped per minute = heart rate × volume per beat
air breathed per minute = breathing rate × volume per breath

You do not need to memorize these. They show how two systems speed up together.

Lesson 6 · When a system is not working properly
LS3.S4LS3.K4

One problem, many effects

Because organ systems depend on each other, a problem in one system usually affects others too. This is why the health of each system matters to the health of the whole person.

To predict the effects, ask three questions. What job does this system normally do? Which other systems need that job done? What will those systems do differently? Often the body tries to make up for the problem. For example, if blood carries less oxygen, the heart beats faster.

These examples are general science, not medical advice. Some classmates may live with conditions like these, so speak about them with care.

Lesson 7 · Looking inside: X-rays
LS3.K5LS3.S5

How an X-ray sees bones

A diagnostic medical procedure is a test that helps health workers find out how well an organ system is working, or find a disease or injury. X-rays are one of the most common.

An X-ray machine sends invisible rays through the body to a detector on the other side. Soft tissues like skin and muscle let most X-rays pass through, so those areas look dark. Dense bone absorbs more X-rays, so bones look white. A break in a bone shows up as a dark line across the white.

X-rays are a kind of radiation, so health workers use the smallest amount needed and cover other parts of the body. A CT scan combines many X-ray images into slices of the body.

Lesson 8 · Blood tests and other procedures
LS3.K5LS3.S5

Clues in a small sample of blood

Blood travels to every organ, so a small sample can tell a lot about many systems. A blood test counts the different blood cells and measures substances like sugar and iron. Each result is compared to a typical range. A result outside the range is a clue, not a diagnosis. Doctors combine it with other tests and with how the person feels.

Other procedures check other systems. An ultrasound uses sound echoes to make pictures of soft organs. An MRI uses strong magnets and radio waves. An ECG records the heart’s electrical signals. A spirometry test measures how much air you can blow out. A urine test shows how the kidneys are working.

Investigation 5 · Heart and lung numbers
LS3.K4LS3.S4

How much blood, how much air?

The circulatory and respiratory systems speed up together. You can estimate how much blood the heart pumps and how much air the lungs move each minute.

B = h × v
B: blood pumped per minute (mL/min) · h: heart rate (beats/min) · v: volume per beat (mL)
A = r × t
A: air breathed per minute (L/min) · r: breathing rate (breaths/min) · t: volume per breath (L)
Worked exampleAt rest, a student’s heart beats 70 times a minute and pumps 70 mL per beat.B = 70 × 70 mL = 4900 mL/min, about 4.9 L each minute.That is close to all of the blood in an adult body every minute.

Teacher notes

  • LS3.K1: the draft’s stray comma (“Complex organisms, exhibit”) is dropped. An organ is defined as two or more kinds of tissue working together, and unicellular organisms are named as the contrast. The heart chain uses cardiac muscle tissue; nerve and lining tissues are named simply.
  • LS3.K2: six specialized cells are shown (nerve, red blood, skeletal muscle, ciliated airway, white blood, fat). The red blood cell’s missing nucleus links back to LS1. Drawings are not to scale.
  • LS3.K3: “can specialize into any type of cell” is taught more precisely. Embryonic stem cells can become almost any cell type; adult stem cells, such as blood stem cells in bone marrow, make only a few related types. Till and McCulloch (Toronto, 1961) are named as Canadian context; please check the wording. Embryonic stem cell ethics are not discussed.
  • LS3.K4: the seven listed systems are taught. Excretory is taught mainly as kidneys, ureters, bladder and urethra, with lungs and skin noted as also removing wastes. Immune and endocrine systems are named once as context only; the reproductive system is not covered.
  • LS3.S4 and K5: health conditions (asthma, anemia, kidney disease, fracture, stomach flu, concussion, smoking damage, torn hamstring) are described in general terms, not as medical advice. Students may live with these conditions, so a sensitivity note appears in Learn and Lab Bench.
  • Numbers: heart rates, breathing rates, about 70 mL per beat and about 0.5 L per breath are rounded typical values. Blood test ranges (hemoglobin, white cells, platelets, glucose, iron) are rounded adult values and vary by lab, age and sex. The blood report, cases and practice problems are made up.
