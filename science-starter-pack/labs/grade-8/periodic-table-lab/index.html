Science 8 · Matter: Atoms and the Periodic Table · M2

Periodic Table Lab

Understanding: Information about elements and their properties can be determined by using the periodic table. Every element has a place set by its atomic number, and that place tells you about its atoms and how it behaves.

Learn

Six short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with what an element is, then learn to read every part of the periodic table.

Lesson 1 · What is an element?

One type of atom, and no simpler substance inside

An element is a pure substance made of only one type of atom. Copper wire is made only of copper atoms. The helium in a party balloon is made only of helium atoms.

An element cannot be broken down into other substances by physical means, such as melting, filtering or boiling. It also cannot be broken down by chemical means, such as burning or passing electricity through it. If you melt copper, you get liquid copper. It is still copper.

A compound is different. Water is made of hydrogen and oxygen atoms joined together. Passing electricity through water splits it into hydrogen gas and oxygen gas, so water is not an element.

Sometimes atoms of one element join in pairs, like the oxygen you breathe (O₂). It is still an element, because every atom in it is oxygen.

Lesson 2 · Names and symbols

Every element has a name and a symbol

Each element is given a name and a chemical symbol. A symbol is one or two letters. The first letter is always a capital and the second is always lower case. Co is the element cobalt. CO, with two capitals, is carbon monoxide, a compound of carbon and oxygen.

Many symbols come from the English name, like O for oxygen. Some come from old Latin names. Iron is Fe from ferrum, and gold is Au from aurum.

Today, a group of chemists from around the world called IUPAC (the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) approves new names. The scientists who make a new element suggest a name. The public can comment, and then IUPAC makes it official. New elements can be named after a place, a scientist, a myth, a mineral or a property.

Lesson 3 · Atomic number

The number of protons decides the element

Every atom of the same element has the same number of protons. This number is the element's atomic number. Every carbon atom has 6 protons, so carbon's atomic number is 6.

If you could add one proton to a carbon atom, it would no longer be carbon. With 7 protons, it would be nitrogen. Atoms of one element can have different numbers of neutrons, but they always have the same number of protons.

In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons. So the atomic number also tells you how many electrons a neutral atom has.

Atomic number = number of protons = number of electrons in a neutral atom

Lesson 4 · Reading a tile

What each box on the table tells you

The periodic table arranges all the elements in order of atomic number, from hydrogen (1) to oganesson (118). Each element has its own box, often called a tile.

A tile usually shows the atomic number, symbol and name. It also shows the atomic mass, which is the average mass of the element's atoms, measured in atomic mass units (u). Many tables also show the element's state of matter at room temperature: solid, liquid or gas.

Only two elements are liquids at room temperature: mercury and bromine. Eleven are gases. Almost all the rest are solids.

In 1869, Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the known elements by atomic mass and left gaps for elements no one had found yet. In 1913, Henry Moseley showed that ordering by atomic number works even better. That is the order we use today.

Lesson 5 · Groups and periods

Columns and rows follow the electrons

A column on the periodic table is called a group. There are 18 groups. In groups 1, 2 and 13 to 18, the elements in a group have the same number of electrons in their outermost shell. This shell is the valence shell, and its electrons are valence electrons.

Lithium, sodium and potassium are all in group 1. Each has 1 valence electron. That is why they react in similar ways.

A row is called a period. Elements in the same period have the same number of electron shells. Sodium and chlorine are both in period 3, so both have 3 shells.

Group → valence electrons. Group 1 has 1, group 2 has 2, group 13 has 3, up to group 18 with 8.
Period → number of shells.

Helium is the one exception in these groups. It has only 2 electrons, but it sits in group 18 because its only shell is full, like the other gases in that group.

Lesson 6 · Metals and non-metals

A staircase line splits the table

The organization of the table lets you tell metals from non-metals. A zigzag staircase line starts near boron and steps down to the right. Metals are on the left of the line and non-metals are on the right. Most elements are metals.

Hydrogen is the one surprise. It sits at the top of group 1, but it is a non-metal gas. Elements right beside the staircase, such as silicon, are called metalloids. They have some properties of metals and some of non-metals.

    Station 4 · Predict a missing element

    Think like Mendeleev

    Mendeleev left gaps in his table and predicted the elements that would fill them. You can estimate a missing atomic mass from the elements on each side of the gap.

    Worked example: germanium (Ge, atomic number 32) In 1871, germanium was still unknown. Mendeleev called the gap "eka-silicon". estimate = (mass on the left + mass on the right) ÷ 2 estimate = (69.72 + 74.92) ÷ 2 = 72.32 u Germanium was found in 1886. Its real atomic mass is 72.63 u, very close to the estimate.
