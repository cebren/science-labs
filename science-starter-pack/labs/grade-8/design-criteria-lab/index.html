Design Criteria Lab

Understanding: Design criteria and testing can help turn ideas into solutions. In this lab you design, test and improve a syringe hydraulic arm, the kind of build many Alberta Grade 8 classes make in the fluids unit.

Learn

Five short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. You will turn a need into clear criteria, work within constraints, test a prototype, read the results and plan an improvement.

Lesson 1 · What counts as success?
CS3.K1

Design criteria are the requirements a solution must meet

Every design starts with a need, a want or a problem. Your class needs a hydraulic arm that can move a small load from one desk spot to another. That is the need. It is not yet clear enough to build or test.

Design criteria are the specific requirements a solution must meet so the need, want or problem is really addressed. Good criteria are specific and, where possible, measurable. “The arm should be strong” is vague. “The arm lifts a 100 g load at least 10 cm” can be checked.

Some criteria are musts: the solution fails without them. Others are wants: nice to have, but not required.

Key idea: criteria describe what success looks like, in words you can check with a test.

Make it measurable

Pick a vague criterion. Then choose the rewrite that you could check with a test.

Hydraulic arm criteria

  • Lifts a 100 g load at least 10 cm Must
  • Moves a paper cup 20 cm sideways in under 30 s Must
  • Loses no more than 5 drops of water after 20 open and close cycles Must
  • Fits on a desk: base under 30 cm × 30 cm Must
  • Looks tidy enough to show at the open house Want
Lesson 2 · Working within limits
CS3.K2

Constraints limit a design

A constraint is a limit you must work within. Common constraints are budget, available materials, time, tools, safety rules and size. Criteria say what the solution must do. Constraints say what you have to do it with.

Constraints can have negative impacts. A tiny budget might force weaker materials, so the design breaks sooner. They can also have positive impacts on innovation. When you cannot buy the usual part, you may invent a smarter, cheaper or lighter one.

A famous exampleIn 1970, the crew of Apollo 13 had to shelter in the small lunar module after an explosion damaged their spacecraft. Carbon dioxide began to build up. The command module’s square air filter canisters did not fit the lunar module’s round openings. Engineers on the ground could only use items the crew had on board, such as plastic bags, cardboard, hoses and tape. Working within that strict constraint, they designed an adapter the crew could build, and the crew made it home safely.

Lesson 3 · Put it to the test
CS3.K3

Testing shows whether criteria were met

A solution can be tested to find out if it meets each design criterion. A good test matches the criterion: if the criterion is about lifting 100 g at least 10 cm, you measure the height the load is lifted.

A fair test changes only what you are testing. Everything else stays the same: the same load, the same starting position, the same operator, the same amount of water in the syringes. These are controlled variables.

Results vary a little each time, so testers run repeated trials, usually at least three, and use the average. One lucky or unlucky trial can mislead you.

Lesson 4 · Read the results
CS3.K4

Data shows strengths and weaknesses

After testing, you analyze the data. Compare each average with its target. A result that beats the target is a strength. A result that misses it is a weakness, and a weakness on a must criterion means the design is not ready yet.

Look for patterns too. A prototype can be the strongest lifter but the slowest mover. Comparing several prototypes on the same criteria helps a team see which ideas are worth keeping.

Watch the direction of each target. For lift height, higher is better. For time and number of leaks, lower is better.

Lesson 5 · Improve and retest
CS3.K3CS3.K4

Use the weakness to plan the next version

Test data tells you where to focus. Prototype B lifts well and does not leak, but it is too slow to move the cup. An improvement should target that weakness.

Every change has trade-offs. In a syringe hydraulic system, a larger output syringe pushes with more force but moves a shorter distance. Fixing one criterion can weaken another, so you retest after every change.

Investigation 3 · CS3.S3

Data analyzer: strength or weakness?

Work out the average of the trials, compare it with the target, then choose the best improvement. Each problem is randomly generated practice data.

Worked example Prototype C, criterion: moves a cup 20 cm in under 30 s. Trials: 26 s, 29 s, 32 s. average = (26 + 29 + 32) ÷ 3 = 87 ÷ 3 = 29 s 29 s is under 30 s, so this criterion is met, but only just. One trial (32 s) failed, so the result is close to the limit. That is a borderline strength worth watching.

Investigation 4 · CS3.S3 and CS3.K4

Decision matrix: which prototype goes forward?

Teacher notes

  • CS3.K1: criteria are taught as specific and, where possible, measurable requirements. The lab adds the must and want distinction (sometimes called requirements and desirables) to help students rank criteria. This is a common classroom extension, not a draft statement.
  • CS3.K2: the line between criteria and constraints is not sharp in practice. Some engineering sources list constraints as a kind of requirement. The lab teaches a working distinction: criteria say what success looks like, constraints are limits on how you can reach it (budget, materials, time, tools, safety, size). Sort It items were chosen to be clear cases.
  • CS3.K2: the Apollo 13 carbon dioxide adapter (April 1970) is used as a positive impact of constraints on innovation. It is described generally; please check the wording suits your class.
  • CS3.K3: testing is taught as a fair test with controlled variables and repeated trials (at least three), building on the Science 7 design process (CS3 in Grade 7). User testing is mentioned only briefly.
  • CS3.K4: analysis uses the mean (average), comparison with a target, and the direction of a target (higher or lower is better). The weighted decision matrix in Investigation 4 goes slightly beyond the statement as a tool for comparing prototypes.
  • Context: the syringe hydraulic arm borrows from the Forces, Motion and Fluids unit. The force and distance trade-off (larger output syringe gives more force but less movement) and air being more compressible than water are stated qualitatively only.
  • Numbers: all prices, trial results, prototype averages and decision matrix scores are made up for practice and labelled as such.
