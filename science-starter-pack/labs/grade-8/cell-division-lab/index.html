Cell Division Lab

Understanding: Cell division is essential for survival and diversity. Every cell in a spruce seedling, a canola plant or your own body came from another cell that divided. One kind of division copies cells exactly so living things can grow and heal. The other kind mixes things up so offspring are all a little different.

Learn

Seven short lessons in order. Each one has a model to try. Start with what DNA is and where cells keep it, then find out why cells divide and how the two kinds of division work.

Lesson 1 · The instruction molecule
LS2.K1

DNA carries the cell’s instructions

DNA is short for deoxyribonucleic acid. It is a very long molecule that carries a cell’s genetic information. You can think of it as an instruction book written in a code of four chemical letters: A, T, G and C.

A section of DNA that holds the instructions for one job, such as making one protein, is called a gene. The cell reads its genes to build the proteins it needs to take in food, release energy, repair itself and divide. Without its DNA, a cell cannot keep itself alive for long.

DNA is shaped like a twisted ladder, called a double helix. In 1953, Rosalind Franklin’s X-ray images helped James Watson and Francis Crick work out this shape. Long strands of DNA are wound tightly into chromosomes. Most human body cells have 46 chromosomes.

Remember: DNA is the molecule. Genes are sections of it. Chromosomes are DNA wound up into neat packages.

Lesson 2 · Where cells keep DNA
LS2.K2LS2.S1

In the nucleus, or loose in the cytoplasm

Most animal and plant cells keep their DNA inside the nucleus. A membrane around the nucleus protects the DNA and controls what goes in and out.

Some cells have no nucleus. Bacteria are the best example. A bacterial cell keeps its DNA in the cytoplasm, mostly in one large loop. Many bacteria also carry small extra rings of DNA called plasmids.

There is one more place to look. Mitochondria, and the chloroplasts in plant cells, hold a small amount of their own DNA. Almost all of the cell’s instructions are still in the nucleus.

Compare: plant cell and animal cell: DNA in the nucleus. Bacterial cell: DNA in the cytoplasm, because there is no nucleus.

Lesson 3 · One set of instructions
LS2.K3

Your body cells share the same DNA

You started as one cell. Every body cell you have came from that cell by division, and each division copied the DNA. So your skin cells, muscle cells and nerve cells all hold the same DNA.

If the DNA is the same, why do cells look and act so differently? Each kind of cell uses different genes. A muscle cell switches on the genes for muscle proteins. A nerve cell switches on the genes for sending signals. The rest of the book is still there, just closed.

There are a few exceptions. Sex cells (sperm and egg cells) carry only half the DNA, one chromosome from each pair. Mature red blood cells lose their nucleus, so they have no DNA at all. Rarely, a copying mistake changes a cell’s DNA a little.

Remember: same DNA in almost every body cell, different genes in use.

Lesson 4 · Why cells divide
LS2.K4

Growth, repair, reproduction and variation

Cell division is when one cell splits to make new cells. The starting cell is the parent cell. The new cells are daughter cells. Cell division does four jobs:

  • Growth: a seedling becomes a tree by making more cells.
  • Repair: new cells replace damaged or worn-out ones, like when a scraped knee heals.
  • Reproduction: new organisms are made. A yeast cell divides into two yeast cells. Plants and animals make sex cells that join to start a new organism.
  • Variation: the kind of division that makes sex cells shuffles DNA, so offspring are different from each other.

Bacteria split in two by a simpler process called binary fission. They copy their DNA loop and pinch in half.

Lesson 5 · Copy and split
LS2.K5LS2.S2

Mitosis makes two identical cells

Mitosis is the kind of cell division that makes two identical daughter cells. Each daughter cell gets a full, exact copy of the parent cell’s DNA. It has the same number of chromosomes as the parent.

Mitosis is how your body grows and repairs itself. Skin, bone and the root tips of plants all grow by mitosis. Some living things also use it to reproduce, like yeast and aspen trees that sprout from roots.

  1. Copy: the cell copies every chromosome. Each one now has two identical halves joined together.
  2. Line up: the chromosomes line up single file across the middle.
  3. Pull apart: the two halves of each chromosome are pulled to opposite ends.
  4. Split: the cell pinches in two. Each new cell has a full set.

Scientists name these stages interphase, prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase. You do not need to memorize the names.

Lesson 6 · Copy, shuffle, split twice
LS2.K6LS2.K4

Meiosis makes four different sex cells

Meiosis is the kind of cell division that makes sex cells for sexual reproduction. In animals these are sperm and egg cells. In flowering plants they form inside pollen and ovules.

Meiosis makes four daughter cells, and they are not identical. Each one has half the parent cell’s chromosomes: one from each pair. In humans, a body cell has 46 chromosomes and a sex cell has 23.

  1. Copy: every chromosome is copied, just like in mitosis.
  2. Pair and swap: matching chromosomes pair up and trade pieces. This is called crossing over.
  3. First split: the pairs separate. Which side each chromosome goes to is random.
  4. Second split: the copies separate. Four cells, each with half the chromosomes.

Crossing over and the random line-up mean every sex cell gets a different mix. When a sperm and egg join, a fertilized egg gets 23 + 23 = 46 chromosomes, and a new, unique combination. This is where variation comes from.

Investigation 4 · Cell and chromosome maths
LS2.K4LS2.K5LS2.K6LS2.S3

Doubling, halving and joining

Every round of mitosis doubles the number of cells. Meiosis halves the chromosome number. Fertilization adds two halves back together.

cells after n rounds = starting cells × 2n
sex cell chromosomes = body cell chromosomes ÷ 2
fertilized egg = sperm + egg chromosomes
Worked example1 cell divides by mitosis 5 times: 1 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32 cells.A canola body cell has 38 chromosomes. Meiosis gives sex cells with 38 ÷ 2 = 19.

Teacher notes

  • LS2.K1: “DNA is the genetic information” is taught more precisely as DNA being the molecule that carries the genetic information, coded in genes. Instructions are linked to the survival of the whole organism as well as the cell. The 1953 double helix work (Franklin, Watson and Crick) is added as one line of context.
  • LS2.K2: “may store DNA in the cytoplasm” is taught as bacteria always keeping their DNA in the cytoplasm (one main loop, often with plasmids), since they have no nucleus. Mitochondria and chloroplasts are noted as holding a small amount of their own DNA.
  • LS2.K3: “cells contain the same DNA” is taught as almost all body cells holding the same DNA. Exceptions are named: sex cells carry half, mature red blood cells have no nucleus and no DNA, and rare copying errors occur. Cell differences are explained by different genes being used.
  • LS2.K4: variation is taught as coming from meiosis (crossing over and random line-up) together with fertilization. Reproduction includes asexual examples (yeast, aspen root suckers). Bacteria are noted as dividing by binary fission, not mitosis.
  • LS2.K5: strictly, mitosis divides the nucleus and the cytoplasm then splits (cytokinesis); the lab treats them together as the draft does. Daughter cells are identical apart from rare copying errors. Phase names are given once as optional context.
  • LS2.K6: the four daughter cells are taught as having half the chromosomes. In human females, meiosis gives one egg cell and three small cells that break down; the lab keeps the general four-cell model and does not detail this. Plant meiosis is described simply as happening in pollen and ovules.
  • Sensitive content: sperm, egg and fertilization are taught only at the cell level. Please check whether Alberta’s parental notice rules for human sexuality content apply to this lesson in your school.
  • Numbers: chromosome numbers (human 46, fruit fly 8, pea 14, corn 20, canola 38, bread wheat 42, potato 48, dog 78) are standard reference values that should be spot checked. Root tip slides are drawings with randomized practice data.
